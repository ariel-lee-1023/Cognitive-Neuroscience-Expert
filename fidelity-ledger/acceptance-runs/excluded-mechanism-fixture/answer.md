Both claims go too far. Accurate behavioral prediction supports a model’s account of input–output behavior under the tested conditions. It does not establish that the brain uses the model’s internal operations. But idealization does not disqualify a model from explaining a mechanism: the question is whether it preserves the components, activities, and organization that matter for the phenomenon.

The first investigator must supply a biological interpretation and evidence for it. What do the network’s units, connections, states, and updates correspond to? Which are proposed biological features, and which are computational conveniences? If several architectures predict the same behavior, their fit cannot establish which architecture the brain implements. Even strong prediction on new trials may leave those alternatives unresolved.

The second investigator confuses selective abstraction with biological error. A population-level model might omit individual spikes while correctly explaining how recurrent interactions maintain information. That omission becomes a problem if spike timing is necessary for the phenomenon or intervention contrast being explained. Adding microscopic detail is useful when it changes a relevant prediction or resolves a disputed operation; detail alone supplies no evidence that the model describes the actual system.

Craver’s framework separates two questions: **How much of the mechanism is specified?** and **How much evidence supports its actuality?** An incomplete sketch can have well-supported biological commitments, while a detailed simulation can remain only a possible mechanism. Computational problem, algorithm, and implementation are also distinct explanatory questions; they are not interchangeable with the component–whole relations within a biological mechanism. [Source framework: supplied Craver reference, chapters 2, 4–6.]

To discriminate accounts, first specify the behavior, system, conditions, and alternatives. The following delayed-memory example is a proposed test strategy, not a report of observed findings. Suppose a recurrent-activity network and an account using a relatively silent storage state both predict ordinary recall accurately.

| Evidence | Discriminating question |
|---|---|
| Independently measured components and dynamics | Do the proposed connections and activities exist, with the timing and interactions the account requires? A decodable signal alone could be a correlate rather than the storage mechanism. |
| Novel task conditions | Before collecting data, derive each model’s predictions for longer delays, distraction, and load. Do they predict different error patterns or recovery trajectories? More conditions compatible with both will not decide between them. |
| Timed perturbations | If sustained recurrence is essential in one model but the rival’s storage state survives a brief interruption, do their predictions diverge after transient disruption during the delay? First establish that the particular models actually make these different predictions. |
| Organization and restoration | Does disrupting a predicted connection or interaction produce the specific deficit, and does restoring the relevant activity restore performance in the predicted way? Rescue strengthens the account only if it does not work through a compensating route. |

Perturbation evidence needs sham, timing, and task controls, checks on spread and target engagement, and attention to sensory, motor, and general-state effects. A deficit can implicate background support rather than the proposed computation; a null can reflect redundancy or compensation. Combine evidence that manipulating a candidate component changes the task phenomenon with evidence that task manipulations recruit that component as predicted. In Craver’s account, this supports constitutive relevance only within an established part–whole relation; it is not proof of reciprocal causation between levels. [Source framework: chapters 3–4 and 7.]

Favor the network’s mechanistic interpretation if its independently specified biological commitments survive tests that its rivals fail. Revise that interpretation if its necessary components or intervention predictions fail despite adequate measurement and target engagement. This can preserve the model’s predictive usefulness while rejecting its biological story. No particular network, task, or dataset is supplied here, so the present evidence supports these standards and proposed comparisons, not a verdict that an actual mechanism has been identified.
