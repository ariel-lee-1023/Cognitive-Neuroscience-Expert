Accurate behavioral prediction supports the network as a model of the tested behavior. It does not, by itself, identify the biological mechanism. Rejecting every idealized model is also too strong: an abstraction can explain a real dependency while omitting details irrelevant to that dependency.

The first investigator confuses reproducing an outcome with establishing how the brain produces it. Different architectures, representations, learning rules, or even non-neural algorithms may generate the same choices. Calling a unit a neuron or interpreting a weight as a synapse does not establish the mapping. The claim must specify what biological populations the units represent, what activity the model states describe, which interactions implement its operations, and whether the proposed learning process has a biological counterpart. A network trained with an artificial procedure might model adult performance while failing as an account of how that performance develops. Stocco's supplied reference explicitly allows neural networks to function either as function approximators or as explanatory theories; the technique alone does not decide their status. [Stocco, Introduction](../../../references/reference-stocco-explanatory-models.md)

The second investigator correctly demands biological constraints but mistakes simplification for disqualification. A population-rate model, for example, could capture the circuit interaction responsible for a behavioral contrast without representing every spike or molecule. The question is whether the omitted details alter the relevant predictions. Craver distinguishes two independent dimensions: evidence that a mechanism is actual, and completeness of its description. A detailed simulation can remain a possible mechanism; an incomplete sketch can contain well-supported claims about actual components. Neither added detail nor predictive accuracy automatically moves a model toward actuality. These component–whole relationships also differ from the computational, algorithmic, and implementation questions a model addresses. [Craver, Chapters 2, 4–6](../../../references/reference-craver-explaining-brain.md)

The useful next step is to make competing models disagree. The following is a proposed illustration, not a result about the unspecified network. Suppose the behavior is remembering a location over a delay, and two models fit baseline accuracy equally well: one stores the location through sustained recurrent activity, while another stores it in a transient synaptic state that survives a brief interruption of firing.

| Evidence | Discriminating prediction and implication |
|---|---|
| Held-out delay lengths, distractors, and error distributions | Fix models and fitting procedures before testing. Compare predicted forgetting, systematic errors, and response times if the models generate them. Generalization failures can reject a model's commitments even when baseline accuracy matches. A relative winner must also fit these outcomes adequately. |
| Simultaneous population recordings | Test the prespecified mapping from internal states to neural measurements, including timing and interactions. The two accounts may predict different delay dynamics and responses to a probe. Decoding the remembered location alone would not establish the storage operation. |
| Brief, timed perturbation during the delay | If the recurrent model requires uninterrupted activity and the synaptic model preserves its state during suppression, predict persistent memory loss versus recovery after suppression. Derive the actual duration and error predictions from each model before collecting data. If the proposed perturbation also disrupts synaptic storage, it no longer isolates this distinction. |
| Perturbation of a proposed interaction, followed by a targeted rescue | Test whether disrupting a specified connection changes downstream dynamics and behavior as predicted, and whether restoring the relevant activity pattern restores performance. This constrains organization more strongly than a component inventory or a general performance deficit. |

Intervention interpretation requires its own evidence. Define the comparison as a specified suppression protocol versus sham in the task population, with neural recovery and subsequent recall error as outcomes. Randomized assignment can identify the protocol's effect, subject to consistent delivery, measurement, and avoidance of biased post-assignment selection. Establishing that the effect specifically reflects disruption of storage further requires checks on perturbation spread, sensory and motor effects, and alternative routes. A deficit can reflect general support; a null can reflect insufficient disruption, redundancy, or compensation. Craver's constitutive-relevance strategy combines such bottom-up tests with task manipulations that change the candidate component's activity, within an established part–whole relationship. This is not a claim of reciprocal causation between independently existing levels. [Hernán and Robins, Chapters 1–3 and 8–9](../../../references/reference-hernan-robins-causal-inference.md); [Craver, Chapters 3–4](../../../references/reference-craver-explaining-brain.md)

Support would increase if independently supported components and organization predict both ordinary behavior and distinctive perturbation, modulation, and failure patterns under the same commitments. A verified mismatch would require revising the relevant mechanism claim, even if behavioral prediction remained excellent. Conversely, an idealized model surviving these tests would undermine blanket rejection of abstraction. The prompt supplies no architecture, task data, or neural evidence, so the present judgment is limited to predictive success; the experiments above specify how that judgment could change.
