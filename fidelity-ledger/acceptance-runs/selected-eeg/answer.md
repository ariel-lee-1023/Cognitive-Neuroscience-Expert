No. Randomization makes assignment exchangeable in the original randomized population; it does not automatically preserve that property among participants selected afterward for tolerance and clean EEG.

Let A be stimulation assignment, S=1 mean inclusion, and Y be the EEG outcome. The analyzed contrast is E[Y|A=1,S=1] − E[Y|A=0,S=1]. If assignment affects inclusion and a participant characteristic U affects both inclusion and Y, restricting to S=1 can open the collider path A → S ← U → Y. For example, stimulation might affect discomfort or movement, while susceptibility to those responses also relates to the neural outcome. The retained treatment groups can then differ in outcome-relevant characteristics despite initial randomization. This is a possible structure, not a finding established by the question; post-assignment selection does not invariably produce bias.

First specify whether the target is the assignment effect in all randomized participants or an effect in a defined subgroup. Comparing observed tolerators does not automatically identify an effect among people who would tolerate both assignments: those counterfactual subgroup memberships are not observed.

To recover the full randomized-population contrast when the outcome is well-defined but missing for excluded participants, selection weighting or standardization may help under explicit assumptions: measured covariates/history suffice to make observation independent of the relevant potential outcome, everyone in the target has a positive probability of being observed under each assignment, and consistency holds. Weighting cannot repair unmeasured informative selection or a subgroup whose outcomes are never observed. Describe exclusions by arm and their timing, assess these assumptions, and use sensitivity analyses where they are uncertain. Merely calling the retained-sample analysis intention-to-treat does not restore randomization's protection.
