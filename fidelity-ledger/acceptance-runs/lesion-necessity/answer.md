No. The lesion and the rate model support different claims, and neither establishes exclusive implementation in that area.

A well-controlled, anatomically specific lesion can support the claim that the area's intact contribution is necessary for expressing the learned response in the tested conditions and at the tested time. That is conditional necessity, not sufficiency or sole ownership of the computation. The lesion could disrupt a relay, an output pathway, general task support, or interactions with other regions. Changes elsewhere in the remaining network can also produce the deficit. Losing an already learned response does not, by itself, distinguish destruction of the learned representation from failure to retrieve or express it.

The relevant causal comparison is the specified lesion versus an appropriate sham or intact control in comparable subjects, measuring the same response over a defined follow-up period. The question does not supply those controls or lesion details, so even that causal interpretation remains conditional. Identifying the effect of a lesion procedure would still not identify the algorithm or its location. [Hernán and Robins, chapters 1–3](../../../references/reference-hernan-robins-causal-inference.md).

A rate model that reproduces the response demonstrates that its rules can generate that behavior. If it also reproduces the lesion deficit, that adds a useful failure-condition constraint. However, multiple algorithms and distributed circuits could reproduce both observations. The model's rates, connections, and update rules need independent biological support before being identified with actual local operations. In Craver's terms, predictive success can support a possible mechanism without establishing the actual mechanism. [Craver, chapters 2–4](../../../references/reference-craver-explaining-brain.md).

Stronger evidence would connect model variables and operations to recorded neural dynamics and circuit organization, then test predictions that distinguish the proposed local algorithm from distributed or relay accounts. For example, selectively and reversibly perturbing a proposed local operation at its predicted time should change a specific feature of behavior and downstream activity, while sensory detection and response execution remain intact. Recordings from connected regions, task manipulations, and appropriately controlled rescue experiments could narrow the alternatives. Even a successful rescue must be checked for recruitment of another route. This is a proposed evidential strategy, not evidence supplied by the question. [Craver, chapters 4 and 7](../../../references/reference-craver-explaining-brain.md).
