The result currently establishes a smaller and later **measured P3 peak**, not impaired cognition. A peak combines overlapping activity, timing variability, noise, and preprocessing effects. I would check the following before interpreting the group difference.

1. **Define the cognitive contrast and identify the component.** Specify the task operation claimed to be impaired and whether the eliciting conditions and scalp distribution support P3a, P3b, or an unresolved mixture. Compare physically equivalent stimuli, target probability, preceding sequences, inter-event intervals, instructions, and response requirements across groups. Inspect accuracy, omissions, reaction-time distributions, and fatigue over blocks; document relevant sensory, age, and medication differences. These can change task engagement or what enters an average without establishing a particular cognitive deficit. Early sensory responses and sensory performance provide useful input checks. Where a within-person manipulation targets the proposed operation, test its interaction with group, rather than relying solely on a group main effect. [Luck, Ch 2; Kappenman–Luck, Ch 1 and P300 overview.]

2. **Audit acquisition and event alignment.** Verify event identities and actual stimulus onset relative to EEG triggers using recorded physical onset signals, or a photodiode/microphone calibration if prospective testing is possible. Check constant offsets, jitter, and session drift separately, including differences between group recording sessions. Check sampling, acquisition filters, clipping, bad channels, and acquisition and analysis references. Reprocess with a consistent, justified reference and document interpolation; examine sensitivity to a defensible alternative reference when the recorded channels permit it. Hardware timing jitter can lower and broaden an average, while a group-specific trigger offset can produce an apparent delay. New calibration alone cannot establish that earlier sessions were correctly timed. [Luck, Chs 3, 8.]

3. **Test filter distortion using the actual pipeline.** Recover filter type, cutoff definitions, order/slope, phase behavior, padding and boundary treatment, and whether filtering occurred before or after epoching. Inspect raw and minimally processed waveforms alongside the reported results, preserving necessary acquisition filtering. Recalculate the group contrast under a small set of justified, less aggressive settings. Inspect the impulse response and, as a proposed calibration, pass plausible P3-plus-neighboring-component waveforms through the exact filters to measure induced amplitude and timing changes. A causal filter can delay activity; zero-phase filtering can still smear activity backward and forward. High-pass filtering can distort broad P3 activity and create flanking lobes. Identical settings across groups do not ensure equal distortion when their waveform shapes differ. Robustness means comparable estimates with uncertainty, not merely significance under one preferred setting. [Luck, Ch 5.]

4. **Separate fewer trials, noisier trials, and different trials.** Report trials presented, retained, and rejected by participant, condition, block, and artifact type, plus participant exclusions. Apply consistent artifact criteria with group labels masked where feasible; inspect raw, corrected, and rejected epochs, ocular channels, and residual muscle or movement contamination. Correction can remove neural activity or leave artifacts. Compare retained versus rejected trials' available behavioral and stimulus characteristics: rejecting more difficult, slow, fatigued, or error trials in patients changes the sampled state. Correcting a blink's voltage does not undo its effect on visual input.

   Use all acceptable trials for the main prespecified analysis and quantify measurement uncertainty. As a sensitivity check, repeatedly subsample clean trials to comparable counts while preserving condition and sequence composition where feasible. This tests trial-count sensitivity; it cannot restore missing states or equalize intrinsic EEG noise. Under independent zero-mean noise, average noise variance scales as 1/N. Fewer trials can therefore make peak estimates less stable and can *inflate* selected positive maxima; unequal rejection does not automatically explain a smaller patient peak. Assess residual noise directly. [Luck, Chs 4, 6.]

5. **Check baseline, overlap, and latency variability.** Inspect prestimulus voltages before baseline subtraction, preceding-event responses, adjacent components such as N2, slow waves, and response-related activity. Baseline differences can shift the entire poststimulus waveform. Plot individual participant averages and scalp distributions, then trial-level ERP images where signal quality allows. Greater trial-to-trial or between-person latency variability can broaden and lower an average even with unchanged underlying amplitude. Jitter alone need not delay a symmetric isolated peak, so evaluate mean timing, timing variability, and overlap separately. Response-locked averages and validated single-trial timing estimates are useful sensitivity checks, but response locking changes component alignment and noisy single-trial peak picking is not ground truth. A matched difference wave can reduce shared activity, but remains a sum of all processes affected by the contrast. [Luck, Chs 2, 4; Kappenman–Luck, Ch 1.]

6. **Measure amplitude and latency separately, with uncertainty.** Choose electrodes and windows from independent evidence or preregister them for a replication; do not select the largest observed group difference and test it as prespecified. Use participant-level mean amplitude over a sufficiently broad, justified window as the primary amplitude check, retaining peaks as secondary measures. A broad window can be less sensitive to jitter, but can include overlap or truncate delayed activity. For timing, consider 50% fractional-area latency if the component area can be meaningfully isolated, with explicit boundaries and polarity handling. It measures central timing, not onset, and becomes unstable when area is weak or canceled by opposite polarity. Report confidence intervals for both effects, account for repeated conditions and multiple comparisons, and do not treat grand-average maxima as independent observations. If using jackknife latency estimates, use the corresponding corrected inference because leave-one-out averages are dependent. [Luck, Ch 6.]

A robust difference after these checks would support altered amplitude and/or timing of a task-related ERP under the stated measurement assumptions. Calling it impaired cognition additionally requires evidence linking that pattern to the specified operation, such as a discriminating task manipulation and convergent behavioral measures. Neither reduced P3 amplitude nor delayed peak latency is a direct, universal scale of cognitive capacity or processing onset.
