A difference in filtered peak latency does **not establish a difference in neural onset**. Peak time describes a maximum of the summed scalp waveform. Zero-phase filtering removes phase delay, but its noncausal temporal smoothing can spread later activity backward and produce apparent activity before its true onset. The same filter in both conditions does not guarantee equal onset bias when their amplitudes, shapes, or overlapping components differ. These distinctions follow from Luck’s supplied first-edition technique reference, chapters 5–6.

I would assess the claim in this order:

1. **Specify what “onset” means.** Is the claim about the onset of an ERP component in each condition, the time the condition waveforms start to differ, or the beginning of a cognitive operation? These are different quantities. The onset of a condition difference need not mark the first activity of the process, and a peak-latency contrast directly tests none of them.

2. **Check the timing and preprocessing chain.** Verify event markers against actual stimulus delivery, including condition-dependent delays and jitter. Inspect reference choice, prestimulus differences and baseline correction, artifacts and their correction, retained trial counts, preceding-event overlap, and response timing. Compare participant waveforms as well as grand averages. Unequal noise and trial or participant latency variability can alter apparent onset and peak timing without a corresponding shift of the underlying process. Luck’s chapters 2–4 support these checks.

3. **Assess filter-induced onset bias explicitly.** Obtain the actual filter type, coefficients/order, cutoffs, effective forward–backward response where applicable, and padding and epoch-boundary handling. Inspect its impulse response. Re-estimate timing with defensible weaker offline filtering, or without that additional filtering when feasible, while recognizing that acquisition filtering remains. As a proposed validation, pass plausible signals with known onsets through the exact pipeline: include identical-onset signals with different amplitudes, overlapping components, and latency variability, as well as genuinely shifted signals. Quantify how often the procedure creates or obscures an onset difference. No universally safe cutoff follows from “zero phase.”

4. **Use an onset-oriented measure and establish what it measures.** For a sufficiently isolated component, prespecify electrodes, polarity, a search window, and a leading-edge fractional-peak criterion, then examine reasonable criterion sensitivity. This is an operational onset estimate whose bias still depends on morphology, noise, overlap, and filtering. Fixed-voltage thresholds can be crossed earlier simply because a response is larger; fractional thresholds also need validation. Fifty-percent fractional-area latency measures the middle of the selected area, not onset. Matched difference waves and multiple electrode sites can help assess component overlap, but subtraction retains every contribution that differs. The component handbook’s chapter 1 specifically shows how an amplitude change in one generator can shift another visible peak without any generator changing latency.

5. **Test the onset contrast directly and report uncertainty.** Estimate the paired condition difference using participant-level measurements and an appropriate uncertainty procedure. If jackknife latency estimates are used, apply the specialized correction for dependent leave-one-out averages. Account for electrode/time-window multiplicity and avoid selecting the measurement using the effect being tested. Comparing the first significant sample in each condition is not a valid substitute: significance timing depends on amplitude and precision, and one significant result versus one nonsignificant result does not establish a timing difference. These measurement cautions are grounded in Luck’s chapter 6; the proposed pipeline validation is my methodological synthesis.

With only the stated peak result, I would report **a peak-latency difference after the specified filtering**. A stronger onset claim requires a reliable, directly tested onset contrast that survives the timing, overlap, variability, and filter checks. Even then, translating ERP onset into the start of a cognitive operation requires evidence linking that component to the operation. No data, filter specification, or timing-validation records were supplied, so the onset claim itself cannot be verified here.
