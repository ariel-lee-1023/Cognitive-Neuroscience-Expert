**Reversing b and d does not, by itself, prove dyslexia.** It tells us that the child sometimes fails to preserve a distinction the alphabet requires; it does not yet tell us why or whether there is a broader reading difficulty.

In Dehaene’s *Reading in the Brain*, the relevant mechanism is **neuronal recycling**: reading recruits visual systems that already recognize objects. Recognizing an animal facing left or right as the same animal is useful. For letters, however, mirror orientation can change identity: **b** and **d** are different. Learning to read therefore includes learning which visual changes to ignore—such as font—and which to preserve—such as this orientation difference. Reversals fit a challenge that literacy acquisition itself creates; they are not specific evidence of dyslexia.

### What evidence is missing?

- **Development and experience:** How far along is the child in learning this writing system? What letter–sound instruction and practice have they received? A single reversal without this context is hard to interpret.
- **The actual task:** Are they reversing letters when writing from memory, copying them, naming them, or reading words? Can they visually distinguish b from d but retrieve the wrong name or write the wrong form? These outcomes implicate different operations; a writing error alone does not establish a decoding deficit.
- **The broader reading profile:** How accurately and fluently do they read familiar words and unfamiliar, pronounceable nonwords? How is spelling? What can they do with speech sounds and oral language? Dehaene emphasizes efficient print-to-sound processing, particularly phonological processing, rather than treating mirror confusion as the defining problem.
- **Persistence and consequences:** Does the difficulty recur across occasions and materials? Is it accompanied by sustained problems decoding words or developing fluency, and how does it change with instruction and practice?

The task distinctions above are an application of the reading account, not a diagnostic test. If reversals occur alongside otherwise progressing reading, they provide little basis for the parent’s conclusion. Persistent, broader decoding and fluency difficulties would make the concern more substantial—but still would not identify the mechanism from reversals alone.

**The useful next evidence is a developmental reading history and a profile of reading performance, not simply more examples of reversed letters.** This 2009 framework explains why the inference fails; it does not supply current diagnostic criteria. It also does not establish that visual orientation exercises alone would address any broader reading difficulty.
