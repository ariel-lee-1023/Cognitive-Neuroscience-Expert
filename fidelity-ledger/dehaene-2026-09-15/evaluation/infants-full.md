**The Number Sense cannot supply an exact replicated effect size for newborn symbolic subtraction as of 2026.** The supplied source is the original 1997 edition, first issued in paperback in 1999; its selected later research checks do not establish a 2026 estimate.

The requested phenomenon also needs clarification. Chapter 2 discusses infants’ responses to small collections and hidden additions or removals. A baby looking longer at an unexpected collection can provide evidence about object or quantity expectations. That is different from demonstrating subtraction using learned number symbols such as “3 − 1.” Novelty, continuous visual properties, and memory for individual objects are alternative explanations to test. Evidence from infants of one age cannot automatically be attributed to newborns.

A later study summarized in the reference—Xu and Spelke (2000)—concerns **six-month-old infants’ approximate discrimination of 8 versus 16, but not 8 versus 12**, under that study’s controls. It concerns neither newborns nor symbolic subtraction, and these ratios are stimulus comparisons, not statistical effect sizes.

To report a replicated effect size, we would need an identified newborn symbolic-subtraction paradigm, its outcome measure, original and replication data, and an effect-size definition with uncertainty. Those materials are absent here, and I have not verified the literature through 2026. **The defensible answer is “not established by these sources,” not a numerical estimate or a claim of zero effect.**
