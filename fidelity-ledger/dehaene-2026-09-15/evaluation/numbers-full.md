# Test the components before naming the difficulty

**This profile does not establish a damaged number sense.** Comparing 8 with 9 is normally harder than comparing widely separated numbers. Reading “89” can succeed without understanding eight tens and nine units; recalling 6×7 can rely on a learned verbal fact. Subtraction can fail at understanding the operation, selecting a strategy, retaining intermediate results, or regrouping.

The plan below applies Dehaene’s distinctions among quantity, symbols, verbal facts, and procedures. Its particular task contrasts are proposed tests, not a diagnostic battery.

## 1. Establish what the observations mean

Record age, language, instruction and practice, whether the difficulty is longstanding or a loss of previously mastered performance, and the exact subtraction errors. Check understanding of instructions, vision, hearing, fatigue, anxiety, and familiarity with the response device.

Use several short sessions with repeated trials, both timed and untimed. Record accuracy, response time, omissions, and worked steps. Ask for strategies on separate trials so explaining does not contaminate timed performance. Compare with suitable age- and instruction-matched expectations; one slow answer has little evidential value.

## 2. Separate the candidate bottlenecks

| Candidate | Tasks and controls | Informative pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Approximate quantity discrimination** | Briefly compare dot collections across easy and hard ratios. Vary dot size, total area, density, and arrangement across stimulus sets, including trials where visual cues conflict with numerosity. Limit counting; check visual discrimination and response speed with nonnumerical tasks. | Consistent difficulty across controlled nonsymbolic displays supports a quantity-processing hypothesis. Difficulty only when irrelevant visual cues conflict also raises attention/control explanations. |
| **Access from symbols to quantity** | Compare Arabic digits across distances and sizes: include 8–9, 1–2, and widely separated pairs. Add spoken-number comparisons and matching digits or number words to sets. Counterbalance response side and use comparable response demands. | Good dot comparison with poor digit-to-quantity matching suggests a mapping/access problem. Slowness across numerical and nonnumerical tasks suggests a broader speed or decision issue. |
| **Exact number and place-value knowledge** | Read and write numbers to dictation; distinguish 89 from 98 and 80 from 8. Build 89 with tens and units, explain the value of each digit, count objects, and produce a requested set. | Fluent reading with weak construction or decomposition separates numeral naming from positional/cardinal understanding. |
| **Fact retrieval versus derivation** | Sample several multiplication facts, addition facts, and simple subtraction facts. Ask how answers were obtained; compare producing an answer with recognizing it. Check related facts and whether answers make quantitative sense. | One memorized product does not establish intact arithmetic generally. Recognition–production differences or table intrusions can implicate retrieval rather than quantity. |
| **Subtraction meaning and procedure** | Compare removing objects, finding a difference, oral calculation, and written calculation. Contrast 8−3, 43−21, 43−27, and 403−27. Inspect every step; vary regrouping, zeros, layout, and number of steps systematically. | Success with objects and simple problems but failure at regrouping points toward place value/procedure. Improvement with written intermediate results suggests memory load contributes. |

## 3. Test the explanation suggested by the errors

If the learner repeatedly subtracts the smaller digit from the larger in each column, contrast a problem where that rule accidentally succeeds with one where it fails. Ask for an estimate and an explanation using tens and units. This tests a specific procedural rule rather than treating all wrong answers alike.

After baseline testing, give a brief explanation targeted to the suspected bottleneck. Test new examples immediately and after a delay, with supports removed. Improvement and transfer inform the next teaching step; they neither prove a neural mechanism nor establish a general intervention effect.

**Decision rule:** consider a quantity-specific account only if difficulties persist across controlled quantity tasks and cannot be adequately explained by symbol mappings, language, attention, response demands, instruction, or procedures. Even then, a behavioral profile does not locate brain damage or by itself diagnose a disorder.

*Framework sources: The Number Sense, chapters 3–5 and 7–8; How We Learn, chapters 7–10. This is a framework-based assessment plan, not current clinical guidance.*
