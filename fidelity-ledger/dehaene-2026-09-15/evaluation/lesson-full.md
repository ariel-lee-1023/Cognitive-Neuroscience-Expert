# A 30-minute lesson that tests durable learning

**Replace repeated viewing with guided decoding, attempts from memory, corrective feedback, and spaced returns.** Immediate recognition may reflect familiarity with the displayed examples. Delayed failure could also reflect weak sound mappings, confusion between similar signs, or a mismatch between practice and testing; it does not by itself identify a consolidation problem.

Assume adults already know the spoken words being represented. Choose **six script units**, reducing the set if learners cannot make useful attempts. For an alphabet teach letter–sound mappings; for a syllabary teach syllable mappings; for a character-based script teach the relevant character/component, pronunciation, and meaning relationships. Do not impose alphabetic rules on every script.

**Target:** learners independently read the taught units and apply the taught relationships to valid, previously unseen examples after a delay.

## The lesson

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | **Diagnose.** Without an answer key, ask learners to read familiar examples, distinguish one confusable pair, and try one new combination. Check that they know the corresponding spoken language. Record whether the difficulty is visual discrimination, mapping, or combination. |
| 3–8 | **Teach explicitly.** Model three units, then three more. Highlight the stroke, orientation, or position that distinguishes similar units. Say the relevant sound and connect it to a known word or meaning. Demonstrate how the script combines units where applicable. Remove unnecessary decoration. |
| 8–13 | **Attempt before reveal.** Show one unit without its label; learners say its sound or meaning. Give specific feedback: “This stroke faces left, so this is __.” Have them apply the correction on another example. Revisit a missed item after intervening items, rather than counting an immediate echoed answer as mastery. |
| 13–17 | **Apply with guidance.** Read short meaningful examples using taught relationships. Model one, then let learners attempt another and explain the relevant distinction. Include confusable alternatives. Avoid requiring unfamiliar vocabulary or untaught spelling rules. |
| 17–22 | **Retrieve again with variation.** Shuffle earlier material and use legitimate changes of font, size, or position. Preserve script-critical orientation and order. Include new combinations only where the learned rules support them. Fade instructional highlighting. |
| 22–25 | **Introduce a brief gap.** Discuss meanings or use a short spoken-language activity without displaying the target signs. This creates separation before the exit test. |
| 25–30 | **Unaided exit test, then correction.** Use taught units, unfamiliar visual versions, and valid new examples. Collect responses before giving feedback; record errors by type. Explain the follow-up practice schedule. |

The design applies Dehaene’s attention, active engagement, error feedback, and consolidation framework. Engagement means generating an answer, not merely clicking or tracing. His neuronal-recycling account motivates learning which visual differences matter and connecting print with language; it does not establish that this exact lesson is effective.

## Assess learning beyond the lesson

Give short, equivalent **unaided tests the next day and one week later**, before that session’s review. Between tests, schedule brief retrieval-and-feedback practice—for example, after the next-day test and on day three. These are workable starting intervals, not a universal optimum. Protect normal sleep; sleep cannot substitute for encoding and practice.

Track accuracy separately for taught items, changed typography, and new combinations; record hesitation and confusion patterns, plus confidence before feedback. Persistent shape errors call for contrastive discrimination; accurate recognition with failed naming calls for mapping retrieval; failed new combinations call for explicit combination practice.

To evaluate the redesign, compare randomly assigned learners receiving it versus the previous lesson, with equal content, teaching time, follow-up practice, and identical delayed assessments. Record attendance, missing tests, and extra practice. Improvement supports the instructional package; it does not isolate a neural mechanism or identify which component caused the benefit.
