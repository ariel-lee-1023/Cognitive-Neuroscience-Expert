**A normal-looking MRI does not rule out a developmental reading difficulty. Persistent trouble decoding unfamiliar words after sustained instruction calls for timely assessment and targeted support, rather than simply waiting.** This description raises concern about dyslexia or another reading difficulty, but it is not enough to diagnose the child.

### What the pattern tells us

A familiar story supplies clues: remembered wording, pictures, and what would make sense next. These can help a child produce the right word while concealing difficulty working it out from print. Try the contrast between recognizing a familiar sentence and reading a new word without that context. The second task tests whether the child can generalize the learned relationships between written units and language sounds.

In Dehaene’s account, reading develops through learning that connects visual representations with language systems. Difficulty learning or using those mappings need not produce an abnormality visible on a routine structural MRI. Research differences between groups of readers are also not a diagnostic test for an individual child. The scan cannot settle the educational question: **what can this child read independently, and what support helps?**

### What to do now

1. **Request a qualified reading or educational evaluation.** Bring examples of unfamiliar-word errors, spelling, work samples, and records of the instruction already provided and the child’s progress. Assessment should examine word and nonword decoding, accuracy and fluency, spelling, oral language and comprehension, alongside developmental history and opportunities to learn. Relevant hearing, vision, language, attention, or other concerns can receive appropriate clinical input. Co-occurring difficulties should guide support, not disqualify the child from it.

2. **Begin or adapt targeted teaching while assessment proceeds.** For an alphabetic writing system, this means explicit work on the print-to-sound relationships and blending skills the child has not mastered, connected with spelling and meaningful reading. Match the teaching to observed errors and prior instruction. More exposure to familiar stories alone may leave the decoding bottleneck untouched. Neither an MRI abnormality nor a definitive diagnostic label should be a prerequisite for discussing help.

3. **Make progress observable.** Establish a starting point and an agreed review date. Check whether the child can read new words using the patterns taught, retain that learning later, and become more accurate and fluent. Repeating a memorized passage successfully is insufficient evidence of transfer. If progress remains limited, revise the teaching and assessment rather than extending an indefinite wait.

4. **Protect willingness to read.** Avoid treating reluctance to read aloud as laziness or forcing public performances. Offer supported, manageable practice with specific, calm correction. Reading aloud to the child can preserve access to interesting stories and vocabulary while independent decoding develops.

A useful response to the teacher is: “The MRI does not explain away the persistent decoding difficulty. Let’s arrange an assessment, document the skills that need help, and agree on support and a progress review.”
