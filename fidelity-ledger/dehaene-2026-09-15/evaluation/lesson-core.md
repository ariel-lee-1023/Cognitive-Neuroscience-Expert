# A 30-minute lesson that prepares learners to remember

**Change the task from recognizing visible examples to retrieving symbol–sound mappings after the examples disappear.** Immediate recognition can be supported by a model still on the page, familiar items, or answer choices. It does not establish that learners can recover the mapping later. Their delayed failure could also reflect weak initial encoding or difficulty distinguishing similar symbols; diagnose those separately.

For this lesson, assume an alphabetic script and teach six unfamiliar letters with their sounds. For another script, substitute the appropriate written unit and pronunciation; do not impose alphabetic rules on it. The goal is to identify the symbols accurately after a delay and apply the taught mappings to new combinations.

## Lesson plan

| Time | What learners do | Instructional purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 min | Without a model, attempt the six symbols; then match visually similar pairs. Record prior familiarity. | Separate an unknown sound mapping from a visual discrimination problem. |
| 3–8 min | Learn three symbols. The teacher highlights each diagnostic feature, models its sound, and gives one short example. Learners distinguish it from a similar symbol. | Direct attention to features that identify the symbol; limit the amount introduced at once. |
| 8–12 min | Hide the examples. Show symbols individually in changing order; everyone attempts the sound before feedback. After an error, show and explain the correct mapping, then test it again after intervening items. | Require retrieval and make feedback repair a specific mistake. |
| 12–17 min | Introduce the remaining three symbols using the same procedure, briefly revisiting earlier symbols between new ones. | Distribute encounters and prevent success from depending on a fixed sequence. |
| 17–23 min | Mix all six. Alternate symbol-to-sound retrieval with selecting or writing a symbol from its sound. Include new, permissible short combinations and modest changes in font. | Practice both directions and test whether knowledge extends beyond memorized examples. |
| 23–26 min | Complete an unrelated verbal activity without viewing the script. | Create a short gap before checking retrieval. |
| 26–30 min | Take an individual, closed-model check, then receive corrective feedback. | Measure independent performance and prepare accurate subsequent practice. |

Allow thinking time. Have every learner answer before revealing the solution; watching a confident classmate is insufficient. Writing can help direct attention to distinguishing strokes, but copying or movement alone does not demonstrate learning.

## Assess what survives

Use a brief **next-day test and a one-week test**, before review on each occasion. Include:

- **Retention:** taught symbols presented alone, without answer choices; score sound accuracy and recurring confusions.
- **Transfer:** new legal combinations built only from taught units, plus readable unfamiliar fonts. Report this separately from practiced-item accuracy.
- **Direction:** symbol-to-sound and sound-to-symbol performance, scored separately. Writing adds motor demands, so also allow selection when diagnosing recognition.

Use parallel forms with comparable difficulty and record intervening practice. Note response time as a secondary measure; speed should not conceal errors. Give feedback after each assessment, recognizing that the first delayed test itself provides retrieval practice before the second.

Schedule short retrieval sessions the following day and later in the week, with corrective feedback. These intervals are practical starting points, not a demonstrated optimum.

This design applies attention, active attempts, error feedback, and consolidation principles. Its benefit for these learners requires evidence: compare delayed retention and transfer with an equal-time version of the previous lesson, ideally assigning learners randomly and balancing prior knowledge. Brain-based plausibility alone cannot establish that it works.
