# Computational Cognitive Neuroscience, 4th Edition — O'Reilly, Munakata, Hazy, Frank et al.
**Format**: md (CompCogNeuro/ed4) | **Pages**: ~316 | **Sections**: 10 chapters, 2 parts | **Depth**: study

**Application boundary:** Necessity claims and parameter defaults below belong to the source's implemented models and stated assumptions. Use them to generate and test mechanisms; they do not establish that every biological system must use the same architecture.

## Mental Model (read first)
Higher cognition is not a set of separate faculties — it is **one small set of neural mechanisms** (point-neuron integration, bidirectional attractor dynamics, inhibitory competition, XCAL learning) reused across every cognitive domain, with functional differences arising from **connectivity and parameters, not different algorithms**. The one thing to take from the whole book: *if you cannot state which mechanism produces a phenomenon, and simulate it, you have a redescription, not an explanation.* The authors' standard of evidence is a running model that reproduces both intact and lesioned behavior from the same parameters.

**Use this book when the question is "by what mechanism?" and you need an implemented, level-crossing answer.** It is the only source in this library that carries explanations continuously from ion channels to Stroop interference.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Ch 1: Introduction — the computational approach
- **Core idea**: Models are worth trusting only to the degree they are *constrained by data at as many levels as possible* and *generate testable predictions*. Complexity in cognition is emergent from simple units; verbal theory and paper diagrams cannot manage that complexity.
- **Emergent phenomena**: behavior that is a property of the interacting network, not of any unit. The book's recurring claim is that seemingly different cognitive functions (vision, memory, reading) reduce to the same mechanisms differently wired — "the neocortex is a fantastic form of silly putty."
- **Levels-of-analysis discipline**: the ideal of a mirror-image detailed model is rejected as neither achievable nor desirable; the goal is the *simplest model that captures the functional principle while remaining biologically defensible*.
- **Anti-pattern** — treating a model's ability to fit data as validation: models are "themselves complex, created by people, and have no necessary relationship to the real system." Fit without prediction and without cross-level constraint is not evidence.

### Ch 2: The Neuron — detection as the primitive
- **Core idea**: The neuron is a **detector**. It receives thousands of inputs, integrates them, and fires only past a threshold. Everything else is implementation.
- **Three input types**: **excitatory** (~85%, AMPA channels, glutamate, Na⁺), **inhibitory** (~15%, GABA, Cl⁻, from *inhibitory interneurons*), **leak** (always-on K⁺ channels). Cortical pyramidal neurons obey a hard constraint: a neuron sends *either* excitation or inhibition, never both — this constraint drives much of the architecture in later chapters.
- **Synaptic weight (net synaptic efficacy)**: the total impact a sender can have on a receiver. "*Everything you know, every cherished memory in your brain, is encoded as a pattern of synaptic weights.*" Computationally the weight determines *what the neuron detects*.
- **Tug-of-war model** — the load-bearing intuition. Membrane potential $V_m$ is a flag pulled between excitation and inhibition/leak. Each side pulls with force proportional to its conductance *and* to how far $V_m$ is from its driving potential:
  $$I_e = g_e(E_e - V_m)\qquad I_i = g_i(E_i-V_m)\qquad I_l = g_l(E_l-V_m)$$
  $$V_m(t) = V_m(t-1) + dt_{vm}\left[g_e(E_e{-}V_m) + g_i(E_i{-}V_m) + g_l(E_l{-}V_m)\right]$$
  When: reasoning about why a neuron's sensitivity is preserved across huge input-scale changes.
- **Integration is fundamentally relative** — the key functional consequence. What matters is excitation *relative to* inhibition, not absolute levels. This is why visual neurons stay in their sensitive range from bright snow to dark woods: overall illumination drives background inhibition alongside excitation. **This is the mechanism-level answer to gain control / normalization questions.**
- **Equilibrium membrane potential**: set $\Delta V_m=0$ and solve —
  $$V_m = \tfrac{g_e}{g_e+g_i+g_l}E_e + \tfrac{g_i}{g_e+g_i+g_l}E_i + \tfrac{g_l}{g_e+g_i+g_l}E_l$$
  Shown in the appendix to be a **Bayesian optimal detector**.
- **Net input**: $g_e(t) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i w_i$ — sender activity × weight, averaged.
- **Rate code vs. discrete spiking**. Discrete: `if (Vm > Theta) then y=1; Vm=Vm_r; else y=0`. The **AdEx (Adaptive Exponential)** model (Brette & Gerstner) adds an adapting threshold, producing **spike rate adaptation**, and fits real cortical spiking with contest-winning accuracy.
- **Critical methodological finding**: you **cannot** build a rate-code approximation from $V_m$ — there are multiple firing rates per $V_m$ value. You must use the excitatory net input $g_e$ compared to a threshold expressed in conductance terms:
  $$g_e^\Theta = \frac{g_i(E_i-\Theta) + g_l(E_l-\Theta)}{\Theta - E_e}\qquad y = f(g_e - g_e^\Theta)$$
- **NXX1 (Noisy X-over-X-plus-1)** — the activation function. XX1 is $f(x)=x/(x+1)$ (Michaelis–Menten form) on $x=\gamma[g_e-g_e^\Theta]_+$, giving **threshold** and **saturation**; convolving with Gaussian noise adds **smoothness**. Biologically the convolution *is* real membrane noise pushing sub-threshold neurons over. Then restore graded dynamics: $y(t) = y(t-1) + dt_{vm}(y^*(x) - y(t-1))$.
- **Term** — **rate code**: a 0–1 real value standing for the spike rate of a ~100-neuron **microcolumn** of similarly-tuned neurons, not one cell.
- **Anti-pattern** — reading off firing rate from membrane potential: empirically one-to-many, so any model or interpretation that treats $V_m$ as a proxy for rate is wrong at the mechanism level.

### Ch 3: Networks — three emergent phenomena
- **Core idea**: Networks produce three things individual neurons cannot: **categorization**, **bidirectional/attractor dynamics**, and **inhibitory competition**.
- **Cortical laminar structure → function** (the anatomy-reads-as-computation argument). Layer thickness varies by area and *that variation is the clue*: **Input** areas (V1) have enlarged **layer 4** (stellate cells, thalamic input); **Hidden** areas have thick **superficial layers 2/3** (the bulk of cortex — abstract categorization); **Output** areas have thick **deep layers 5/6** (subcortical projection). PFC has an even blend of all layers.
- **Connectivity is predominantly bidirectional** (Felleman & Van Essen; Sporns & Zwi): an area sending feedforward almost always receives reciprocal feedback. Feedforward runs Hidden→Input→Hidden up the hierarchy; feedback runs Hidden/Output→Hidden/Output down. Lateral connections act as "self feedback." **This bidirectionality is what later makes biologically plausible error-driven learning possible.**
- **Distributed representation** — many graded detectors active at once. Justified by the *amorphous* (no crisp definition) and *polymorphous* (many simultaneous valid categorizations) nature of real categories. Evidence: Tanaka's graded IT tuning curves; **Haxby et al. (2001)** — object identity decodable from distributed fMRI patterns, *and accuracy barely drops when the maximally-responsive area is excluded*. That last clause is the actual refutation of strict modularity, and the part usually omitted when the study is cited.
- **Coarse coding** — a distributed code where each value is encoded by the *relative balance* across a few broadly-tuned detectors (3 photoreceptors covering the visible spectrum). Efficient: few neurons, fine discrimination.
- **Localist representation** / **grandmother cell** — one unit per category. The "Halle Berry neuron" (Quiroga et al. 2005) is real but does *not* overturn distributed coding: a huge number of neurons respond to the same image, and even that cell shows graded responses to distractors. Localist codes are used in models for input/output convenience, not as biological claims.
- **Weights as projection** — the linear-algebra view: the weight vector dot-products the input vector, **projecting** the high-dimensional input along a behaviorally relevant dimension; learning is finding a good **basis set**. Detector view and projection view are the same operation described discretely vs. continuously.
- **Attractor dynamics / multiple constraint satisfaction** — the gravity-well picture. Many different starting states get sucked into the same stable state = the cleaned-up interpretation. Explains **top-down imagery**, **top-down ambiguity resolution** (the Dalmatian image), **pattern completion**, and **tip-of-the-tongue** states (failure to settle). **Energy** (Hopfield) / **Harmony** (Smolensky) are the sign-flipped formalizations; local updates monotonically reduce energy.
- **Inhibitory competition** gives **sparse distributed representations** (~15–25% active). Functions: set-point activity regulation (the AC-thermostat analogy), **selective attention** (only the most excited detectors clear threshold), and a Darwinian **selection pressure** that makes learning specialize detectors. Without it every neuron weakly detects everything and "accomplish[es] nothing."
- **Feedforward vs. feedback inhibition** — the distinction that matters: **feedforward reflects net excitatory *input*** (anticipatory), **feedback reflects actual activation *output*** (reactive). Pure feedback oscillates; too much feedforward is sluggish; a balance is robust.
- **FFFB inhibition function** — replaces the 1st-edition **kWTA (k-Winners-Take-All)**:
  $$g_i(t) = \text{Gi}\left[\text{ff}(t)+\text{fb}(t)\right],\quad \text{ff}(t)=\text{FF}[\langle g_e\rangle - \text{FF0}]_+,\quad \text{fb}(t)=\text{fb}(t{-}1)+dt[\text{FB}\langle y\rangle - \text{fb}(t{-}1)]$$
  Defaults: Gi=1.8 (the *only* parameter usually tuned; higher = sparser; up to ~3.5 for very sparse layers), FF=1.0, FF0=0.1, FB=0.5, dt=0.7. FFFB is more *proportional* than kWTA's rigid set-point, and closer to how interneurons actually behave.
- **Why shunting inhibition matters**: because inhibition acts through the tug-of-war (only resisting once $V_m$ rises), neurons can activate and then be pulled back. Subtractive inhibition would either block activation entirely or fail to control it. **A mechanism-level reason the specific biophysics is not incidental.**

### Ch 4: Learning — XCAL and the floating threshold
- **Core idea**: Learning = **modification of synaptic weights**, driven by **postsynaptic Ca²⁺ level** in the dendritic spine. Low Ca²⁺ → LTD; high Ca²⁺ → LTP.
- **The five-step plasticity cascade**: (1) postsynaptic $V_m$ elevated — dominated by the **backpropagating action potential** down the dendrites, so the whole neuron knows when it fired; (2) elevated $V_m$ expels **Mg⁺** from NMDA channels, unblocking them; (3) presynaptic spike releases glutamate; (4) glutamate binds NMDA, which opens *only if also unblocked* → Ca²⁺ influx; (5) Ca²⁺ drives second-messenger cascades changing AMPA receptor number/efficacy. **NMDA is thus a coincidence detector requiring both pre- and postsynaptic activity.** **VGCCs** (voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels) add postsynaptic-only Ca²⁺; **mGluR** are metabotropic and modulate rather than pass ions.
- **Hebbian learning**: $\Delta w = xy$. Hebb predicted the NMDA channel functionally before its discovery. Limitation: weights only increase, without bound — "won't do anything useful in a computational model."
- **STDP (spike timing dependent plasticity)** (Bi & Poo 1998): pre-before-post → LTP; post-before-pre → LTD; fits a causal story. **Boundary condition, stated plainly**: STDP "does not generalize well to realistic spike trains" where neurons fire continuously over hundreds of ms (Shouval et al.). It is a *test* for plasticity models, not the learning rule itself. **This is the book's model case of not over-reading a beautiful in-vitro result.**
- **XCAL (eXtended Contrastive Attractor Learning)** — the book's learning rule, derived by *convergence of bottom-up and top-down*: a piecewise-linear fit to the highly detailed biophysical **Urakubo, Honda, Froemke & Kuroda (2008)** model, which independently resembles the **BCM** function and the empirical Ca²⁺→LTP/LTD curve.
  $$\Delta w = f_{xcal}(xy,\ \theta_p),\qquad f_{xcal}(xy,\theta_p)=\begin{cases}(xy-\theta_p) & xy>\theta_p\theta_d\\ -xy(1-\theta_d)/\theta_d & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
  with $\theta_d=0.1$. The Hebbian product $xy$ approximates Ca²⁺ because NMDA needs both sides active.
- **The single unifying idea — one function, two time scales of the floating threshold $\theta_p$:**
  - **Self-organizing (BCM-like)**: threshold = **long-term** average receiver activity $y_l$. **BCM** (Bienenstock, Cooper & Munro 1982): $\Delta w = xy(y-\theta)$, $\theta=\langle y^2\rangle$. Produces **homeostasis**: quiet neurons lower their threshold and "get back in the game"; overactive neurons raise it and "stop hogging everything." Empirically supported by Kirkwood, Rioult & Bear (1996) — dark-reared rats show a *lowered* LTP threshold.
  - **Error-driven**: threshold = **medium-term** average synaptic activity $\langle xy\rangle_m$ (an emerging *expectation*), compared to **short-term** $\langle xy\rangle_s$ (the *outcome*). Because $f_{xcal}$ is near-linear this directly computes $\Delta w \approx x_sy_s - x_my_m \approx x(y_s-y_m)$ — the **delta rule** (Widrow & Hoff 1960) $\Delta w = x(t-y)$ in disguise.
  - **Combined**: $\Delta w = \lambda_l f_{xcal}(x_sy_s, y_l) + \lambda_m f_{xcal}(x_sy_s, x_my_m)$.
- **Term** — **credit assignment**: the delta rule keeps the sending activation $x$ so that only *active* senders get credit/blame; graded activity gives graded credit.
- **Timing framework (the alpha-cycle proposal)**: a **Trial** = 100 ms (10 Hz alpha, deep layers 5/6 + thalamus); a **Quarter** = 25 ms (40 Hz gamma, superficial layers 2/3) — first 3 quarters = **minus phase** (expectation), final quarter = **plus phase** (outcome); a **Cycle** = 1 ms. Terminology from the Boltzmann machine.
- **Contrastive Attractor Learning (CAL)**: the network learns on the contrast between the *late* settling state (better-integrated, the target) and the *trajectory* toward it. The **X** = using a time-averaged state eXtended across settling rather than two discrete snapshots — more biologically accessible. General principle: **later information trains earlier information**, because later information is better.
- **Honest gap flagged by the authors**: the BCM-style long-term floating threshold has solid empirical support; the *fast* error-driven threshold "has not yet been tested empirically" and "stands as an important prediction of this computational model." Preliminary support: Lim et al. (2015).
- **Error-driven vs. self-organizing tradeoff** — the reason to combine them: self-organizing is **robust** (local statistics only) but **myopic/greedy** (no cross-layer coordination); error-driven **coordinates** across layers and solves hard problems but stops when error hits zero and leaves random-looking weights. A balance works best.
- **Backpropagation relationship** — stated precisely, not hand-waved: XCAL/CAL can be *derived from* backprop. The biological difference is that XCAL uses **bidirectional activation dynamics** to communicate error (neurons "feel" the expectation-outcome difference through reciprocal connections and learn on their own state change), whereas backprop propagates a delta value backward across synapses in the wrong direction. Neurons closer to the error source learn most.
- **Weight bounding + contrast enhancement**: exponential approach `if dWt>0 then Wt += (1-Wt)*dWt else Wt += Wt*dWt` keeps weights in [0,1] gracefully — but piles them near 0.5, killing selectivity and breaking inhibitory competition. Fix: sigmoidal contrast enhancement $\hat{w}=1/(1+(w/(\theta(1-w)))^{-\gamma})$ (γ=6, θ=1.25). Interpretation: plain $w$ is the internal plasticity variable (e.g. CAMKII phosphorylation state); $\hat{w}$ is the measurable synaptic efficacy.
- **Learning rate** ($\epsilon$, typically 0.04, decreasing over time): **slower is smarter**. The learning rate sets the effective time window over which experiences are averaged, so slow learning integrates more statistics. The tradeoff is latency to behavior — the authors link this to humans' protracted development.
- **"When exactly is there an outcome?"** — the biggest open problem in the framework, and the authors say so. Four generating situations: (a) explicit teacher correction; (b) expectation of what someone else will say; (c) motor action → predicted sensory outcome; (d) **predictive auto-encoder** — predict your own next input. Current framework: deep layers predict the thalamic pattern in the next alpha trial's plus phase. Alternative marker: a **phasic dopamine burst** flags a salient outcome and gates stronger error-driven learning on top of baseline BCM learning.
- **Leabra** = *Learning in an Error-driven and Associative, Biologically Realistic Algorithm* — the whole package: point neurons + FFFB inhibition + bidirectional connectivity + combined XCAL learning. The name evokes "Libra," the balance.

### Ch 5: Brain Areas — the comparative frame
- **Core idea**: Four major systems differ in *learning signal* and *representational dynamics*, and those differences explain the division of labor.

| Area | Reward | Error | Self-Org | Separator | Integrator | Attractor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Basal Ganglia | +++ | --- | --- | ++ | - | --- |
| Cerebellum | --- | +++ | --- | +++ | --- | --- |
| Hippocampus | + | + | +++ | +++ | --- | +++ |
| Neocortex | ++ | +++ | ++ | --- | +++ | +++ |

- **Separator vs. integrator** — the single most useful axis in the book. Evolutionarily older areas (BG, cerebellum, hippocampus) **separate**: similar inputs → more distinct patterns. Robust, memorization-like, poor generalization. Neocortex **integrates** across experiences to extract generalities — better transfer, at the risk of wrong generalizations.
- **Attractor dynamics cost**: hippocampal CA3 recurrence buys pattern completion but requires precise E/I balance — "the hippocampus is the single most prevalent source of epileptic activity."
- **Lobe-level functional map**: **Occipital** — V1 and up. **Temporal** — *what* pathway into IT; A1 and language; anterior temporal = **semantic knowledge**; MTL→hippocampus = episodic. Characterization: **categorizing**. **Parietal** — *where* pathway; also number, mathematics, abstract relations, frames-of-reference translation; **how** pathway (vision guiding action); S1. Characterization: **metrical** (continuous), in direct contrast to temporal's discrete categories. **Frontal** — M1 → SMA/premotor → PFC, with a posterior-anterior gradient of abstraction; **OFC** (reward value of stimuli), **ACC** (cost/difficulty/uncertainty of actions), **VMPFC** (autonomic, neuromodulatory control).
- **Anti-pattern** — explaining PFC by calling it "the executive": the authors flag this explicitly — "you don't have a little guy in a pinstripe suit sitting in there. It is all just neurons!" **Homuncular labels are the failure mode this book is organized against.**

### Ch 6: Perception and Attention
- **Core idea**: Hierarchical detectors, and attention as **competition**, not as a spotlight module.
- **Early vision mechanism chain**: photoreceptors differ in **wavelength**, **spatial frequency**, and **temporal response** → retinal **center-surround / Difference-of-Gaussian** receptive fields respond only to **contrast**, canceling uniform illumination → V1 **simple cells** integrate a line of aligned LGN on-center cells to detect **oriented edges**; **complex cells** (incl. **end-stop**) integrate over location and polarity.
- **Why V1 has oriented edges, topographically**: oriented edges are the dominant **statistical regularity** of natural images (Olshausen & Field), so *self-organizing* XCAL extracts them. **Topography** arises because neighborhood excitatory connectivity makes nearby neurons co-activate, and activity drives learning → neighbors learn similar things. (Model: `v1rf`.)
- **The invariance/discrimination dilemma** — the crisp statement of why object recognition is hard: bottom-up similarity structure runs *directly against* the desired output structure. Same object in different locations may share *no* retinal features; different objects in the same location share many. **Therefore self-organizing learning alone cannot solve it — error-driven learning is required.**
- **Fukushima's incremental solution**: solve invariance and discrimination *interleaved*, a bit at a time per layer — each layer integrates over a range of locations *and* builds more complex feature conjunctions. **Why it must be incremental**: a fully invariant line detector loses the spatial relationships that distinguish T from L; and learning complex objects independently at every location is combinatorially hopeless. Empirical fit: Kobatake & Tanaka (1994) show complexity *and* receptive-field size increasing together V2→V4→PIT→AIT. (Model: `objrec`.)
- **Spatial attention**: the dorsal *where/how* pathway constrains lower-level ventral processing (V1/V2/V4) to features inside the attended region, enabling recognition in cluttered scenes.
- **Hemispatial neglect**: right parietal damage → neglect of left space, across *multiple reference frames simultaneously* (whole display *and* within each line in line bisection).
- **Posner spatial cueing task**: valid cue → faster RT; invalid → slower than neutral (reallocation costs time); cue-target interval kept short to prevent eye movements, hence **covert attention**. Neglect patients show a disproportionate invalid-cue cost when the cue is in the good field and the target in the neglected one.
- **The decisive theoretical comparison — Posner's disengage model vs. the competition model.** Posner: neglect = damage to a **disengage mechanism**. Competition account: damaged side cannot compete against the intact side, so effects appear only when there is something to compete against. **They make opposite predictions for *bilateral* parietal damage (Balint's syndrome):** disengage predicts *increased* invalid-cue costs on both sides; competition predicts *reduced* attentional effects on both sides, because competition itself is lessened. **The data support competition** (Verfaellie, Rapcsak & Heilman 1990). *This is the book's cleanest worked example of how to adjudicate between two verbal theories with a lesion dissociation.* (Model: `attn_simpl`.)

### Ch 7: Memory — Complementary Learning Systems
- **Core idea**: The **weight-based vs. activation-based** division cuts across all memory. And a *single* system cannot be good at both episodic and semantic learning — the demands are in direct conflict.
- **Catastrophic interference** (McCloskey & Cohen 1989): on the **AB–AC paired associate task**, people retain ~50% of AB after 20 AC iterations; a generic backprop network drops to **0% immediately**. McCloskey & Cohen concluded neural networks were invalidated. The book's reply is the methodological lesson: identify whether the data indict the framework or a parameter choice. Here, raising inhibition (sparser activity → less overlap) already reduces interference — the hippocampus takes that trick to an extreme.
- **Hippocampal anatomy as computation**: cortex → **PHC** (dorsal) / **PRC** (ventral) → **EC** → **DG** and **CA3** via the **perforant pathway** → CA3 recurrent collaterals form the **engram** → **Schaffer collaterals** to **CA1**, which is an **invertible auto-encoder** of EC, so recall runs CA3→CA1→EC→cortex. Function: **bind disparate elements of an episode and reinstate them**.
- **Pattern separation** — sparse coding makes overlap improbable: at 1% activity (DG) two random episodes overlap with probability 0.01%; at 25% (cortex), 6.25%. First identified by **Marr (1971)**. Reinforced by the diffuse, random perforant-path projections producing **conjunctive representations**. Evidence: CA3/CA1 far sparser than EC/subiculum; DG separates more than CA3 across a square↔circle morph (Leutgeb et al. 2007).
- **Pattern completion** — cued recall via CA3 recurrence. Human memory is **content addressable**: any sufficiently specific subset can cue retrieval.
- **The separation/completion tradeoff** (O'Reilly & McClelland 1994) — a genuine tension, not a free lunch: too much separation and a retrieval cue is treated as novel and encoded anew; too much completion and novel episodes get absorbed into old memories. Hippocampal anatomical parameters can be read as optimizing this tradeoff, likely supplemented by PFC control biasing toward encoding vs. retrieval as task demands require.
- **Complementary Learning Systems (CLS)** (McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly 1995; Norman & O'Reilly 2003) — **the central framework of the chapter**. Hippocampus: sparse, separated, fast, one-shot, low interference. Neocortex: overlapping, distributed, slow, integrative, semantic. *The fix that makes a network good at episodic memory is exactly what makes it a bad model of neocortex* — hence two systems. Supporting prediction that holds: exceptional episodic memory co-occurs with impaired generalization (savants; McClelland 2000).
- **Learning-rate demonstration**: at lrate=1 a model only tracks the last outcome; at 0.005 it accurately and stably computes the underlying .66 probability. **This is why slow learning is the price of extracting statistics.**
- **Amnesia**: H.M. (Henry Molaison) — **anterograde** (no new episodic) plus **retrograde** loss, but preserved language, semantics, motor skills, and new motor learning. Slower and more brittle new semantic learning. The **temporally-graded (Ribot) retrograde gradient** is the evidence for **consolidation**. Pharmacological dissociation: **midazolam** (benzodiazepine, GABA-A dense in hippocampus) blocks hippocampal encoding while sparing reinforcement learning → near-pure anterograde amnesia (Hirshman et al.; Frank, O'Reilly & Curran 2006).
- **Consolidation — with the controversy stated**: sleep replay is observed, but "measured levels of reactivation are relatively weak," and there is "considerable controversy over the presence of temporally-graded retrograde gradients in well-controlled animal studies." Verdict offered: probably real, at least sometimes, not ubiquitous. CLS prediction: consolidated memories become **semanticized** and integrated, not faithful copies.
- **Spatial coding**: **place cells** (DG/CA3/CA1, remapping across environments, no topography — consistent with random perforant projections and pattern separation); **grid cells** (EC, hexagonal lattice, oscillation-dependent); **head direction cells** (dead reckoning).
- **Theta waves (8–12 Hz in rats)**: hippocampal areas fire out of phase, letting the system alternate **encoding vs. retrieval** (Hasselmo, Bodelon & Wyble 2002). Ketz, Morkonda & O'Reilly (2013) map this onto Leabra minus/plus phases: strong EC + weak CA3 = encoding (plus phase for the CA1↔EC auto-encoder); strong CA3 + weak EC = recall (minus phase for the CA3→CA1 pathway).
- **Familiarity vs. recollection — the dual-process model.** **Perirhinal cortex** yields a graded **familiarity** signal, hypothesized to arise from the **sharpness** of representations after single-trial self-organizing learning; the hippocampus yields **recollection** of episode details. Evidence: preserved familiarity with hippocampal lesions. "After many years of controversy, it is now widely accepted." Honest gap: why perirhinal sharpness is consciously accessible while similar signals elsewhere are not "has not been identified."
- **Priming** — below awareness. **Weight-based** (slow cortical change; can persist a year+ from a single exposure — a strong constraint against models with synaptic weight decay) vs. **activation-based** (residual firing; very short-lived). Paradigm: **stem completion**; intact in hippocampal patients.
- **Anti-pattern** — inferring absence of memory from failure to recall: "whether a given memory can actually be retrieved depends critically on how the system is probed."

### Ch 8: Motor Control and Reinforcement Learning
- **Core idea**: **BG selects which action; cerebellum executes it well.** Clean division of labor — and notably *no direct connections between them*.
- **BG circuit mechanism**: **striatum** (caudate/putamen; matrix medium spiny neurons = 95%; also patch/striosomes projecting to DA system, and pausing **cholinergic interneurons**) → **Direct/Go** pathway inhibits **GPi** (tonically active) → **disinhibits thalamus** → thalamocortical loop fires → action initiated. **Indirect/NoGo** inhibits **GPe** → disinhibits GPi → *more* thalamic inhibition → action blocked. **Hyperdirect** pathway: **subthalamic nucleus (STN)** receives frontal input directly and diffusely excites GPi = **global NoGo**, raising the gating threshold under conflict to "buy more time" (Frank 2006).
- **Gating** — the key concept: BG **enables** an action without specifying its content. Content lives in the thalamocortical system.
- **Dopamine's two-receptor mechanism**: **D1** dense on Go (excitatory effect), **D2** dense on NoGo (inhibitory effect). A **burst** excites Go / inhibits NoGo → reinforces selection; a **dip** disinhibits NoGo → reinforces avoidance. Tonic DA shifts the Go/NoGo balance at choice time: higher DA → riskier choices.
- **Convergent evidence chain** (worth reproducing as a model of cross-species triangulation): optogenetic D1 vs. D2 stimulation in mice produces opposite gating and opposite repetition effects (Kravitz et al. 2010, 2012); D2 neurons increase activity on negative RPEs predicting avoidance (Zalocusky et al. 2016); pathway-selective blockade doubly dissociates Go-learning from NoGo-learning (Hikida et al. 2010); **Parkinson's** DA depletion impairs Go-learning and *enhances* NoGo-learning, reversed by medication (Frank, Seeberger & O'Reilly 2004); healthy individual differences track D1/D2 genetic variants and PET receptor expression.
- **Dopamine encodes reward prediction error, not reward** (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997): burst to unpredicted reward; after conditioning, burst moves to CS and disappears at reward; **dip** when predicted reward is omitted.
- **Rescorla–Wagner** (1972) $\delta = r - \hat{r}$, $\hat{r}=\sum xw$, $\Delta w = \delta x$ — mathematically identical to the delta rule. **What it fails to explain**: the CS-onset burst.
- **Temporal Differences (TD)** (Sutton & Barto) adds future value: $\delta = (r+f)-\hat{r}$ with $f=\gamma\hat V(t{+}1)$. Derived from the discounted **value function** $V(t)=r(t)+\gamma V(t{+}1)$; TD enforces **consistency of estimates across time**, so $\delta = (r(t)+\gamma\hat V(t{+}1)) - \hat V(t)$. The CS burst arises because the CS onset is itself unpredicted, so $f$ cannot be canceled.
- **Actor–Critic**: critic converts rewards → RPEs; actor produces motor output. **Why RPE and not raw reward is the right training signal**: raw reward would keep driving learning on long-mastered skills (weights growing, interference with new learning). **OpAL (Opponent Actor Learning)** (Collins & Frank 2014): separate G and N weights, scaled by DA at choice — low DA amplifies costs, high DA amplifies benefits.
- **PVLV** (Mollick et al. 2020; Hazy et al.; O'Reilly et al.) — the *biological* implementation TD leaves abstract. **LV (Learned Value)**: **BLA** learns CS→US associations (sensory input from cortex, US from **lateral hypothalamus**), driving DA bursts via **CEA**→VTA/SNc. **PV (Primary Value)**: **ventral striatum** (VSpatch/VSmatrix) inhibits DA neurons to shunt bursts at predicted USs, and drives dips via the **lateral habenula (LHb)**. $\delta_{pv} = US - PV$; $\delta_{lv} = US - LV$. Testable constraint that *matches* odd conditioning data: the CS must still be active at US time for LV learning — hence a memory trace must be sustained (**trace conditioning**).
- **Cerebellum**: input via **mossy fibers** → ~40 billion **granule cells** (each receiving only 4–5 mossy inputs) → **parallel fibers** → ~15 million **Purkinje cells** (up to 200,000 inputs each) → inhibit **deep cerebellar nuclei**. One **climbing fiber** per Purkinje cell from the inferior olive carries the **error/training signal**, driving LTD at active granule synapses. **Marr (1969) / Albus (1971)**: massive high-dimensional expansion in granule cells achieves **separation**, approximating a **lookup table** — robust for arbitrary functions, poor at generalization, which is arguably *correct* for motor control where improper generalization is costly. Same trick as the SVM. Connectivity constraint: cerebellum receives from parietal and motor frontal cortex but **not** from PFC or temporal cortex.

### Ch 9: Executive Function — PFC/BG dynamic gating
- **Core idea**: Executive function = **robust active maintenance** in PFC + **dynamic gating** by BG + **phasic dopamine** to train the gating. No homunculus, no central executive.
- **Evidence PFC is doing this**: **environmental dependency syndrome** (the patient who undressed and got into a researcher's bed on seeing it) — without PFC, behavior is driven by immediate sensory affordances. And **dreaming**: PFC is among the most inactivated areas during dream sleep, which is why dreams lack continuity.
- **Robust active maintenance** — defined narrowly and deliberately: sustained neural firing maintaining a goal or item. The authors define **working memory** as *synonymous with robust active maintenance in PFC* in this context, precisely because the term is used inconsistently in the literature. **Delay-period activity**: Fuster & Alexander 1971; Kubota & Niki 1971; oculomotor delayed response task.
- **Two biological mechanisms for maintenance**: (1) **recurrent excitatory connectivity** — corticocortical loops within a PFC stripe *and* corticothalamocortical loops (layer VI ↔ thalamic relay cells); (2) **intrinsic maintenance currents** — **NMDA** and **mGluR** receptors giving a longer window of excitability. **The NMDA Mg⁺ block is the "hook" for gating**: only already-sufficiently-activated neurons benefit, so a BG-driven thalamic burst can switch maintenance on.
- **The functional argument for activation-based memory**: updating firing patterns changes behavior *immediately*, whereas synaptic change is slow. This is the mechanism of **cognitive flexibility** / rapid task switching (**WCST** in adults, **DCCS** in children). Posterior cortex and BG adapt via slow weight change; PFC adapts via fast activation change.
- **A-not-B task** (Piaget 1954; Munakata 1998 model; Diamond 1990 for DLPFC dependence): infants reach back to A after watching the toy hidden at B. **Development is operationalized in the model as the strength of recurrent excitatory connections** — "older" networks maintain longer. A striking case of a simple parameter explaining a developmental transition.
- **PFC functional organization**: **lateral** = "cold" cognitive control (interconnected with posterior sensory/motor); **medial** = "hot" affective/motivational (interconnected with subcortical). **Dorsal** follows the posterior *How* pathway → **DLPFC** controls motor planning/parietal; **ventral** follows the *What* pathway → **VLPFC** controls temporal-lobe identification and semantics. On the medial side: **ACC** = "hot how" (effort, probability of success, conflict, uncertainty of actions; projects to STN to delay impulsive choice — Frank 2006; Cavanagh et al. 2011); **OFC/VMPFC** = "hot what" (affective value of stimuli). Caveat given: the dorsal/ventral distinction "has been considerably more controversial."
- **Stroop model** (Cohen, Dunbar & McClelland 1990) — **the single most important conceptual correction in the chapter.** Word reading dominates color naming purely as a function of *amount of training*. PFC provides top-down support that lets the weaker pathway compete. **The PFC does *not* send directed inhibition to the word-reading pathway.** It provides **excitatory top-down support to the weaker (color) pathway**, which then out-competes the dominant pathway via *lateral* inhibition. **Inhibition is a collateral, automatic cortical process; control is excitatory biasing of the relevant, not inhibition of the irrelevant.** The same correction applies to A-not-B (Morton & Munakata 2002; Stedron et al. 2005). *Whenever a task is described as "requiring inhibition," check whether the mechanism is actually excitatory support for a competitor.*
- **Stripes**: microcolumn (~20 pyramidal neurons, ~50 µm) → **stripe** (~100 microcolumns, ~250 µm × 1 mm) → **stripe cluster** (~10 stripes). Perhaps ~20,000 stripes in human frontal cortex. Each stripe is **independently gatable** by its own BG loop — this is what permits selective updating of one item while maintaining others.
- **PBWM (Prefrontal cortex Basal ganglia Working Memory)** (O'Reilly & Frank 2006; Hazy et al.): PFC stripes split into superficial (2,3) and deep (5,6) layers; the **Matrix** layer (striatal matrisomes) has Go/NoGo units projecting to a combined **GPiThal** layer, one unit per stripe. Go → deep layers update to current superficial pattern; NoGo → continue maintaining. PVLV dopamine trains Matrix.
- **The temporal credit assignment problem, and its solution**: the decision *what to maintain* happens long before the payoff for having maintained it. Current PBWM solves it with a **synaptic-tag-based trace mechanism** — a gated Matrix unit tags its active synapses; a later phasic DA outcome potentiates all tagged synapses. Empirical grounding: actin-based synaptic tags persist up to ~90 minutes (Redondo & Morris; Rudy; Bosch & Hayashi). **The authors explicitly report this as a replacement for their earlier CS-dopamine scheme, which required the amygdala to decode PFC states and was "much more complex and error-prone."** *A model case of a framework revising its own mechanism on tractability grounds.*
- **Why multiple stripes**: parallel exploration of gating strategies rather than sequential; and genuine multi-item working memory. Consequence: **"superstitious" gating** in stripes whose gating merely coincides with useful gating also gets reinforced.
- **Output gating** vs. maintenance gating: PFC neurons show *either* delay-period firing or output firing, not both. So PBWM pairs maintenance stripes with separately-gated output stripes — information can be held "offline" before being released to drive behavior.
- **SIR (Store / Ignore / Recall) task**: the minimal demonstration. The network starts knowing nothing about the semantics of S/I/R and must learn by trial and error to fire Go on Store, NoGo on Ignore (so stored items aren't overwritten), and output-gate on Recall.
- **Hierarchical PFC-BG** (Badre & Frank 2012; Reynolds & O'Reilly 2009; Collins & Frank 2013): anterior PFC holds **task-sets** contextualizing lower loops. Payoff: abstract, context-independent representations that support **generalization and transfer**, and allow appending new knowledge to existing structures. Empirically, humans (including infants) form such hierarchical task-sets *even when not cued to and even when it does not help learning*.
- **Alternative frameworks — compared, not dismissed**: **Baddeley (1986)** — phonological loop + visuospatial scratchpad + central executive; **Shallice (1988)** — supervisory attentional system (SAS); **traditional symbolic AI / ACT-R**. The stated critique of the AI approach: "it basically designs in the very functionality it aims to explain," and researchers use the very high-level cognition they are explaining to do the designing. Fair concession: symbolic approaches do best on formal math and logic; neural approaches do best on the automatic, lower-level processes. Hybrids exist (**SAL** = Leabra + ACT-R). Chunking of memory representations explains WM capacity limits as a *learning-to-manage-items* problem rather than a fixed slot count (Soni & Frank 2024).

### Ch 10: Language — the integration test
- **Core idea**: Language recruits all prior mechanisms; a **distributed lexicon** replaces a stored word list.
- **Distributed, embodied semantics** (Allport 1985): concrete word meaning lives in modality-specific areas (thunder in auditory, velvet in somatosensory). The **anterior temporal pole** may act as a coordinating **hub** (Patterson, Nestor & Rogers 2007), especially for abstract words.
- **Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA)** (Landauer & Dumais 1997): word co-occurrence within paragraphs + **SVD** dimensionality reduction. **Why raw co-occurrence fails**: synonyms rarely co-occur. SVD/PCA (≈ Hebbian self-organizing learning) recovers them because they co-occur with *the same other words*. Explicitly the same process as V1 edge extraction — idiosyncrasies wash out, strongest statistical groupings emerge. Honest limitation noted: unlike edges, individual LSA clusters have no clean interpretation; only the distributed pattern matters. (Model: `sem`.)
- **Spelling→sound**: English has almost no absolute rules, only **subregularities** — context-dependent partial regularities (*mint/hint* vs *mind/find*, with *pint* as exception). Regularity strength ≈ how many other letters the pronunciation depends on. **Wickelfeature** encoding (Seidenberg & McClelland 1989) captured context but was too rigid and generalized poorly to nonwords; **PMSP** (Plaut et al. 1996) hand-coded letter + multi-letter units. This book's alternative: **reuse the object-recognition hierarchy** — spatial invariance for letters plus increasingly complex letter combinations. Benchmarked against Glushko regulars/exceptions, McCann & Besner controls and pseudo-homophones, Taraban & McClelland; the model tracks human nonword performance closely (e.g. Glushko regulars 95.3 model / 93.8 people). (Model: `ss`.)
- **Triangle model** (Plaut & Shallice 1993) — **orthography ↔ phonology (direct)** and **orthography ↔ semantics ↔ phonology (indirect)**, with *no single lexicon*. Acquired dyslexia by lesion site: **Phonological** — impaired nonword reading, damage to the direct pathway. **Deep** — more severe direct-pathway damage; **semantic substitutions** ("orchestra"→"symphony") because everything routes through semantics without the direct pathway's constraint, plus visual errors. **Surface** — nonwords intact, semantics impaired; *exception* words (yacht) impaired, implying people normally memorize exceptions via the semantic route while the direct route handles regulars. **This triple dissociation from a single model under different lesions is the chapter's strongest evidential argument.** (Model: `dyslexia`.)
- **Sentence Gestalt** (St. John & McClelland 1990): syntax and semantics merge into an evolving distributed representation of overall sentence meaning, avoiding **role-filler binding**. **The mechanistic objection to binding**, stated memorably: rapid bind/unbind is "rather like the rotation of a wheel on a car — it tends to rip apart anything that might otherwise try to attach"; neurons need stable physical synapses. Advantage: ambiguity that plagues syntactic parsing largely dissolves if you only ever compute meaning (*Time flies like an arrow* / *Fruit flies like a banana*; *found by the nosy dog* / *the sleeping dog*; *put the baby to sleep* / *put the dog to sleep*). Honest limits given: tiny language, unclear generalization to new words and scale; Rohde (2002) scales further but reintroduces structured thematic-role propositions (agent, experiencer, goal, instrument, patient, source, theme, …), moving away from the unstructured gestalt.

## Worked Example — how the book adjudicates a theory (Posner disengage vs. competition)
The reasoning template worth copying:
1. **Phenomenon**: neglect patients show a disproportionate RT cost specifically for *invalid* cues when cue is in the good field and target in the neglected field.
2. **Theory A (Posner, box-and-arrow)**: a dedicated **disengage** module is damaged. Fits the unilateral data.
3. **Theory B (competition, implemented)**: bidirectionally interacting spatial and object pathways; the damaged side cannot compete with the intact side, so deficits appear only when there is competition to lose.
4. **Find the case where they diverge**: *bilateral* parietal damage (Balint's syndrome). Theory A → disengage deficits on *both* sides → invalid-cue costs *increase* bilaterally. Theory B → competition is reduced on both sides → attentional effects *decrease*, invalid-cue effect shrinks in magnitude. **Opposite predictions from the same unilateral fit.**
5. **Data decide**: attentional effects are *reduced* (Verfaellie et al. 1990) — competition wins.
6. **The model then reproduces both intact and both lesion patterns with the same parameters** (`attn_simpl`), which is what converts "consistent with" into a mechanistic account.

The generalizable move: two theories that fit the standard finding equally well are separated by finding the manipulation on which a box-and-arrow account and an interaction/competition account must diverge — usually **bilateral vs. unilateral damage, or graded vs. all-or-none degradation**.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **When someone names a cognitive function (attention, inhibition, executive control), ask which mechanism implements it** — otherwise the term is a homunculus. The book's own test: "you don't have a little guy in a pinstripe suit sitting in there."
- **When a task is described as "requiring inhibition of a prepotent response," suspect the real mechanism is excitatory support for the competitor.** Stroop and A-not-B both work this way. Directed inhibition is rare; lateral inhibitory competition plus top-down excitation is the default.
- **When a phenomenon requires fast one-shot learning AND generalization, test the CLS tradeoff.** In the source framework, sparse fast learning and overlapping gradual learning make competing demands. Ask how a proposed single-system model handles both, and compare its assumptions and predictions rather than declaring it impossible by definition.
- **Use separator vs. integrator as the first question about any brain area.** BG/cerebellum/hippocampus separate (robust, memorizing, poor transfer); neocortex integrates (generalizes, risks wrong generalizations).
- **When choosing a learning rate, remember slow ≠ worse.** Learning rate sets the averaging window; a fast rate tracks only the most recent outcome. If a system must estimate a probability or extract statistics, it *needs* a slow rate. Default ε ≈ 0.04, decreasing.
- **Sparse activity ≈ 15–25% in cortex, ~1–5% in DG/CA3.** If a model runs much denser, expect interference; much sparser, expect failure to generalize. In Leabra, tune **Gi** (default 1.8, up to ~3.5 for very sparse layers) and generally nothing else.
- **Within this rate-code approximation, membrane potential alone does not identify firing rate.** Check the model's conductance and threshold assumptions. Separately, failure of recall need not mean absence of memory; probe conditions affect retrieval.
- **Treat STDP as a constraint to satisfy, not a learning rule to implement.** It does not survive realistic spike trains.
- **A dopamine signal is a prediction error, not a reward signal.** If an account has DA tracking reward magnitude, it predicts the wrong thing at CS onset and at omission. Bursts → Go/D1; dips → NoGo/D2. Tonic level shifts risk preference.
- **Gating separates enabling from specifying.** BG decides *whether*; cortex supplies *what*. Any account where BG specifies action content is mixing levels.
- **When two verbal theories fit the same finding, look for the manipulation on which they diverge** — typically bilateral vs. unilateral lesions, or a graded parameter (development, training amount, damage severity) that one theory treats as all-or-none.
- **Prefer the model that reproduces intact *and* damaged behavior from one parameter set.** Fitting only intact data is weak evidence; the dyslexia triple dissociation is the standard to aim for.
- **Flag the open problems rather than papering over them**: (a) when exactly does an "outcome" occur that should drive error-driven learning; (b) the fast error-driven floating threshold is an untested prediction; (c) why perirhinal familiarity is consciously accessible; (d) consolidation gradients are contested in controlled animal studies; (e) the cerebellum's cognitive role remains "somewhat elusive."

## Key Takeaways
1. **One mechanism set, many functions.** Point-neuron integration + bidirectional attractors + inhibitory competition + XCAL explains perception, memory, RL, executive function and language — differences come from connectivity and parameters.
2. **The floating threshold unifies self-organizing and error-driven learning** by changing only its time scale: long-term average (BCM/homeostatic) vs. medium-term average (expectation vs. outcome ≈ delta rule).
3. **Complementary Learning Systems argues for complementary learning dynamics under its assumptions.** Test how competing architectures handle the episodic/generalization tradeoff rather than treating two systems as a universal logical necessity.
4. **Cognitive control is excitatory biasing of the weak pathway, not inhibition of the strong one** — with lateral inhibition doing the competing.
5. **Basal ganglia gate; they do not specify.** The same disinhibitory Go/NoGo circuit that gates motor actions gates working-memory updating in PFC, trained by the same dopaminergic RPE.
6. **Adjudicate theories by finding where they diverge** (bilateral lesions, graded parameters), and demand that a model reproduce intact and lesioned behavior together.
7. **State the open problems.** This book's credibility rests on marking its own untested predictions — do the same when using it.
