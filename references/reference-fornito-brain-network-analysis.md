## Mental Model (read first)
This is the library's **network / connectomics** source. Its governing discipline: a brain graph is a **model**, and every graph-theoretic result is conditional on three modelling choices made before any analysis — **how you define nodes, how you define edges, and how you threshold**. Most contested findings in the field trace back to one of those three rather than to biology.

Its second discipline is the **null model**: a topological measure has no interpretation in isolation. "Small-world," "rich club," "modular," and "hub" are all *comparative* claims, and the comparison is to an explicitly specified ensemble of random networks. **Choosing the null model is choosing the hypothesis.**

**Reach for this book when you need**: what a graph measure actually means; whether a network difference between groups is topology or an artifact of thresholding; how to define hubs, modules, or rich clubs defensibly; the multiple-comparisons problem at the scale of edges; or generative models of why the connectome is wired as it is.

**Coverage note**: front-loads the modelling-choice chapters (3, 10, 11) and the topological constructs that cognitive claims rest on (5, 6, 8, 9). Compressed: Ch 2 (micro/meso/macro connectomics acquisition methods), Ch 4 (degree and weight distributions), Ch 7 (walks, paths, diffusion, navigation and communication models). Dropped with reason: detailed matrix-visualization technique (3.3) and multivariate approaches (11.3) — tooling rather than inference.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Ch 1: Graphs as models — what the abstraction buys and costs
- **Core idea**: a graph is a deliberately impoverished model of a complex system — nodes and the relations among them, and nothing else. The payoff is that a large body of mathematics becomes available; the cost is everything the abstraction discards.
- **The governing epistemic stance, repeated across the book**: "simple models are still useful, **provided that they are not too simple**." And the field's real challenge, stated at the end of Ch 3: "to determine the **minimal level of detail** required to gain a sufficiently comprehensive understanding of brain structure and function."

### Ch 3: Connectivity matrices and brain graphs — the six properties, and where current methods fail
- **Connectivity matrix** (raw weights) vs. **adjacency matrix** (post-threshold, often binarized). Keep these distinct: nearly every reported topological measure is computed on the *adjacency* matrix, i.e. after a modelling choice.
- **The six properties of an ideal brain graph** — the book's most useful audit checklist:
  1. **Spatial embedding** — trivially available, since connectomes are usually built in a standard stereotactic space.
  2. **Heterogeneity of node properties** — **"commonly ignored in neuroscience."** The field defines each region "purely on the basis of its extrinsic connectivity." The defence offered is real but partial: functional specialization is *largely* determined by connectivity profile (Passingham et al., 2002). The cost is also real: thousands of neuron types at microscale; regional variation in cell density, chemoarchitecture, and signature cell types (cerebellar Purkinje cells, motor-cortex Betz cells). Tractable in *C. elegans*, where sensory/motor/interneuron classes can be distinguished; **not tractable at meso- and macroscale, where "it is difficult to delineate boundaries between nodes in a biologically meaningful way" and "the number of nodes that are delineated can be arbitrary given the lack of clearly defined spatial borders."**
  3. **Weighted connectivity** — readily captured, but "the validity of the weights… as an index of the weight of neuronal connectivity depends on the method used."
  4. **Directionality** — mappable by electron microscopy and tract tracing; **cannot be resolved by non-invasive human structural imaging**. Diffusion MRI in particular cannot recover direction. For functional networks, invasive recording or effective-connectivity models can, but computational burden has limited these to small networks.
  5. **Heterogeneity of edge types** — hard without invasive methods. Examples of doing it: separating chemical synapse from gap-junction networks in *C. elegans*; using **laminar organization of interregional projections** in macaque to distinguish feed-forward from feed-back (Felleman & Van Essen 1991; Markov et al. 2013); tract-tracing plus genetic labelling in *Drosophila* to separate excitatory from inhibitory; distinguishing positive from negative correlations in human fMRI.
  6. **Dynamism** — "traditionally been ignored," most studies analysing a single snapshot. **The consequence, stated plainly: "any cross-sectional snapshot of brain connectivity—be it structural or functional—will only offer a limited picture of brain organization."** Neuronal ensembles synchronize and desynchronize rapidly (Varela et al. 2001) and also show slower multi-timescale dynamics (Buzsáki & Draguhn 2004); fast timescales are accessible to invasive recording, EEG and MEG but **not directly to fMRI**.
- **The practical upshot**: as you demand more properties in the graph, **the set of methods able to deliver it shrinks**. "For example, diffusion MRI cannot be used to resolve directionality."

### Ch 4–5: Node measures, centrality, and hubs
- **Node-level connectivity**: **degree** (number of edges) and **strength** (sum of edge weights) are the primitives; **degree distributions** and **weight distributions** characterize the network globally. Brain networks have **fat-tailed weight distributions** — a fact that later motivates *local* rather than global thresholding.
- **Centrality** is a family, not a measure: degree, strength, betweenness, closeness, eigenvector and related indices each formalize "importance" differently. **Ch 5's organizing point is that a node can be central on one measure and peripheral on another**, so a hub claim must name its centrality measure.
- **Identifying hub nodes** (5.2) — the practical problem is that there is no natural threshold for "hub." Standard approaches combine multiple centrality measures rather than relying on one, precisely because they disagree.

### Ch 6: Components, core–periphery, and rich clubs
- **Connected components**: a network is **node-connected** when every node can reach every other by some path. **"In the absence of significant pathology, we expect nervous systems to be node-connected networks."** This expectation becomes a constraint on thresholding (Ch 11).
- **Core–periphery organization** and **rich clubs** — the rich club is the claim that high-degree nodes are more densely interconnected *among themselves* than expected. **The whole construct is null-model-dependent**: the rich-club coefficient φ(k) is compared against an empirical null distribution of φ(k) values generated from randomized networks (Ch 10). Without that normalization the coefficient rises with k trivially, because high-degree nodes have more chances to connect to each other.

### Ch 7: Paths and communication models — what a "short path" presupposes
- **Walks, trails, paths, cycles**, then **shortest-path routing**, **diffusion processes**, and **navigation**.
- **The conceptual point that matters for cognitive interpretation**: shortest-path measures (efficiency, characteristic path length) implicitly assume signals travel along *globally optimal* routes — which requires that the system knows the global topology. **Diffusion and navigation models relax that assumption**, and the book treats them as competing models of neural communication rather than as alternative statistics. *Whenever a paper interprets "global efficiency" as communication capacity, it has adopted the shortest-path routing assumption; say so.*
- **Path length cannot be computed between disconnected components** — which is why fragmentation (Ch 11) is not merely inconvenient.

### Ch 8: Motifs, clustering, and small worlds
- **Network motifs** — recurring subgraph patterns, counted against a null expectation.
- **Clustering coefficient** and **degeneracy**; **small-worldness** as the combination of high clustering with short path length. Humphries' index of small-worldness is the standard scalar summary.
- **Network economy** (8.3) — the wiring-cost framing: brain topology reflects a **trade-off between minimizing wiring cost and achieving topological efficiency**, not the optimization of either alone. This is the conceptual bridge to the generative models in Ch 10.
- **Small-worldness is a ratio against a null**, so everything in Ch 10 applies. It is also strongly affected by density, so everything in Ch 11 applies too.

### Ch 9: Modularity — communities and node roles
- **Defining modules** (9.1) — partitioning nodes into communities with dense within-group and sparse between-group connectivity. **The complications the chapter takes seriously**: module detection is typically an optimization over a quality function with **no unique solution**, results vary across runs and across resolution parameters, and the number of modules is not given by the data.
- **Node roles** (9.2) — once a partition exists, nodes can be classified by whether their connections stay within their module or span modules (provincial vs. connector hubs). **This is the construct that carries most cognitive weight**: "integration" claims usually reduce to connector-hub or between-module connectivity claims.
- **Comparing and aggregating partitions** (9.3) — because a single partition is unstable, defensible practice compares partitions across subjects, runs, or parameter settings and aggregates them.
- **Dynamic modularity** (9.4) — module structure changing over time, the formal apparatus behind "network reconfiguration" accounts of task performance and learning.

### Ch 10: Null models — the inferential core of the whole field
- **Generative models defined**: rules for growing a network in silico. "These rules represent a **generative model** of the network—a model of the processes thought to be important in shaping the topology." Successful replication of observed properties "suggests that the rules defining the model may play an important role in brain network development… **putative mechanisms involved in generating the observed data**."
- **The reframing that makes this a statistical tool**: "generative models… **define expectations for network organization under a specific hypothesis. The hypothesis is determined by the rules of the generative model.**" Hence two distinct applications:
  1. **Uncover constraints on wiring** — specify a model that reproduces observed topology; competing models are competing hypotheses about developmental, aging, or disease constraints. "If we find that the **Erdős–Rényi** model provides a poor fit… we should think about other possible models with additional rules."
  2. **Set expectations under the null** — generate an ensemble of random networks, sample the null distribution of the property, and ask whether the observed value is unlikely under it. Null networks also **normalize** measures so they can be compared across datasets.
- **The load-bearing caveat**: "**Critically, the validity of this approach depends on the appropriateness of the null model.**" A rich club, a small world, and a modular structure are all statements of the form *more than expected under model M* — and changing M changes the answer.
- **Rewiring null models** (10.2) — randomize an observed network by swapping edges while preserving chosen properties (degree sequence being the standard). **The choice of what to preserve is the choice of what counts as "chance."**
- **Functional connectivity networks need their own nulls** (10.3), because correlation matrices have structural constraints (e.g. transitivity of correlation) that edge-swapping ignores — randomizing a correlation matrix as if it were a free graph can produce matrices no correlation structure could generate.
- **Generative models of the connectome — the substantive findings**:
  - **Exponential distance rule (EDR)**: $p_{ij} \propto e^{-\eta D_{ij}}$, where $D_{ij}$ is physical distance and $\eta$ tunes the distance penalty. **At $\eta = 0$ this reduces to Erdős–Rényi** — so the distance penalty is the single added ingredient. Motivated by empirical negative-exponential decay of connection probability with distance. Ercsey-Ravasz et al. (2013) found EDR reproduced, in a dense 29-region macaque tract-tracing connectome, the asymmetries in unidirectional connections, the three-node motif frequency spectrum, the eigenvalue spectrum, and the core structure.
  - **Axonal growth models** (Song et al. 2014) — seed axons in a modelled cranium, grow along linear trajectories with exponentially distributed lengths, targets set by distance-dependent attraction. Reproduced distance-dependence of edge weights, in/out-degree distributions, clustering distribution, and motif spectrum.
  - **Economical clustering model** (Vértes et al. 2012): $p_{ij} \propto k_{ij}^{\gamma} D_{ij}^{-\eta}$, where $k_{ij}$ is the number of **common neighbours**. **Explicitly a trade-off**: adding an edge between nodes with many shared neighbours completes many triangles, "but this favoritism is tempered by wiring length." $\gamma$ and $\eta$ set the balance and are fitted by **simulated annealing**. **The negative result is the informative one: "simple distance-based or preferential attachment models were not sufficient"** to reproduce degree distribution, clustering and global efficiency of human fMRI networks — a clustering bias was required.
  - **Developmental timing as a constraint** (Nicosia et al. 2013): the neuron–synapse relationship in *C. elegans* undergoes a **phase transition at hatching** ($E \sim N^2$ before, linear after), captured by a model trading a long-range-connection penalty against preferential attachment, where the worm's elongation increases distances and hence the penalty.
  - **The collective conclusion**: this work "**underscored the important role of spatial constraints** in determining brain network organization, while also highlighting a role for additional constraints."

### Ch 11: Statistical connectomics — thresholding and inference
#### Thresholding: the choice that generates most artifacts
- **Why it is unavoidable and dangerous**: thresholding removes noisy or spurious links, "**but this step can complicate statistical analysis.**" The framing question: with a patient and a control group, "which single threshold should we choose to make a fair comparison?" — critical **"because many graph theoretical measures are dependent on the number of edges in the graph."**
- **Weight-based thresholding** — fix $\tau$ (arbitrarily, or by a significance criterion). **Consequence: connection density then varies between networks.** Worked example: applying $\tau = 0.20$ to a schizophrenia patient and a control yields **53% density in the patient and 75% in the control**, purely because mean connectivity differs.
- **Density-based thresholding** — let $\tau$ vary per person to fix density $\kappa$. In the same example, matching $\kappa = 0.20$ requires $\tau \approx 0.30$ for the patient and $\approx 0.41$ for the control.
- **THE artifact to know — and it is a live one in the clinical literature.** Density-matching forces the patient's network to retain **more low-value correlations, which may be spurious**, and "one consequence of retaining a higher proportion of potentially spurious connectivity estimates is that **the network topology will appear more random**." Several disorders, schizophrenia among them, are reported to show more random topology alongside mean connectivity reductions (Rubinov et al. 2009; Lynall et al. 2010; Stam 2014). **"The increased randomness in network topology seen in schizophrenia may therefore be a consequence of the density-based thresholding procedure itself."** Affects weighted and binary analyses alike.
- **The conundrum stated honestly, with no clean resolution offered**: topological measures vary with edge count → use density thresholding. But "individual or group variations in connection density may themselves **convey meaningful information**, suggesting that any effect of these variations on network topology is real and should not be removed" → use weight thresholding. **"The choice between the two methods depends on whether or not a difference in connection density is viewed as a confound."** Recommended practice: examine how results vary across thresholds under both approaches.
- **Group-level thresholds** — retain connections statistically robust across the sample (e.g. one-sample *t*-test against zero). Shown to reduce false positives and negatives in diffusion MRI (de Reus & van den Heuvel 2013). **Two costs**: they reduce inter-individual variability in binary structure, so remaining variation is carried almost entirely by weights; and **"any reconstruction bias that consistently generates a false positive connection across the sample will also be declared significant"** — a one-sample *t*-test cannot suppress consistent false positives.
- **Signed weights** need an explicit decision. A positive edge implies "functional cooperation or integration," a negative one "segregation or antagonism." To keep both, threshold on the **absolute** value and carry polarity into later analyses; to keep only positive connections, threshold the signed matrix directly.
- **Network fragmentation**: stringent thresholds split the network into disconnected components, violating the expected node-connectedness and breaking path-based measures. Fixes: restrict the range of $\tau$/$\kappa$ to values preserving connectedness, or **build up from the minimum spanning tree (MST)**, adding edges in decreasing weight order until the target density is reached — which guarantees node-connectedness by construction.
- **Local thresholding** — motivated by the fat-tailed weight distribution: a global threshold "penalizes" connections to nodes with low average connectivity and misses multi-scale structure.
  - **Disparity filter** (Serrano et al. 2009): normalize each edge weight by the node's strength, then compare against a null in which $k-1$ points are dropped uniformly on the unit interval. **The property to understand before using it**: it "will only declare edges significant when there is considerable heterogeneity in the edge weights attached to a given node." Worked counterexample — a node with three edges each of weight 0.70 gives normalized weights of 0.33 each, exactly the chance-expected spacing, so **none is significant despite high raw correlations**. Hence the name: it filters on *disparity*, not magnitude. Edges may be asymmetric after normalization; retain if significant for either endpoint. Does not enforce a target density.
  - **MST + k-nearest-neighbour graph** (Alexander-Bloch et al. 2010): start from the MST, add edges by increasing $k$ until the target density is reached. Cannot hit a density exactly, since one increment of $k$ may add several edges.

#### Inference
- **Global (omnibus) testing** — test a single global measure (average clustering, global efficiency, Humphries' small-worldness) against diagnosis, age, or ability. **Nonparametric statistics are recommended** because "it is often difficult to know the population distribution of most network measures a priori."
  - **Its failure mode, stated precisely**: "simple and offers insight into global network properties, but **lacks specificity and may lack power when the effect of interest is limited to only a few connections or nodes**. For example, if the clustering coefficient shows a difference at a single node in a network of hundreds of nodes, an omnibus test performed on the average clustering coefficient is unlikely to reveal a significant effect."
- **Mass univariate testing** — the same test run independently across many nodes or edges, yielding a **statistical parametric network** (Ginestet & Simmons 2011). Localizes effects but creates the multiple-comparisons problem at a brutal scale.
  - **The scale asymmetry that determines your correction strategy**: with a family of **nodes**, $J = N$, and "controlling the FDR may provide sufficient statistical power." With a family of **edges**, $J \approx N^2$ — which is why "most connectome-specific multiple comparison procedures have been designed for the case when the family comprises connections."
  - Generic corrections: **Bonferroni** ($p_j \le \alpha/J$) for strong FWER control; **FDR** (Benjamini & Hochberg 1995). Connectome-specific procedures **"exploit the spatial and topological properties of brain networks"** to gain power over generic corrections while still controlling FWER — the same logic by which cluster-based correction beats Bonferroni in voxelwise fMRI (see [[reference-jahn-brain-book]]).
  - You may **constrain the family a priori** to a subset of nodes or edges to reduce the correction burden — the network analogue of a confirmatory ROI.
- **Interpreting clinical network differences** — the chapter's warning against reading a difference map as a mechanism. "Identifying a set of connections that is either increased or decreased in a given patient group may be useful, but the map will be most informative when it **tells us something about the underlying disease process**." The interpretive difficulty is that disorders produce mixed patterns: schizophrenia shows reduced structural connectivity **coupled with both increased and decreased functional connectivity**; multiple sclerosis, despite being a disease of structural deterioration, likewise shows a complex mix. Three candidate mechanisms the book names for framing such maps: **transneuronal degeneration** (deterioration of remote connected areas, via aberrant signalling, axonal transport deficits, or prion-like propagation), **dedifferentiation** (breakdown of normally segregated specialized processes — e.g. reduced frontoparietal activation with increased activation elsewhere in schizophrenia, alongside reduced modularity), and impaired interregional synchronization following lesion.

## Worked Example — auditing a "patients show more random network topology" claim
The chapter 11 reasoning, as a procedure. Run it on any group-difference network result:

1. **What are the nodes?** Which parcellation, how many, and were boundaries biologically motivated or arbitrary? Node count changes nearly every measure, and at macroscale "the number of nodes that are delineated can be arbitrary."
2. **What are the edges?** Structural or functional; weighted or binary; directed (and if human diffusion MRI, direction is *not* recoverable); how were negative weights handled?
3. **Was mean connectivity different between groups?** If yes, this is where the artifact enters. Lower mean connectivity in patients is extremely common.
4. **Which thresholding was used?**
   - *Density-matched* → the patient networks necessarily retained more low-weight, likely-spurious edges, which sit approximately at random → **the "more random topology" may be manufactured by the method**. This is the Rubinov/Lynall/Stam pattern the book explicitly flags.
   - *Weight-matched* → densities differ between groups, and most topological measures are density-dependent → **the difference may be a density effect rather than a topology effect**.
   - Either way the finding is confounded; the question is which way.
5. **Did they show results across a range of thresholds and both methods?** This is the recommended defence, and its absence is a real limitation.
6. **Did the networks fragment?** If path-based measures (efficiency, characteristic path length) were computed, were all networks node-connected? Were MST-based methods used to guarantee it?
7. **What null model normalized the topology measure?** Small-worldness, rich-club coefficients and modularity are all *ratios against a null*. Which properties did the null preserve — degree sequence only? For functional networks, was a correlation-appropriate null used?
8. **If specific edges were reported, how was FWER controlled** across $J \approx N^2$ hypotheses?
9. **Finally, what mechanism does the map imply?** Transneuronal degeneration, dedifferentiation, impaired synchronization — or is the map being presented as though it were itself an explanation?

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **Report the node definition, edge definition, and threshold before any topological result** — they are modelling choices, not preprocessing, and the result is conditional on all three.
- **A brain graph ignores node heterogeneity by default.** If your claim depends on regional specialization (cell types, chemoarchitecture, laminar structure), a connectivity-only graph does not carry it.
- **Never infer directionality from diffusion MRI.** Direction requires tract tracing, electron microscopy, invasive recording, or an effective-connectivity model.
- **Treat any cross-sectional graph as a snapshot of a dynamic system**, and note that fMRI cannot access the fast timescales that EEG/MEG can.
- **Density-matched thresholding manufactures apparent randomness in the lower-connectivity group.** Suspect it whenever "more random topology" co-occurs with reduced mean connectivity — the schizophrenia literature is the standing example.
- **Weight-matched thresholding leaves densities unequal, and most measures are density-dependent.** There is no free choice here: decide explicitly whether a density difference is a confound or a finding, and say which.
- **Show results across a range of thresholds under both schemes.** A single-threshold group comparison is not interpretable.
- **Check node-connectedness before computing path-based measures.** Build from the minimum spanning tree if you need a guarantee.
- **Group-level thresholds cannot suppress consistent false positives** — a reconstruction bias present in every subject will be declared significant by a one-sample *t*-test.
- **Use local thresholding (disparity filter) when weight distributions are fat-tailed**, but know it is sensitive to *heterogeneity*, not magnitude: uniformly strong edges at a node can all be non-significant.
- **Every topological claim is a claim against a null model. Name the null and what it preserves.** Small-world, rich club, modularity, motif frequency — none has meaning as a raw number.
- **Use a correlation-appropriate null for functional networks**; edge-swapping randomization ignores the constraints on correlation matrices.
- **Treat a generative model as a hypothesis about wiring constraints, and report the models that failed.** Vértes et al.'s finding that distance and preferential attachment were *insufficient* is what makes the clustering term informative.
- **Module partitions are unstable**: report the resolution parameter, run the detection many times, and aggregate or compare partitions rather than reporting one.
- **Distinguish global from localized effects before choosing a test.** Omnibus tests on global measures miss effects confined to a few nodes; mass univariate localizes but costs you $J \approx N^2$ comparisons.
- **Prefer connectome-specific FWER procedures to Bonferroni for edge-level families**, and consider constraining the family a priori — the network analogue of a confirmatory ROI.
- **Shortest-path measures assume globally optimal routing.** If you interpret efficiency as communication capacity, state that assumption; diffusion and navigation models are the alternatives.
- **A difference map is not a mechanism.** Ask which process — transneuronal degeneration, dedifferentiation, impaired synchronization — the pattern implies, and note that structural and functional changes frequently move in opposite directions within the same disorder.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Node definition, edge definition, and threshold are modelling choices** made before analysis, and they determine the result more often than biology does.
2. **The six-property audit** (spatial embedding, node heterogeneity, weights, directionality, edge types, dynamics) tells you what your graph does *not* represent — and current methods deliver few of them at once.
3. **Density-based thresholding can manufacture "random topology" in a lower-connectivity group.** This is the most consequential artifact in the clinical network literature.
4. **Every topological measure is comparative.** Small-worldness, rich clubs, modularity and motifs are meaningful only against an explicitly specified null, and the null encodes the hypothesis.
5. **Generative models turn "why is it wired this way?" into a testable comparison.** Spatial/wiring constraints dominate, but distance alone is insufficient — clustering bias is required for human functional networks.
6. **Global tests lack localizing power; edge-level tests face $J \approx N^2$.** Choose deliberately, and use topology-exploiting correction.
7. **Module partitions are non-unique.** Report resolution and aggregate across runs.
8. **Interpret difference maps against candidate disease mechanisms**, and expect structural and functional changes to dissociate.
