# Computational Psychiatry: A Primer — Peggy Seriès (ed.)
**Format**: md (from pdf, MIT Press 2020) | **Pages**: ~504 | **Sections**: 11 chapters | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
This is the library's **psychopathology and model-fitting-practice** source. Its premise is a diagnosis of a field in trouble: mental illness carries an enormous burden, "there has been **stagnation in the discovery of new pharmacological drugs and treatments in recent decades**," and diagnosis has been problematic for centuries. The **DSM** has proven "clinically very valuable," but its **heterogeneous phenotypes and atheoretical structure make it difficult to connect to biological mechanisms**. For most disorders "it will be impossible to pin down a single cause, a single organic substrate, or a single time course."

The research program, aligned with NIMH's **RDoC**, uses formal models to generate candidate research measures such as learning rate, decision threshold, and prior precision. A group difference can motivate a mechanistic hypothesis, but clinical use requires evidence of reliable measurement, predictive validity, and usefulness in the intended population. **RDoC is a research framework, not a diagnostic guide or a replacement for current diagnostic systems** ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/about-rdoc)). This distinction is an editorial clarification, not a claim that the book established clinical validation.

**Reach for this book when you need**: how cognitive models generate candidate research measures; parameter and model recovery, comparison, and group modelling; or the computational hypotheses about psychiatric disorders covered by this source. Whether a measure supports clinical decisions requires separate validation.

**Coverage note**: front-loads Ch 2 (Seriès's methods survey, including the model-fitting workflow — the most transferable content in the book) and Ch 1's diagnostic framing, then the disorder chapters' conclusions. Compressed: Ch 4 (cognitive control models) and Ch 5 (valence systems / utility) — overlapping [[reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro]] and [[reference-forstmann-model-based-cogneuro]]. The DDM and RL sections of Ch 2 are summarized only where they add a clinical angle, since [[reference-forstmann-model-based-cogneuro]] and [[reference-stocco-explanatory-models]] treat them at greater depth.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Ch 1: Why psychiatry needs this — the RDoC framing
- **The nosology problem, stated concretely**: DSM criteria are polythetic — "to meet criteria for major depressive disorder, at least five of" a list of symptoms — so two patients with the same diagnosis may share almost nothing. Kraepelin's original challenge was building a nosology "in the face of diverse, nonspecific clinical presentations, and in the absence of a clear understanding of natural causes."
- **RDoC research framing**: study dimensions of psychological and biological functioning across health and illness and across units of analysis. This research strategy does not establish individual diagnostic thresholds or replace clinical diagnostic systems; evaluate any proposed clinical measure separately.
- **What computation adds to RDoC specifically**: causally linking distinct units of analysis; **explicitly incorporating time**, "enriching our ability to understand how functional neurocognitive architectures develop and to identify **critical temporal windows** associated with abnormal developmental trajectories"; and modelling dynamic interactions between constructs across neurodevelopment.
- **The prevention argument**: understanding developmental trajectories "should open up a range of potential **preventative** approaches to mental illness."

### Ch 2: Methods — the four model families and what each is *for*
The chapter's own framing, worth keeping as a selection rule: "The choice of the model will depend on **the data to be modeled, the hypothesis that is tested, and the questions to be addressed.**"

- **Neural networks / circuit models** — "explore the relationship between **connectivity, dynamics, and function**." In psychiatry, used to ask how **attractor dynamics** could be impaired.
- **Drift-diffusion models** decompose choice and RT differences into candidate drift, boundary, or non-decision-time differences under the model. Such group-level decompositions are research hypotheses; recoverability, measurement reliability, and clinical validity must be established before individual clinical interpretation.
- **Reinforcement learning models** — model learning dynamics where states or objects carry reward/punishment. **Why they dominate clinical work**: "Because of the link between **prediction-error signals and dopamine**, reinforcement learning models have shown to be very promising tools in understanding impairments in learning and decision making in mental illness."
- **Bayesian models** — account for learning and decision making as statistical inference. **Two distinctive uses**: assess "how **optimal** a given performance is," and "**discover the internal models** that participants have learned or use." And the clinical payoff: "Because they explicitly model **beliefs**, they can be used to describe mental illness in terms of **maladaptive or broken beliefs and false inference**."

#### The Bayesian framework in detail (Seriès's own section)
- **The premise**: we live in a world of uncertainty with ambiguous input and limited receptors, so "the best our brain can do is to try to **guess** what is happening" — the brain as a "guessing machine," forming and evaluating hypotheses via **posterior ∝ likelihood × prior**.
- **Four assumptions this makes as a model of the brain** — worth checking individually before accepting a Bayesian account: (1) uncertainty is tracked by maintaining probabilities over interpretations; (2) the brain has developed, through development and experience, **an internal model in the form of priors and likelihoods**; (3) evidence and priors are combined **in a principled way, through Bayes' rule or an approximation**; (4) machine-learning algorithms are a legitimate source of hypotheses about implementation.
- **The empirical base**: **multisensory integration** gives Bayes clear, testable predictions — sources should be weighted by **reliability**, and the combined estimate should be more reliable than either alone. This predicts illusions where modalities conflict and one is far more reliable (**ventriloquism**, **McGurk**). "When Bayesian model predictions are compared to experimental data, the general finding is that **human behavior is well approximated by Bayesian integration**."
- **How to find a prior — a genuinely useful method**: "A good way to discover the brain's expectations or assumptions is to study perception or cognition **in situations of strong uncertainty or ambiguity**." That approach has revealed priors for **light coming from above**, object symmetry, smooth change in space and time, horizontal/vertical orientations and perpendicular corners, **convexity**, uniform background colour, slow or stationary objects, gaze directed at the observer, and convex faces.
- **Priors are learned and revisable**: participants "learn quickly, effortlessly, and unconsciously the statistics of the perceptual environment," which biases estimation toward the expected — "sometimes even **'hallucinating' expected inputs, even when they are absent**." Even long-term priors (light-from-above, slow motion) update when the environment changes. *That hallucination result is the bridge to the psychosis chapter.*
- **Decision theory** extends inference to action: Bayes gives beliefs about states but not what to do. Choose the action minimizing **expected loss**, $\sum_s L(a,s)P(s|D)$. **The relationship to RL, stated exactly**: Bayesian decision theory "encompasses the methods of reinforcement learning… The difference, of course, is that here we have to **infer** the states instead of them being **given**, as commonly assumed in reinforcement learning formalisms." **When the state $s$ is known, BDT reduces to RL.**
- **Tractability**: "Exact Bayesian inference is thought to be **intractable** for most everyday problems," so the live research question is which heuristics approximate it. The honest open question: "**It is as yet unclear whether the Bayesian approach is only useful at the 'computational' level… or whether it can be also useful at the 'implementation level.'**"
- **Three classes of failure mode** that could underlie mental illness (Huys et al. 2015) — a clean taxonomy for framing any computational-psychiatry hypothesis:
  1. **Abnormalities in the framing of the problem** — the priors, likelihoods, or utilities;
  2. **Abnormalities in the mechanisms of cognition** used to solve it;
  3. **Abnormalities in the historical data** available from the environment — i.e. abnormal experience such as trauma.
- **Predictive coding** (Rao & Ballard 1999) — a hierarchy where **feedback connections carry predictions of lower-level activity and feedforward connections carry the residual prediction errors.**
  - **The relationship to Bayes, stated carefully**: the two "are concepts that are often **confounded**." They "do not necessarily imply each other (Aitchison and Lengyel 2017), [but] predictive coding is often proposed as an effective way for Bayesian inference to be **implemented**: while Bayesian inference would describe the general **computation**… predictive coding would describe the **algorithm** that is being carried out."
  - **Why they connect** — the derivation worth knowing. With Gaussian beliefs summarized by mean μ and **precision π** (inverse variance), sequential Bayesian updating becomes
    $$\mu_{post} = \mu_{prior} + \frac{\pi_{likelihood}}{\pi_{posterior}}(x - \mu_{prior}), \qquad \pi_{posterior} = \pi_{prior} + \pi_{likelihood}$$
    The term $(x - \mu_{prior})$ **is a prediction error** — so "Bayesian inference can be implemented by **iteratively updating predictions with the prediction error** produced by each new measurement."
  - **Precision-weighting is the mechanism that matters clinically**: $\pi_{prior}$ is confidence in the existing prediction; $\pi_{likelihood}$ is the reliability of new data. The ratio $\pi_{likelihood}/(\pi_{prior} + \pi_{likelihood})$ **plays the role of a learning rate** — so beliefs are highly sensitive to new data when little is known ($\pi_{prior}$ small) and insensitive once much has been gathered. **Aberrant precision is therefore a single mechanism that can produce either over- or under-weighting of evidence.** And the result generalizes beyond the univariate Gaussian case.
  - **The unresolved implementation debate**, flagged rather than glossed: whether visual-cortex activity should be read as **prediction errors** (Rao & Ballard) or as **the predicted input itself**, or whether distinct neuron classes represent each.
- **Hierarchical Gaussian Filter (HGF)** (Mathys et al. 2011, 2014) — a hierarchical generative model in which all states above the lowest evolve as **coupled Gaussian random walks**, each state setting the step size of the level below. In the canonical face/house task: **$x_1$** = uncertainty about outcomes, **$x_2$** = uncertainty about cue–outcome contingencies, **$x_3$** = uncertainty about **volatility** — how fast contingencies change. Mean-field approximation yields analytic update equations "whose form resembles reinforcement learning updates… with **dynamic learning rates and precision-weighted prediction errors**," and whose coupling parameters are **fitted per participant**.
  - **The worked clinical result**: fitting HGF to autistic participants (Lawson et al. 2017) showed they "tended to **overestimate the volatility** of the sensory environment, at the expense of building stable expectations that would lead them to be surprised when aberrant outcomes arise."
- **Belief propagation and circular inference** — in a hierarchical generative model (leaf ← tree ← forest), sensory information climbs the hierarchy while priors descend, each node computing a belief and passing messages. **The critical correction**: a message from node $i$ to node $j$ subtracts the effect $j$ already had on $i$. "Without it, the algorithm would produce **loops**… In such 'loopy' belief propagation, **the consequences are treated as causes, and vice versa**, and the information in the upward and the downward stream can be **mixed and overcounted**." Jardri & Denève propose this **circular inference** could underlie schizophrenia symptoms "and may also be present to some extent in the general population."

#### Model fitting and comparison — the workflow (Section 2.5)
**Three general steps, in order: build the model → validate on artificial data → apply to real data.**
1. **Clarify the hypotheses first.** Build a model that (i) is flexible enough and connected to prior literature, (ii) **contains parameters that directly relate to your hypotheses**, and (iii) is as simple as possible given those constraints. **The recommended strategy: build a series of models starting from a simple "null" model that omits the element of interest, then add features to see how much each contributes.** Include a probabilistic component for intrinsic variability.
2. **Validate on artificial data** — generate data with hand-set parameters, then:
   - **Parameter recovery** — can the fitting procedure recover the parameters you used? **"This is an important step prior to interpreting any parameters."**
   - **Model recovery** — can you distinguish the behavior generated by different models and reliably identify the right one?
   - **Do this before collecting data**, "since they may suggest changes in experimental parameters, such as the number of trials or the number of subjects to run."
3. **Validate on the real data** — compare model-generated data (with fitted parameters) against the real data; for learning experiments, plot learning curves and ask whether the model captures their shape.
- **Fitting**: maximum likelihood maximizes $\log p(a_{1:T}\mid\theta)$ with trial history represented by the model. If parameters trade off, inspect the likelihood, identifiability, and recovery under the actual task. MAP adds a log prior and may stabilize estimates with justified priors, but can bias them and cannot create missing information. Evaluate prior sensitivity and recovery; empirical Bayes also introduces assumptions and uncertainty.
- **Model comparison**: the marginal likelihood $p(A\mid M)=\int p(A\mid\theta,M)p(\theta\mid M)d\theta$ averages likelihood with respect to the parameter prior. $BF_{12}=p(A\mid M_1)/p(A\mid M_2)$ compares specified models and priors. Report the direction, numeric value, estimation method, and prior sensitivity. Verbal evidence scales are conventions; a raw BF above 5 is not universally conclusive. Do not confuse BF, log BF, and twice log BF. The old threshold attribution to this book remains unverified. BIC is an asymptotic approximation under regularity conditions; AIC targets a different predictive criterion. Relative preference still requires absolute predictive checks.
- **The warning that must accompany any model-comparison result**: "**Model comparison is always relative: even the best amongst a set of models may still be too poor to provide any meaningful information.** The interpretation of parameters in the models should only follow at the end, once one model has been chosen as a satisfactory characterization of the data."
- **Group studies** — three options, with verdicts: **fixed effects** (all individuals share parameters) "**confounds inter- and intra-individual variability and is therefore not recommended**"; **random effects** (entirely separate parameters) "can inflate noise"; **hierarchical** (parameters cluster around a population value) is the recommended solution. Separately, one can allow **different individuals to use different models** (random effects over models) or **nest models** to represent a mixture of strategies used across the whole group.

### Ch 3: Biophysical circuit models — E/I balance
- **Working memory** depends on **persistent delay-period activity**, modelled with **attractor** networks; **decision making** corresponds to **ramping activity**, i.e. evidence accumulation. *Both link directly to [[reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro]] and [[reference-forstmann-model-based-cogneuro]].*
- **The prediction-then-confirmation story that makes this chapter exemplary**: simulations found that excitatory reverberation and sustained activity "**require slow synapses, and particularly NMDA receptors**." That "theoretical prediction was verified experimentally and is meaningful for research in psychiatry: **NMDA impairments have indeed been observed in schizophrenia** and other psychiatric illness."
- **The general mechanism**: changes in the **balance of synaptic excitation and inhibition** (via NMDA impairment) "give rise to specific impairments in working memory and decision making." Also emphasizes **linking propositions** for cognitive processes (§3.3) — the same construct as in [[reference-forstmann-model-based-cogneuro]].

### Ch 5: Valence systems — why choice is fragile
- The chapter's closing framing is the best one-sentence statement of computational psychiatry's rationale: "although it might seem that nothing could be simpler than learning to favor actions that lead to positive outcomes, there are actually many richly complicating factors" — tailoring behavior to long-run goals, adapting goals to context, and using hard-wiring and heuristics "to avoid as much of the cost and danger of learning as possible." **"These factors leave an architecture of choice replete with readily exposable flaws and vulnerability to the sort of psychiatric disorder on which this book concentrates."**

### Ch 6: Psychosis and schizophrenia — the best-developed case
- **Negative symptoms** ("broadly, the failure to act to obtain reward") "have been modeled using **RL models with some success**."
- **Positive symptoms** (delusions, hallucinations) are "**less straightforward**." Two families of account:
  1. **Abnormal dopamine signaling in striatum** — contributing to delusions through **aberrant salience**, aberrant RPEs, or aberrant gating of thoughts.
  2. **Abnormal synaptic gain at both ends of the cortical hierarchy** — producing "an imbalance (or alternatively a **loss of adaptability**) in the encoding of **precision**… such that **prior beliefs are underweighted, and sensory evidence is overweighted**."
- **The candid verdict**: "**Such models have not yet given a full account of positive symptoms.**"
- **Cognitive symptoms** — concentration and working memory problems modelled with **spiking attractor networks**: "**NMDAR hypofunction, perhaps resulting in an increased E/I ratio (due to disinhibition), can make attractors unstable and easily affected by random fluctuations** in neural firing." This explains spatial working memory performance in schizophrenia **and** "apparent biases in probabilistic inference" — one mechanism, two symptom classes.
- **Psychological biases** such as **jumping to conclusions** "are beginning to be understood in modeling terms."
- **What is still needed**: "a deeper understanding of how the **thalamus, striatum, and cortex** all interact with each other, and with dopamine, in performing inference."

### Ch 7–10: Depression, anxiety, addiction, Tourette
- **Depression** — modelling supports theories implicating "**negative (perceptual) biases, deficient cognitive control, impaired reward learning, and beliefs about the controllability of the environment**." The chapter's methodological demands are unusually specific: **clever task design, replication with larger samples**, and — importantly — **not neglecting moderate-to-severe and treatment-resistant patients** "rather than participants with low mood or mild forms of depression, who are often easier to study." **The stated goal: move "from findings that are able to distinguish between groups of patients and healthy control participants to results that show convincing individual differences along symptom dimensions"** — the prerequisite for individual treatment prediction. *That group-vs-individual distinction is the field's central unsolved problem.*
- **Anxiety** — associated with abnormal learning about **aversive** outcomes. Findings so far: anxious individuals "utilize **different decision-making and learning heuristics**, show **reduced sensitivity to statistical aspects of the environment**, and **biased evidence accumulation during threat perception**." Honest status: "computational work in anxiety is **in its infancy**," with promise to "identify novel cognitive processes… **not apparent using standard analytic approaches**."
- **Addiction** — the framing claim: "**addiction is fundamentally a problem with decision making**," so decision models (economic, motivational/pharmacological, or neurosystem) "have been important to our definitions and understanding," and "have led to new treatments and new modifications that could improve those treatments." *Connects to model-free RL's habitual inflexibility in [[reference-stocco-explanatory-models]].*
- **Tourette syndrome** — the book's most complete mechanistic chain, and the clearest demonstration of the method:
  - **Dopaminergic hyperinnervation** → increased **phasic and tonic** dopamine → **overactive habit system** → "**tics likely being persistent, maladaptive motor habits**," which explains why the motor loop is centrally involved in both.
  - **Treatment consilience**: this "explains why **all medications with well-established efficacy for TS reduce dopaminergic neurotransmission**."
  - **Premonitory urges** — "aversive, distressful sensations" implicating somatosensory cortices and insula — **precede and are terminated by** tics. Termination "likely elicits **positive prediction errors**, which are signaled by phasic dopamine and **reinforce tics**" — excessive under hyperinnervation, further contributing to tic learning.
  - **The strategy statement, which is the methodological lesson of the whole book**: "We started with a **well-motivated computational model of the function** of CBGTC circuits and the role of dopamine therein, **for which there is much evidence independent of TS**; to understand dysfunction computationally, it is fundamental to **first understand function**, to be able to understand how such function may be disrupted. We then used this model to investigate the multiple implications of a **simple and parsimonious hypothesis**… We found that this simple hypothesis… provided an explanation for a very broad range of experimental and clinical findings."

### Ch 11: Perspectives
- The closing assessment is appropriately modest: "Computational psychiatry is still **in its infancy**. While the potential of the field is clear, there is still much to do to take computational psychiatry **from the laboratory to the clinic**. As the field matures, improved and **unified methodologies** will be needed, as well as **large-scale, collaborative, interdisciplinary consortia**."
- **Autism** is treated as a Bayesian case in §11.1: "the influence of prior expectations might be **weaker** compared to that of sensory inputs, which could explain why patients feel overwhelmed by a world perceived as being **'too real.'**"

## Worked Example — building a computational-psychiatry study that will survive scrutiny
The Ch 2 workflow plus the Ch 10 strategy, combined:

1. **Start from a model of *function*, not dysfunction.** The Tourette chapter's explicit method: use a circuit model "for which there is much evidence **independent of** the disorder." A model built to fit patient data explains nothing.
2. **State a simple, parsimonious hypothesis about what is disturbed** — dopaminergic hyperinnervation; NMDA hypofunction raising E/I ratio; aberrant precision-weighting — and classify it: is this a failure of **framing** (priors/likelihoods/utilities), of **cognitive mechanism**, or of **historical data** (abnormal experience)?
3. **Choose the model family from the question**: circuit model for connectivity–dynamics–function; DDM to decompose an RT/accuracy difference; RL for reward-driven learning (and its dopaminergic link); Bayesian for beliefs and optimality.
4. **Build a model series from a null model upward**, so each added feature can be credited.
5. **Simulate the proposed design and test parameter and model recovery before interpretation, preferably before collection.** If ML estimates trade off, evaluate task information, identifiability, parameterization, and sample size. Consider MAP or hierarchical regularization with justified priors, then test bias, prior sensitivity, and recovery again. A stable MAP estimate alone does not show the data identify the parameter.
6. **Check predictions against observed data and compare candidate models using a justified criterion.** For a Bayes factor, report its direction, numerical scale, priors, and estimation uncertainty; qualitative thresholds are conventions, not universal conclusions. Evaluate prior sensitivity and predictive adequacy. BIC approximations and predictive criteria have different assumptions.
7. **Only now interpret parameters** — and only after confirming the winning model is adequate in absolute terms, since "even the best amongst a set of models may still be too poor."
8. **Assess pooling for the group design.** Hierarchical modelling can balance individual noise and shared structure; compare its assumptions and recovery with the alternatives.
9. **Distinguish group differences, individual prediction, and clinical utility.** Associations with symptom dimensions can be informative but do not establish diagnostic or treatment value. Match the severity spectrum and treatment history of the sample to the intended inference; severe or treatment-resistant cases are not a universal study requirement.
10. **Look for consilience beyond the fit** — Tourette's account explains the *pharmacology* of effective treatment, not just the behavioral data. That is what distinguishes a mechanism from a curve fit.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **Model function before dysfunction.** A model with independent evidence for the healthy case, perturbed by one simple hypothesis, is far stronger than a model fitted to patient data.
- **Classify the proposed failure mode**: framing (priors, likelihoods, utilities), cognitive mechanism, or environmental history. Accounts that don't specify which are underdetermined.
- **Choose the model family from the question**, not from familiarity: circuits for dynamics, DDM to decompose RT/accuracy, RL for reward learning and dopamine links, Bayesian for beliefs and optimality.
- **Run parameter recovery before interpreting any parameter.** "This is an important step prior to interpreting any parameters" — and do it *before* data collection, since it constrains trial and sample size.
- **Run model recovery too**: if two candidate models generate indistinguishable behavior in your design, no amount of fitting will adjudicate them.
- **When parameters trade off, evaluate regularization and recovery.** MAP can help under suitable priors; compare prior sensitivity, identifiability, and recovery for the actual model rather than treating MAP as uniformly superior to ML.
- **Choose a comparison criterion for the inferential goal.** Bayes factors compare prior-weighted marginal likelihoods; BIC is an approximation under regularity conditions, and predictive criteria address other goals. Report priors and sensitivity for Bayesian evidence. Relative superiority does not establish absolute adequacy.
- **Consider hierarchical group models when partial pooling fits the study.** Assess individual variation, model heterogeneity, priors, and recovery before choosing pooled, separate, or hierarchical estimates.
- **In predictive-coding accounts, precision is the parameter that does the work.** The learning rate is $\pi_{likelihood}/(\pi_{prior}+\pi_{likelihood})$, so aberrant precision produces over- *or* under-weighting of evidence from a single mechanism — but say which direction and at which hierarchical level.
- **Do not conflate Bayesian inference with predictive coding.** They do not imply each other; Bayes is a computational-level description, predictive coding a candidate algorithm.
- **Do not assume Bayesian models constrain implementation.** Whether the framework is useful below the computational level is explicitly unresolved.
- **Probe priors under high uncertainty.** Ambiguous or impoverished stimuli reveal the brain's assumptions; strong stimuli mask them.
- **Validate the intended individual-level use.** A group difference or symptom correlation does not establish reliable diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment selection. Sampling across severity and treatment histories may improve coverage when relevant to the target population; no single severity stratum is mandatory for every study.
- **Look for consilience outside the fitted data**: does the account explain pharmacology, developmental timing, or comorbidity? Tourette's model explaining why all effective drugs reduce dopaminergic transmission is the standard.
- **Report what the model does not explain.** The schizophrenia chapter states plainly that current models "have not yet given a full account of positive symptoms."
- **Treat RDoC as a dimensional research framework.** It does not itself provide clinical break points or prove that no useful categorical distinction can exist. A proposed threshold requires evidence appropriate to its intended use.
- **When a Bayesian account and an RL account compete, ask whether states are given or inferred** — BDT reduces to RL when the state is known, so the difference is precisely whether state uncertainty matters in your task.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Computational parameters are candidate research measures across heterogeneous symptom categories.** RDoC is a research framework, not a diagnostic replacement. Parameter recoverability, reliability, predictive validity, and clinical usefulness remain separate questions.
2. **Four model families, four jobs**: circuits (connectivity–dynamics–function), DDM (decomposing RT/accuracy), RL (reward learning, with the dopamine link), Bayesian (beliefs, optimality, and "broken inference").
3. **In the Gaussian belief-update example, precision weighting determines the influence of prediction error.** Predictive coding can implement related updates under particular assumptions; Bayesian inference does not imply that algorithm or identify a neural implementation.
4. **Parameter recovery and model recovery on simulated data come before real data**, and before any parameter is interpreted.
5. **Model comparison is relative.** Winning a comparison does not make a model adequate.
6. **Hierarchical group modelling is a useful default to test**, not a requirement independent of design, heterogeneity, priors, and recovery.
7. **One mechanism can explain several symptom classes** — NMDA hypofunction → unstable attractors → both working-memory deficits and probabilistic-inference biases in schizophrenia.
8. **Translation from research parameters to individual clinical decisions requires separate evidence.** This 2020 source cannot establish the current validation status of any particular measure; check the intended population, endpoint, and external validation.
