## Mental Model (read first)
Pressure changes how an existing skill is executed. In deliberative tasks, worry can compete with maintaining intermediate results and selecting a strategy. In practiced routines, pressure can induce **explicit monitoring** of components that ordinarily run together, disrupting fluent coordination: **paralysis by analysis**. First establish competence on a comparable task without evaluative pressure; failure to acquire a skill is different from failure to express it. These are competing or coexisting explanations, not diagnoses obtainable from one bad performance.

**Use for:** exams, presentations, skilled movement, and errors that appear when consequences or audiences change. Combine [Klingberg](reference-klingberg-overflowing-brain.md) for competing demands, [How the Body Knows Its Mind](reference-beilock-body-knows-mind.md) for action and support, and [How We Learn](reference-dehaene-how-we-learn.md) for acquisition and retention.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapters 1, 5, 7 — Diagnose the task before prescribing attention
**Locators:** “The Curse of Expertise,” “High-Powered Failure”; “Bombing the Test”; “Paralysis by Analysis,” “When Paying Attention Is a Good Thing.”

| Candidate explanation | Operation that fails | Observations that would favor it | First comparison or intervention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Insufficient learning or unreliable retrieval | A needed rule, representation, or movement is not yet available | Errors also occur on matched, low-pressure, unsupported trials | Teach the missing step, practice with feedback, and test delayed retrieval |
| Worry competing for working memory | Intrusive thoughts compete with maintaining goals/intermediate results or strategic control | Competence is present; failure increases with evaluation and memory demand, beyond timing alone | Compare pressure and memory load separately; permit intermediate notes where appropriate |
| Excessive monitoring of a practiced routine | Stepwise control interferes with timing and coordination | The specific action is fluent in ordinary conditions but worsens with component-by-component instructions | Compare a practiced holistic/outcome cue with a mechanics cue at matched difficulty |
| Changed task or physical condition | Different information, time, equipment, fatigue, or movement constraints | The rehearsal/performance comparison is not actually matched | Reproduce the altered condition before attributing the change to pressure |

The diagnostic table is **editorial synthesis** from the book's distinctions. “Expert” refers to the particular operation and context, not years in a profession. An experienced lawyer can have fluent speech but face a novel argument requiring deliberate reasoning. High arousal is not evidence that worry caused an error; slower responses can preserve accuracy. Inspect speed, accuracy, omissions, and strategy together.

**Explicit versus procedural memory:** explicit knowledge can be deliberately described or used to reason through a problem; procedural learning supports skilled execution without conscious access to every component. These are useful tendencies, not a complete partition into “thinking tasks” and “sports tasks.” Keep strategic planning separate from the movement that implements it.

### Chapter 1 — High-powered failure and strategy dependence
**Locator:** modular arithmetic experiment; “Assessing Working-Memory,” “Pausing the Choke,” “Get Used to It!”

**Working memory** maintains task-relevant information while other processing proceeds. In the book's mod-math studies, higher-span students benefited from multistep computation under ordinary conditions, but lost that advantage under pressure. Strategy analysis matters: an inaccurate shortcut can survive pressure precisely because it demands little working memory. Lack of a pressure-related decline need not mean good absolute performance.

The implication is conditional: vulnerability depends on the strategy used and its resource requirements. Do not turn “high-powered failure” into a law that more intelligent people always choke more, or treat a span score as a personal stress forecast. The book's horsepower metaphor does not identify one brain region, a single fixed capacity, or a cause of intelligence differences.

**Pause before planning:** when a problem requires explicit reasoning, identify its underlying structure before acting. The book contrasts experts' initial physics classification with novices' surface-feature matching. Extra time helps only if relevant knowledge and a productive strategy are available; a pause during an automatic movement is a different intervention.

### Chapters 2–3 — Acquisition changes the useful form of control
**Locators:** “Training Success,” “Practice, Practice, Practice”; “Less Can Be More.”

**Practice and automaticity:** reliable experience can reduce dependence on conscious control of individual components. Practice should include the kinds of information and contingencies the real task presents, while retaining feedback and opportunities to correct errors. Repetition alone does not establish flexible expertise. Pressure rehearsal is a proposal to test once the basic operation is sufficiently learned; escalating pressure indiscriminately may obscure learning needs.

**Less can be more:** strong top-down control can sometimes keep a familiar but unhelpful representation active. This is a reason to examine fixation and task demands, not a prescription to impair executive function. Deliberate reasoning still matters for unfamiliar problems, error correction, and selecting a safe course of action.

**Curse of expertise:** fluent performance may conceal intermediate steps from the performer. Reconstruct instruction from observed novice errors and task analysis rather than assuming that an expert's introspection fully describes the skill. The book's working-memory/video-game enhancement discussion is historical; apply the later transfer checks in Klingberg's reference before recommending generic training.

### Chapters 4–5 — Anxiety and social evaluation are contextual
**Locators:** “Brain Differences Between the Sexes: A Self-Fulfilling Prophecy?”; “Math Anxiety,” “The Stress Spillover,” “What High-Stakes Tests Tell Us.”

**Math anxiety** can involve avoidance, anticipatory worry, and interference during computation. Poor learning and anxiety can reinforce one another; an association between them does not establish which came first. Separate lack of knowledge, retrieval difficulty, time pressure, and worry during execution.

**Stereotype threat** names the possibility that an evaluative situation makes a negative group stereotype relevant to performance. Treat this as a context-dependent hypothesis; group membership alone neither establishes threat nor explains a person's score. Book-era group contrasts and intervention examples are not universal effects or biological rankings. A fair comparison specifies what was changed in the instructions, cues, or evaluation conditions and what behavior was measured.

**Stress spillover:** evaluation-related processing can extend beyond the immediate event. Check fatigue, prior demands, and recovery when comparing rehearsal to performance; do not interpret every later lapse as a direct neural consequence of an earlier worry.

### Chapter 6 — The choking cure has intervention-specific conditions
**Locators:** “Writing Can Do Wonders,” “The Calm, Cool, and Collected Brain,” “Thinking Differently,” “Seeing Is Believing.”

**Expressive writing:** the book describes writing privately about thoughts and feelings concerning an upcoming test for about ten minutes before performance. The proposed mechanism is reduced intrusive worry, leaving resources for the task. This differs from writing intermediate answers during the task, and from values affirmation addressing a threat to self-worth. Improvement after writing does not by itself show that working memory mediated the change.

**Later evidence check:** Myers, Davis & Chan (2021) alternated intervention and control tasks across four authentic college exams; expressive writing did not reduce anxiety or improve performance in that setting. Thus a low-cost trial may be reasonable when worry is salient, but guaranteed score gains and the book's categorical cure language are unwarranted. [Primary study](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-021-00309-x).

**Reappraisal, meditation, and imagery:** the book proposes changing interpretations of pressure, practicing attentional regulation, and rehearsing success. Distinguish a proposed mechanism, a subjective improvement, and demonstrated task benefit. Do not infer durable attentional capacity from one calmer session, or prescribe the book's clinical analogies as treatment. Establish the task-specific benefit before using a new routine in a consequential performance.

### Chapters 7–8 — Protect coordination without abandoning strategic attention
**Locators:** “Degrees of Freedom,” “When Paying Attention Is a Good Thing”; “Just Do It!,” “Distract Yourself,” “Focus on the Goal.”

**Degrees of freedom:** beginning performers may rigidly couple joints; skilled coordination permits more differentiated movement. Under stress, stiffening and coupling may recur. This explains a candidate path from attention to disrupted execution, but observed stiffness could also reflect biomechanics or fear. It does not prove the proposed attentional cause.

**External focus / strategy focus:** direct attention to a relevant movement effect or task goal, rather than each body component. The book contrasts technique goals with strategic goals in soccer and describes holistic cues such as “smooth” in putting. A desired action effect differs from preoccupation with winning or being judged. Cue choice needs practice and feedback; a cue that omits essential information can hurt.

**Distraction as a probe:** a modest secondary task sometimes improved practiced golf performance by reducing component monitoring. This is not a general recommendation to multitask. Added load can damage deliberation, target detection, or unfamiliar movements. Prefer a task-relevant cue to unrelated distraction in a mixed task; do not transfer a putting experiment directly to driving or surgery.

**Attention remains useful:** decide where to aim, recognize a changed situation, and correct a newly learned technique. Then allow well-practiced components to run. The learning stage, novelty, and level of control determine whether attention helps. Reduced frontal activity or changed EEG coherence is not a literal switch for “turning off the prefrontal cortex.”

### Chapter 7 exception and Chapter 8 teaching boundary
**Locators:** “The Yips”; “Those Who Can Do Shouldn't Teach.”

The book distinguishes performance-related yips from possible focal dystonia. Preserve that alternative: recurrent involuntary jerks, tremor, or freezing cannot be classified as overthinking from a performance story. Its Type I/II distinction is historical and does not constitute a clinical assessment or exhaust mixed presentations. Persistent movement symptoms warrant appropriate assessment rather than repeated attention experiments alone.

Verbalizing mechanics can disrupt some expert performances; it does not follow that experts cannot teach. Demonstration, decomposed practice, and feedback can support learning, with detailed analysis separated from the next fluent execution. The provocative chapter heading is not a universal rule about coaching competence.

### Chapter 9 — Mixed tasks in the business world
**Locator:** “Think About What to Say, Not What to Avoid Saying,” “Speaking in Front of Others,” “Anticipation.”

A presentation combines practiced delivery with controlled retrieval, listening, and novel question answering. Use an outline or visible agenda to preserve goals, rehearse transitions, and allow time to structure unfamiliar answers. A practiced cue may support delivery while explicit reasoning supports the answer. Avoidance instructions such as “don't say the wrong thing” can add monitoring demands without supplying a useful action.

These workplace applications are editorial adaptations of the book, not trials validating a complete presentation protocol. Mimicry, first-impression, and celebrity narratives are not used as reliable persuasion techniques.

## Worked Example
**Reconstruction of the book's modular-arithmetic example, Chapter 1:** determine whether 32 ≡ 14 (mod 6). Subtract to obtain 18, then check divisibility by 6: the congruence is true. The shortcut “all numbers are even, therefore true” agrees here, but fails for 52 ≡ 16 (mod 8): 36 is not divisible by 8. A person may know the algorithm yet abandon it under pressure.

**Editorial diagnostic extension:** first test the rule on unfamiliar low-pressure items, then compare equivalent items with evaluation cues while matching time limits. A separate notes-allowed condition tests whether external intermediate values help. If errors persist without pressure and with visible steps, strengthen knowledge before treating the problem as choking. If notes help, they establish benefit from support, not increased internal capacity or uniquely prove worry; task simplification and strategy changes remain alternatives. Do not use an extra distractor merely because it helped a practiced golfer.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- Before calling a failure choking, establish the relevant competence and match difficulty, timing, equipment, and fatigue across conditions.
- When worry competes with deliberate computation, reduce competing demands and support the needed steps; when a skill is missing, teach and practice it.
- When component monitoring disrupts a practiced movement, test a familiar action-effect or holistic cue. Preserve deliberate strategic decisions and corrective practice.
- In mixed tasks, assign control by operation and moment; one “focus more/less” instruction is insufficient.
- Evaluate an intervention with accuracy and speed, ordinary and pressured conditions, and retention where learning is claimed. A benefit does not uniquely identify its mechanism.
- Treat writing, imagery, and pressure rehearsal as conditional options; record what evidence would make you change course.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pressure, task demands, strategy, and expertise interact; a poor result alone does not identify the cause.
2. Worry and excessive monitoring can require different attentional changes.
3. Protecting performance and acquiring a skill are distinct goals.
4. Neural labels and dramatic examples do not establish an individual's mechanism.
