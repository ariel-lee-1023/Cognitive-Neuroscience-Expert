# The Oxford Handbook of Event-Related Potential Components — Emily S. Kappenman & Steven J. Luck (eds.)
**Format**: md (from pdf, OUP) | **Pages**: ~1677 | **Sections**: 23 chapters, 4 parts | **Depth**: study

## Mental Model (read first)
This is the library's **electrophysiology and temporal-dynamics** source — the answer to a corpus that was otherwise fMRI-heavy and therefore blind to millisecond structure. ERPs "provide a narration of neural processes as they unfold millisecond by millisecond, **adding whole pages to the story of the mind that behavioral and imaging techniques leave blank**."

Its governing discipline is a single distinction that most published ERP work quietly violates: **peaks are not components**. A peak is "a reliable local positive or negative maximum in the observed ERP waveform." A **component** is "a scalp-recorded voltage change that reflects a specific neural or psychological process." The observed waveform is the *weighted sum* of many simultaneously active components, so **a change in one component routinely produces apparent changes in peaks belonging to others**. Everything methodological in the handbook follows from that superposition problem.

**Reach for this book when you need**: what an ERP component is and where the voltage physically comes from; whether an amplitude or latency effect is real; the functional interpretation of a named component (N170, MMN, P300, ERN, N400, N2pc, CDA, LRP, CNV); how ERPs relate to oscillations and ICA; or component abnormalities in a clinical or developmental population.

**Coverage note**: front-loads Ch 1 (Kappenman & Luck's conceptual framework) — the chapter that governs the rest — plus the component inventory and its interpretive constraints. Compressed: the clinical and special-population chapters (development, aging, schizophrenia, depression, neurodegenerative disease, nonhuman primates) to their diagnostic claims. Dropped with reason: recording hardware, filtering, and artifact-rejection procedure — covered in Luck's companion textbook rather than this handbook.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Ch 1: ERP Components — the Ups and Downs (Kappenman & Luck) — the governing chapter
#### Where the signal physically comes from
- ERPs are **voltage fluctuations in the ongoing EEG, time-locked to an event**. Most are small relative to background EEG and become visible only through **averaging** across epochs — historically the first case where signal averaging "revealed the existence of novel, previously unknown, neural processes."
- **The physical substrate, stated precisely**: scalp voltage reflects **summed postsynaptic potentials (PSPs)** in large numbers of **cortical pyramidal cells oriented similarly with respect to the scalp**. A PSP at one end of a pyramidal neuron creates an electrical **dipole**; spatially aligned dipoles sum into an **equivalent current dipole** large enough to record, requiring **thousands of simultaneously active neurons**.
- **Three consequences that constrain every ERP claim**:
  1. **"Except in a few unusual cases, scalp ERPs do not reflect action potentials."** ERPs "represent the **inputs** to a group of neurons rather than the **outputs** of those neurons."
  2. Because such large aligned populations must fire together, **"much of the neural activity in the brain that gives rise to cognition and behavior is not visible to an electrode placed on the scalp."** ERP silence is not evidence of neural silence.
  3. **Polarity is not interpretable functionally.** Positive vs. negative at an electrode depends on dipole orientation relative to that electrode (and on reference choice); "it is not usually possible to link the polarity to the type of neural processing (such as inhibition versus excitation)."
- **The one unambiguous advantage**: electrical potential propagates at essentially the speed of light, so transmission through brain, meninges, skull and scalp is instantaneous. **"ERPs provide a direct and instantaneous millisecond-resolution measure of activity related to neurotransmission."**

#### The superposition problem — the central difficulty
- PSPs last **tens to hundreds of milliseconds**, so "as new mental processes are unfolding, the previous neural activations persist." Source-localization work suggests **as many as 10 equivalent current dipoles may be active at once** (Di Russo et al. 2002; Picton et al. 1999) — i.e. ~10 simultaneous components.
- **The mixing model**: each generator contributes to each electrode via a **weighting factor** set by generator position/orientation and tissue conductance. The full set of weights is the **mixing matrix**; techniques that recover components compute an **unmixing matrix**.
- **Therefore the waveform at any electrode "contains information about all of the neural generators in the brain, not just the generator sources located close to the electrode."** Worse, the **skull's high resistance** relative to brain and scalp causes voltage to **spread laterally**, blurring the relationship between an electrode and the cortex beneath it.
- **The inverse problem, stated without hedging**: "**an infinite number of internal generator configurations could produce any observed distribution of ERP activity over the scalp.** Thus, there is no technique that can determine, with certainty, the locations of the sources and the waveform at each source without bringing in difficult-to-verify assumptions or other sources of evidence." *Treat any ERP source-localization claim as assumption-dependent by construction.*

#### Peaks ≠ components — the four failure modes, from the simulation
The chapter's simulated three-component example (Fig. 1.2) is the most useful single figure in the handbook. Its lessons:
1. **Peak timing ≠ component timing.** In the simulation, observed peaks appear to span 0–90, 90–180 and 180–450 ms, while the underlying components actually span 0–200, 50–325 and 100–450 ms. **You cannot read a component's duration off a peak's duration** — especially when a small component neighbours a large one (assessing N2 duration next to a large P3 is the given example).
2. **Reducing one component can *increase* a later peak.** Decreasing component C2's amplitude increased the amplitude of Peak 3, **even though C3 did not change** — inviting the erroneous conclusion that two components were modulated when only one was.
3. **Changing one component's amplitude can masquerade as another's latency shift.** Increasing C1's amplitude changed both the amplitude *and* the apparent latency of Peak 2, "even though no latency shift occurred for any of the underlying components." Symmetrically, **measured peak-amplitude changes can result from latency shifts.**
4. **The simulation understates the problem** — it used 3 generators where modelling suggests 6–10 are active within a 150 ms window.
- **A real, historically important instance**: in the language literature "it is not always clear whether a putative increase in N400 amplitude might actually be a decrease in P3 amplitude, and **a great deal of work was needed to determine that the P600 component elicited by syntactic anomalies was different from the P3 wave.**"
- **The partial remedy**: "considerable information about the underlying component structure can often be obtained by examining the waveforms from **multiple electrode sites**, because different components will be weighted differently at each electrode."

#### Latency jitter — the averaging artifact everyone should check
- **Averaging assumes the signal's timing is the same on each trial**, and it usually is not — just as reaction time varies trial to trial, so does component timing. This variability is **latency jitter**.
- **What jitter does to the average**: the averaged waveform is a **"smeared-out" version**, reflecting **the earliest onset and the latest offset across trials — not the average onset and offset.** It also **reduces measured peak amplitude**.
- **The diagnostic trap, stated explicitly**: comparing two conditions that differ *only* in jitter "might lead to the erroneous conclusion of a smaller component in condition A than in condition B." **A "smaller component" is frequently a more variable one.**
- **The same logic applies to grand averages across subjects**: a grand average likewise reflects the earliest onsets and latest offsets across subjects, not the average. And individual subject waveforms vary substantially in shape — a measurement window chosen from the grand average (e.g. for P2) may capture quite different activity in individual subjects, some of whom peak outside it.
- **Measurement practice to keep in mind**: amplitudes and latencies are measured **per subject from that subject's average**, with each subject contributing one value to the statistical test — but most papers *display* the grand average.

#### Four alternative definitions of "component," and what each assumes
- **Source localization** — a component *is* a neural generator. Uses biophysical assumptions about current flow to define scalp distributions, plus further assumptions (a fixed number of discrete dipoles, or maximal smoothness of current over cortex) to force a unique solution. Close to the functional definition "on the assumption that most brain processes occur in discrete areas," but more than one process may occur in a region.
- **PCA** — no biophysical assumptions; uses covariance across electrodes. A **"lumping" technique**: finds a small number of components explaining most variance. Consequence flagged: "spatial PCA by itself is **unlikely to produce components that are related to individual neural and psychological processes**."
- **ICA** — a **"splitting" technique**: maximizes independence, so each component carries maximal information. **Why this is conceptually apt**: "for something to count as a unique process, it must be **dissociable** from other processes… and this is exactly the type of independence that ICA uses." Empirically, ICA components often show single-dipole-like scalp distributions despite no biophysical assumptions. **Two practical problems**: ICA runs per subject, so cross-subject (and cross-experiment) correspondence is hard to establish; and **the number of ICA components always equals the number of electrodes**, so true components may be lumped together or split apart.
- **Time-frequency analysis** — decompose EEG into oscillations and estimate band power over time. Relates to ERPs two ways: (a) oscillations with **random phase across trials disappear in the average** and are invisible in conventional ERPs; (b) a stimulus may **perturb the phase** of an ongoing oscillation, producing across-trial phase consistency, in which case "a component in an average ERP may actually consist of a portion of an **ongoing oscillation** rather than reflecting a discrete voltage deflection that is elicited by the stimulus."
  - **The warning worth quoting**: "many different processes might lead to oscillations in a given frequency band, so it is problematic to assume that power in a given frequency band in one experiment reflects the same process reflected by power in that same frequency band in another experiment… **Assuming that a given band reflects a specific process would be analogous to assuming that any positive deflection in the P3 latency range reflects a single process.**" *This is a reverse-inference caution for oscillations, exactly parallel to the fMRI case in [[reference-jahn-brain-book]] — note that "reverse inference" appears in this chapter's own keywords.*

### Ch 2–3: Oscillations and ICA
- **Ch 2 (oscillatory dynamics)**: the motivating recognition, dating to the 1980s–90s, that "an ERP only represents a **certain part** of the event-related EEG signal." Because time-locked averaging discards non-phase-locked activity, oscillatory analysis "extracts information from the EEG/MEG signal that is **largely lost** with the traditional time-locked averaging" — hence "at least a useful addition to the traditional ERP approach."
- **Ch 3 (Makeig & Onton, ICA perspective)**: reframes the ERP as averaging potentials from spatially coherent cortical source areas **plus non-brain sources normally treated as artifacts**. Introduces **ERP-image plotting** to visualize across-trial variability rather than averaging it away, and ICA to "undo the effects of source signal mixing at scalp electrodes."

### The component inventory — Part II
- **Sensory evoked potentials** (Pratt) — auditory, somatosensory, visual. **Short-latency** components are clinical workhorses and, within cognitive ERP studies, serve "mostly as **controls for the integrity of the sensory input**." Auditory **middle- and long-latency** components reflect cortical activity that "often includes elements of cognitive processing."
- **N170** (Rossion & Jacques) — occipitotemporal, **100–200 ms**, face-sensitive; historically related to the earlier-reported **vertex positive potential (VPP)**. The chapter's conclusion: N170 reflects **both** "the initial basic-level categorization of the stimulus as a face" **and** "the coding of individual face representations." It explicitly engages the **controversial expertise question** — whether N170 processes are partly recruited for non-face objects after extensive visual experience. Also noted in Ch 1 as one of the components where the **polarity reversal on the opposite side of the dipole is easily observed**.
- **MMN** (Näätänen & Kreegipuu) — a **change-specific** auditory component "elicited **even in the absence of attention**," usable as "an **objective index of sound-discrimination accuracy and auditory sensory memory**."
  - **The claim that makes MMN theoretically important**: the central auditory system performs "complex cognitive operations, such as **generalization leading to simple concept formation**… **rule extraction**, and the **anticipation of the next stimulus** — at the **preattentive** level."
  - **The decisive evidence — abstract-feature MMN.** In Saarinen et al. (1992) there was **no physically identical repeated standard**: tone pairs varied randomly in absolute frequency, with all standards being *ascending* pairs and deviants *descending*. MMN was elicited by direction-deviants **in an ignore condition**, showing the preattentive representation encoded the abstract invariant "rising pair" rather than any physical feature. *This is the cleanest demonstration in the handbook that a preattentive system extracts relational structure, not just stimulus features.*
- **P300** (Polich) — subdivided into **P3a** and **P3b**. Polich's integrative proposal: the two "result from **neuroinhibition** that is engaged when incoming stimuli garner attentional processes to **facilitate memory encoding**," with neuropharmacological contributions sketched by neurotransmitter system.
- **Anticipatory slow waves** — three distinguished by eliciting context: the **Bereitschaftspotential (BP)** (movement preparation), the **contingent negative variation (CNV)**, and the **stimulus-preceding negativity (SPN)**. Mechanistically, all are "the result of a summation of a large number of postsynaptic potentials in the cell columns of the cortical brain areas that will — in one way or another — be **involved in the processing of the future event**."
- **LRP (Lateralized Readiness Potential)** — negativity over motor cortex **contralateral to the responding hand**, beginning **before the response is emitted**; its onset indexes "the time at which the brain began preparing to make the response." **Its distinctive use: measuring "the preliminary activation of a response that is never actually produced"** — i.e. covert, partial response activation invisible to behavior.
- **ERN/Ne** (error-related negativity) — accompanies errors in speeded performance. Theories "must contend with a wealth of experimental data, both in healthy subjects and in individuals with neurological and psychiatric conditions," and are further constrained by the **error positivity (Pe)**, **feedback-related negativity (FRN)**, **correct response negativity (CRN)**, and **theta oscillations**.
- **N2pc and related contralateral components** (Luck) — the ERP signature of **focusing attention onto a peripheral location**. The chapter frames a processing sequence for complex arrays: **store a target template in working memory → detect relevant features → focus attention on a peripheral location → shift overt attention → expand or contract attention around the object**, with ERP components corresponding to each step. Motivating distinction: simple-stimulus attention acts as a **gain control** on feedforward processing (Ch 11), whereas complex arrays require **iterative, reentrant processes and perception–action loops**.
- **CDA (contralateral delay activity)** — the working-memory component, used to index the number of items held in visual working memory.
- **Episodic memory components** (Wilding & Ranganath) — focused on **retrieval**, including the parietal **old/new effect** as an index of recollective processing.
- **Language components** (Ch 15) — the N400 family plus P600; the chapter covers "the main models of what these ERPs reflect in terms of linguistic and neural processes" and flags **methodological issues specific to language research**. Language is singled out because it "is a cognitive function that is **not readily investigated using neuroscience approaches in animal models**" — which is precisely why a non-invasive millisecond method matters here.
- **Emotion components** (Ch 16) — including the **late positive potential (LPP)**. **The warning from Ch 1 applies directly**: the LPP "appears as a single component that is hundreds of milliseconds in duration; however, the LPP **may actually be composed of several distinct shorter-duration components**." The chapter opens by conceding a "definitional imbroglio… as many conceptualizations of emotion as there are emotion researchers."

### Part IV: Special populations — what ERPs are good for
- **Development** (Coch & Gullick): "there are very few neuroimaging tools amenable to measuring brain development in typically developing infants and children. Of the tools available, ERP recording is **perhaps uniquely well suited** to the challenges." Also flags the specific problem of **identifying components in developing populations**, where morphology and latency differ from adults.
- **Aging** (Friedman) — a good worked case of component-by-component dissociation. Early sensory components (**P50, N1, P2**): older adults show **not sensory deficits per se** but possible **inhibitory** deficiencies at early stages. Longer-latency components (**N2b, P3a/novelty P3, P3b**) are modulated more by task than by sensory properties; **N2b and P3b latencies are prolonged**, consistent with general behavioral slowing. **The most interesting finding: P3a "does not habituate in older adults, suggesting that older adults continue to recruit prefrontal cortical mechanisms for events that should no longer capture their attention."** Difference-defined measures (MMN, parietal old/new, N400, ERN, MFN) are treated separately.
- **Schizophrenia** (Ch 19) — the rationale is mechanistic: schizophrenia involves altered synaptic connectivity and neurotransmission, and "**since ERP components are typically generated by the summation of postsynaptic potentials**… these measures are well suited to assess such pathophysiological alterations." Five components mapped to five systems: **MMN** → sensory memory; **P50** → sensory gating; **N100** → sensory processing/inhibition; **P300** → selective attention and working memory; **N400** → semantic processing. Each is evaluated on **(1) systems indexed, (2) abnormality, (3) sensitivity and specificity, (4) clinical correlates, (5) relationship to genetic variation** — *a template worth reusing for any biomarker claim*. Verdict: "**well validated biomarkers** for schizophrenia" with promise for genomic/epigenomic and pharmacological work.
- **Depression** (Ch 20) — methodologically notable for combining **current source density (CSD)** with **PCA**, which "take better advantage of both the temporal resolution of ERPs and the spatial resolution of dense electrode arrays than traditional analysis methods of **reference-dependent** surface potentials."
- **Neurodegenerative disease** (Verleger) — names the two directions of research explicitly: "**using ERPs to learn something about the disease** and **using the disease to learn something about ERPs**," and focuses on the former (diagnosis, cognitive consequences, prognosis).
- **Nonhuman primate homologues** (Woodman) — establishing cross-species ERP homologues, with the honest note that methodological differences across species "are a necessary complication."

## Worked Example — deciding whether an amplitude effect is real
The Ch 1 procedure, made explicit. Run it on any reported ERP difference:

1. **Is the claim about a peak or a component?** If the measurement is "amplitude at the P3 peak," it is a peak claim, and peaks are sums.
2. **What else changed in the waveform?** If an adjacent deflection also moved, suspect a single underlying component change producing two apparent effects (simulation case 2). If a *latency* shift accompanies an amplitude change in a neighbouring peak, suspect that one component's amplitude change is masquerading as another's latency shift (case 3).
3. **Could this be latency jitter rather than amplitude?** A condition with more variable timing yields a **broader, lower-amplitude** average with **earlier apparent onset and later apparent offset**. If the manipulation plausibly increases trial-to-trial variability (harder task, patient group, older adults), jitter is a live alternative — and single-trial or ERP-image inspection is the check.
4. **Does the effect hold across electrode sites in a way consistent with one generator?** Different components weight differently across electrodes; multi-site consistency is the main practical leverage against superposition.
5. **Is a difference wave available?** Isolating a component by subtracting a matched condition removes components common to both — the standard defence, and the reason so many components (MMN, N2pc, LRP, old/new effect) are *defined* as difference waves rather than as raw peaks.
6. **If the claim is anatomical, what assumptions produced it?** Infinitely many generator configurations fit any scalp distribution; source localization requires added assumptions (fixed dipole count, smoothness) that must be stated.
7. **If the claim is about an oscillation, is the band being reverse-inferred?** Theta in one experiment need not index the same process as theta in another — the explicit analogue of assuming any positive deflection in the P3 window is one process.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **Never equate a peak with a component.** The observed waveform is a weighted sum of ~6–10 simultaneously active generators; a peak's amplitude, latency and duration are all properties of the sum.
- **A change in one component can create, enlarge, shrink, or shift a peak belonging to another.** Before attributing an effect to a named component, ask which single underlying change could produce the whole observed pattern.
- **Suspect latency jitter whenever a component looks "smaller."** Averages reflect earliest onset and latest offset, not mean timing, and jitter reduces peak amplitude without reducing single-trial amplitude.
- **Read grand averages as range summaries, not as typical subjects.** Choose measurement windows with individual-subject waveforms in view.
- **Use difference waves and multi-electrode patterns to isolate components** — these are the two practical defences against superposition.
- **ERPs index synaptic input, not spiking output**, and only from large, similarly-oriented pyramidal populations. **Absence of an ERP effect is weak evidence of absence of neural processing.**
- **Do not interpret polarity functionally.** Positive vs. negative reflects dipole orientation and reference choice, not excitation vs. inhibition.
- **Treat every source-localization result as assumption-laden.** Infinitely many configurations fit any scalp map; state the constraints (dipole count, smoothness) that selected the reported solution.
- **Choose PCA vs. ICA by what you want**: PCA lumps (data reduction, poor mapping to individual processes); ICA splits (independence ≈ dissociability, better conceptual match) — but ICA is per-subject, hard to align across subjects and experiments, and always returns exactly as many components as electrodes.
- **Do not reverse-infer from frequency band.** Theta in one paradigm need not be theta-as-process in another; the same caution that applies to a brain region applies to a band.
- **Distinguish evoked from induced activity.** Phase-random oscillations vanish in the average; conversely, a stimulus-induced phase reset can make an ongoing oscillation *appear* as an evoked component.
- **Use ERPs when the question is about timing, covert processing, or preattentive operation** — the LRP measures responses never emitted; MMN operates without attention; N2pc times attentional focusing. These are questions fMRI cannot answer.
- **For clinical biomarker claims, demand the five-part evaluation**: systems indexed, abnormality, sensitivity *and* specificity, clinical correlates, genetic relationship. Sensitivity alone is not a biomarker.
- **In developmental and aging work, do not assume adult component identity.** Morphology, latency and scalp distribution differ; component identification is itself a research problem.
- **Beware components that look long and unitary.** The LPP is the standing example of a "single" long component that may be several shorter ones.
- **When behavior and ERP disagree, that is often the finding** — e.g. P3a failing to habituate in older adults, indicating continued prefrontal recruitment for events that should no longer capture attention.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Peaks are not components.** This single distinction governs the interpretation of essentially every ERP result.
2. **Superposition is unavoidable**: ~6–10 generators active within a 150 ms window, each contributing to every electrode, with skull resistance blurring everything laterally.
3. **The inverse problem has no unique solution.** Source localization requires assumptions that must be stated and cannot be verified from the data alone.
4. **Latency jitter mimics amplitude reduction** and distorts onset/offset estimates in both trial averages and grand averages.
5. **ERPs measure postsynaptic input from large aligned pyramidal populations** — instantaneous, millisecond-resolved, but blind to most neural activity and uninterpretable by polarity.
6. **The components are individually well-characterized tools**: N170 (face categorization + individuation), MMN (preattentive change detection, including abstract rules), P3a/P3b, LRP (covert response preparation), ERN (error monitoring), N2pc (attentional focusing), CDA (VWM load), N400/P600 (language), LPP (emotion).
7. **MMN's abstract-feature results show preattentive extraction of relational invariants**, not merely physical-feature deviance.
8. **ERPs' comparative advantage is timing, covert processes, and populations that cannot be scanned** — infants, children, and patients — not spatial localization.
