## Mental Model (read first)
This is the library's **inferential-hygiene** source. Its center of gravity is not "how to click through FSL" but *what a neuroimaging result is allowed to mean*: the mass-univariate multiple-comparisons problem, circular/biased analysis, reverse inference, and the criteria for a genuine double dissociation. Its governing discipline is stated as a mantra and then made concrete: **"Look at your data"** — and, crucially, the book's complaint is that the mantra is useless unless you are told *what to look for and what to do about it*, which it then does step by step.

Second governing idea: **every preprocessing step is a modeling assumption**, so each one must be justified, and the justification cuts both ways (the book argues *against* slice-timing correction in several cases, and *for* leaving too much skull rather than too little).

**Reach for this book when you need**: whether a claim survives multiple comparisons; whether an ROI was independently defined; whether a reverse inference is warranted; what a real double dissociation requires; the rationale (not just the command) behind a preprocessing step; or the GLM/HRF machinery underlying "activation."

**Coverage note**: this distillation front-loads the fMRI Short Course appendices (the transferable inferential content), the preprocessing rationale, the GLM/HRF chapters, ROI analysis, and MVPA/RSA concepts. Compressed: open-science tooling (BIDS, MRIQC, fMRIPrep, GitHub), design optimization, diffusion (MRtrix), functional connectivity (CONN), meta-analysis (GingerALE). ## Frameworks & Structure

### Appendix A: Cluster Correction — the multiple-comparisons problem
- **Core idea**: Group-level maps come from a **mass univariate analysis** — "we carry out as many statistical tests as there are voxels." With hundreds of thousands of voxels, uncorrected testing guarantees false positives.
- **Terms**: **false positive rate / alpha level** — the probability of rejecting the null when the null is true, conventionally 0.05.
- **The dead salmon** (Bennett et al., 2009): a dead salmon in a scanner, shown pictures of humans socializing, analyzed like any other study *without* multiple-comparisons correction, yields significant voxels. **The message stated exactly: "a certain number of voxels will pass a significance threshold regardless of whether there is a true effect or not."** *This is a demonstration of the correction problem, not a critique of fMRI as such — cite it precisely.*
- **Bonferroni correction** — divide alpha by the number of tests. **Why it fails for imaging, in numbers**: 100,000 voxels at α=0.05 → each voxel must pass p = 0.0000005. "Either the effect must be phenomenally strong, or the study highly powered" — and imaging effects are usually neither.
- **The deeper reason Bonferroni is wrong here, not just harsh**: it **assumes each test is independent**. Voxels are not — bright voxels are surrounded by brighter voxels, dark by darker, and the same autocorrelation holds in the timecourse. Because you can estimate a voxel from its neighbors, the tests are dependent. **Bonferroni is therefore too severe: it protects the false-positive rate at the cost of a high false-negative rate.**
- **Cluster correction** — the dominant method (Woo et al., 2014). It *exploits* the non-independence: instead of testing voxels, test **clusters**.
  - **The mountain-range picture**: voxel height = z-value; the **cluster-defining threshold** is a cross-section through the range at a given height (e.g. z=3.1, p=0.001); only peaks above it remain.
  - **The critical distinction most people miss, stated explicitly: "a cluster-defining threshold is not the same as an alpha level."** Clusters that size might arise just as often from pure noise. The real question is *how many clusters of a given size would we expect by chance?*
  - **The answer comes from simulation**: generate artificial datasets with the same dimensions and **smoothness** as the real data but composed of pure noise; record the largest cluster; repeat thousands of times; build a **distribution of maximum cluster sizes**; then ask what percentage of the time a cluster as large as yours arises. Below 5% → reject the null.
- **Implementations, with what each estimates**:
  - **AFNI**: `3dFWHMx` on the **errts** file (residuals — i.e. what was not modeled, i.e. noise) with `-acf` returns three **autocorrelation function** parameters plus estimated smoothness in mm. *Note: estimated smoothness is higher than your smoothing kernel, because the kernel adds to smoothness already present in the data.* Then `3dClustSim -acf ... -athr 0.05 -pthr 0.001` gives the minimum contiguous-voxel count. **Round up, always** (8.1 voxels → require 9).
  - **Contiguity definitions that change the answer** — state which you used: **NN1** faces touch; **NN2** faces or edges; **NN3** faces, edges, or corners. And sidedness: **1-sided** (same sign only), **2-sided** (positive or negative), **bi-sided** (split clusters of differing sign).
  - **FSL `randomise`** — a **permutation** approach, randomly permuting the signs of specified contrasts. **"Usually about 5000 permutations or more should be selected in order to create a robust distribution."** Offers **TFCE** as a thresholding option.
  - **SPM** reports four numbers that are routinely conflated — keep them straight: **FWEp** (voxel-level z needed to pass Bonferroni-corrected p=0.05), **FDRp** (voxel-level z for FDR-corrected p=0.05), **FWEc** (cluster extent needed for FWE p=0.05 at your cluster-forming threshold), **FDRc** (same for FDR).
- Note flagged in the text: some researchers trust AFNI's cluster correction over SPM's (citing the PNAS cluster-failure literature), and the workflow for running `3dClustSim` on SPM residuals is given.

### Appendix B: Biased Analysis — circularity / double dipping
- **Core idea, defined precisely**: **circular analysis (non-independence)** occurs "when data are selected based on some criteria, and then tested for significance on that same criteria."
- **The non-imaging intuition that makes it obvious**: to test whether Four Loko improves exam scores, observe which students improved, then run the group analysis *only on those students*. **No longer a random sample.**
- **The imaging case, with its full arc** — this is the best-documented methodological correction in the book:
  1. **Grill-Spector et al. (2006)** extracted data from each condition's *significant* voxels in the fusiform face area and found selective activity for faces, animals, cars, and sculptures.
  2. **Baker et al. (2007)** showed that an ROI **outside the brain**, containing significant voxels by chance, reproduces **the same pattern** — which clearly should not happen.
  3. Rerunning with **independent ROIs** on non-brain tissue gave noise, as it should.
  4. A **cross-validated, unbiased** reanalysis of the original data showed FFA responsive to faces **but not selectively responsive to the other categories** — the original pattern disappeared.
- **Why bias inflates effect size — the mechanism, not just the label.** Suppose the true effect in a set of voxels is 0.3. Threshold at z=1.65 (uncorrected p<0.05). The surviving region overlaps the true-effect voxels **but also includes noise voxels — and by construction only noise voxels that happen to be above threshold.** That one-sided selection biases the effect upward. An independent ROI (e.g. via cross-validation) also contains true-effect and noise voxels, **but its noise voxels are not biased high or low** — the estimate may come out slightly above or below the truth, but it is not systematically inflated.
- **The 2009 "Voodoo Correlations" paper** ("Puzzlingly High Correlations in fMRI Studies") made this charge broadly. **The two counter-arguments, and the two rebuttals — worth memorizing because the counter-arguments sound reasonable**:
  - *Counter*: correct for multiple comparisons and the effect is really there. *Counter*: in an exploratory analysis, what's the harm in looking inside significant voxels to see what drives the effect?
  - **Rebuttal 1**: "the magnitude of the effect is just as important as detecting whether the effect is there, and biased analyses will systematically overestimate it. **Why? Because small studies by definition can only detect large effects.**"
  - **Rebuttal 2**: a published biased analysis will be read as inferential regardless of caveats. Practical consequence given: **"If you insist on presenting them in a figure, at least don't include error bars."**
- **Four ways to build an unbiased ROI**: (1) an **atlas**; (2) a **sphere at coordinates reported by another study** — ideally an identical or very similar design, at minimum one tapping the same cognitive process; (3) a **meta-analysis map** (Neurosynth); (4) a **localizer** scan — a separate run generating the contrast (not to be confused with an anatomical localizer).
- **The subtle form of bias that catches careful people**: you preselect an ACC ROI for a confirmatory analysis; it fails to pass correction; you look at the whole-brain map, see the effect is more pre-SMA, and switch ROIs. **"This is also a biased analysis, because now you know where your effect is before you decide where to extract from."** *Preselection is only protective if it is actually binding.*

### Appendix G: Reverse Inference — what a blob does and does not license
- **Definition**: presented with a result or end state, inferring the cause. Everyday analogues given: coughing → cold (or allergies); wet ground → rain (or sprinklers).
- **The graded worked example that makes the point**: a single right-motor-cortex cluster → most neuroimagers would infer a left-hand movement, and would probably be right; the alternatives (imagined movement, tactile stimulation blurred across motor and somatosensory cortex by imperfect normalization and a large smoothing kernel) are few. **But** dorsal anterior cingulate + bilateral anterior insula coactivation is consistent with **cognitive control, pain, salience, surprise**, and more. **The inference degrades with the number of processes that activate the region.**
- **Poldrack (2006, TiCS) — the base-rate criterion.** "You always need to be aware of the **base rate** of activation for a given region." Worked figure: **the anterior cingulate has a base activation rate of ~20%** — 20% of studies find activity there, across a wide range of designs. Used as a prior, that means "there is a high chance that the activity in that region may represent something other than what you think it does."
- **Selectivity, defined**: a region is selective if it activates for one condition and nothing else. **The more conditions or processes that activate a region, the less selective it is for any one of them.**
- **How to strengthen a reverse inference — the two legitimate routes**:
  1. **Converging non-imaging evidence.** Galvanic skin response is much higher for painful/stressful stimuli than for merely novel ones; consistently high GSR alongside elevated ACC BOLD makes the pain/stress claim stronger. *The general move: add a measure that discriminates among the candidate causes.*
  2. **Necessity evidence.** "Usually the only sure-fire way to determine whether a brain region is **necessary** for a given process is through **lesion studies**, or through a process that temporarily disrupts neural activity in a localized region, for example using **transcranial magnetic stimulation**."
- **The concrete procedure recommended before writing a discussion section**: type your region into **Neurosynth**, open the meta-analysis map, click the center of mass, click `Studies`. For "amygdala," within the first ten of **over 1,500** studies you find positive and negative valence, emotional faces, fear, face trustworthiness — plus disgust, salience, novelty. **"Clearly, if you are making a strong claim about what effect your study represents, you will need a strong enough study design that has ruled out all the alternatives."**
- **Also flagged**: Poldrack's argument for a **cognitive ontology** — a controlled vocabulary distinguishing cognitive processes — which "seems to not yet have gained a wide following." And the caveat that meta-analytic base rates are themselves biased, since "certain terms may be overrepresented due to trends in the field or used indiscriminately."
- **The stakes are named**: scanner billing of $500–$1000/hour; growing interest in fMRI "mind-reading," including **lie detection and courtroom admissibility**.
- **The reader's checklist, verbatim in force**: "Is this conclusion supported by the evidence? What other possible explanations are there? Was the study designed to rule out these alternatives? Is it true that this blob I'm seeing in this figure supports the author's hypothesis, and nothing else?" Plus the general warning: **"be on guard against explanations that seem too neat, too easy, and too obviously in line with the researcher's hypothesis."**
- **Author-side obligations**: if the design cannot distinguish two causes, "be upfront about that, and report as much of the data that is relevant" (see Taylor et al. 2023 on highlighting all effects while outlining the significant ones). And **if you will claim selectivity, design for a double dissociation.**
- **Afterword — the methodological pivot**: multivariate approaches (**MVPA**) may support stronger decoding inferences, per **Haxby et al. (2001)**, extended now to naturalistic stimuli.

### Appendix H: Double Dissociations — the criteria
- **Association vs. dissociation.** An **association** is "Given condition X, we see activation in brain region Y" — the ubiquitous "The neural correlates of…" paper. Associations are limited: they constrain the functional architecture weakly and **obscure differences in activity within a region across conditions**. Exploratory association studies with no hypothesis are the ones most vulnerable to faulty reverse inference — and an outside reader, not knowing how the experiment was built, is **more** likely to be persuaded by them.
- **Forward inference** (Henson 2006): design from a specific cause to a specific effect, ruling out alternatives.
- **The assumption forward inference rests on — name it**: **pure insertion**, that adding one condition does not affect the others. "In reality, it is probably more complicated than that": adding a working-memory task to a Flanker task might produce nonlinear increases in overall cognitive effort or spillover between tasks (cf. the **Gratton effect**).
- **The classical dissociations**: **H.M.** — long-term memories retained, new episodic memories impossible → hippocampus is necessary for *forming* new memories but not for *retaining* already-formed ones (a single dissociation). **Broca's vs. Wernicke's** — damage to left inferior frontal gyrus → **non-fluent aphasia**; damage to left posterior superior temporal gyrus → **fluent aphasia** (speech and writing preserved but disordered) → producing vs. understanding language (a double dissociation).
- **The five criteria for claiming a double dissociation** — the most operationally useful list in the book:
  1. Within **R1**, a significant positive effect of **Condition A**, but not of Condition B;
  2. Within **R2**, a significant positive effect of **Condition B**, but not of Condition A;
  3. Within **R1**, **A significantly greater than B**;
  4. Within **R2**, **B significantly greater than A**;
  5. A significant **Region × Condition interaction**.
- **The red herring, explicitly**: a significant Region × Condition interaction with **no significant activity for any condition in R2** is "a genuine but **trivial** interaction, which could be obtained by using a region that doesn't contain any grey matter, such as the **ventricles**." *An interaction alone is not a dissociation.*
- **Additional assumption**: the conditions should be **orthogonal**, achievable with a complete factorial design.
- **Worked examples**: Henson et al. (1999) — **Remember vs. Know**; greater posterior cingulate activity for Remembered but not Known words, greater lateral PFC for Known vs. Remembered, significant Region × Condition interaction, with New words as a control showing no significant activity in either region. Jahn et al. (2016) — independent ROIs built from Neurosynth peak z-scores for "Pain," "Conflict," and "Prediction Error"; the ventral ROI showed a Pain effect but not cognitive effects, the dorsal ROIs the reverse; ROI × Condition interaction F(4,100)=11.33, p<0.001 → double dissociation between dorsal cingulate (below the cingulate sulcus, pain) and pre-SMA (above it, cognition).
- **Alternative approaches**: **TMS as a "virtual, temporary, reversible lesion"**, penetrating ~1–2 cm into cortex — with the honest limit that depth reach is uncertain, so **fMRI remains better for subcortical structures and cortex buried deep in gyri**. And **de la Vega et al. (2016)** — k-means clustering of Neurosynth data yielding a **tripartite parcellation** of medial frontal cortex with **functional preference profiles** (log odds-ratio): posterior → motor and gaze; middle → conflict and pain; anterior → decision-making, reward, social.
- **Why functional preference profiles beat a p-value**: they display **statistical significance and effect magnitude across multiple conditions simultaneously**, letting the reader see that several conditions produce similar effects in a region with only one or two reaching significance.

### Appendix D: Unpacking a Contrast
- **Core idea**: a significant contrast does not tell you what produced it. For a significant Left−Right difference in right motor cortex, the possibilities are: (a) Left > 0 and greater than Right; (b) Left ≈ 0 and **Right negative**; (c) a combination; (d) **both negative, with Left simply less negative.**
- **"The only way to determine which of these scenarios is the true one is to extract beta weights for each condition separately."** *A contrast estimate is a difference; interpreting it as a level is the single most common error in reading activation figures.*
- Extending the same logic across regions gives the double dissociation above.

### fMRI Short Course: experimental design and the analysis chain

#### Experimental design
- **The Flanker task** as the worked example: a central arrow flanked by arrows pointing the same (**Congruent**) or opposite (**Incongruent**) direction; **cognitive control** operationally defined as "the ability to ignore irrelevant stimuli in order to do the task correctly." Subjects are slower and less accurate on Incongruent trials.
- **The design principle that governs everything downstream**: *"If you can design a behavioral task that produces a strong and reliable effect, you will increase your odds of finding an effect in your imaging data. fMRI data is notoriously noisy — **if you don't see a behavioral effect in your study, you most likely will not find an effect in your imaging data either.**"*
- **Jitter is not decoration in fMRI, it is required.** In a behavioral study, variable inter-trial intervals are optional flavor. In fMRI, because the BOLD response peaks ~4–6 s and returns to baseline ~12 s, **trials within ~10 s of each other produce overlapping, convolved responses** — jitter is what makes the individual conditions separable. In the example study: 2000 ms trial, then a jittered 8,000–14,000 ms interval (incrementing in 1-second steps).
- **fMRI has two dependent measures** — reaction time *and* the BOLD response — where the behavioral version has one.

#### Quality checking ("look at your data" made concrete)
- **On the anatomical**: watch for (1) **ripples like ripples in a pond** — a motion signature that, if large enough, makes brain extraction or normalization fail; (2) **abnormal intensity differences within grey or white matter** — possible aneurysms or cavernomas, "report to your radiologist right away."
- **On the functional**: same intensity checks plus **distortions (stretching, warping)** — commonly in **orbitofrontal cortex just above the eyeballs**. Flip through volumes like pages of a book, looking for sudden jerky movements.
- **Deidentification** explained: OpenNeuro data have headers stripped *and faces erased* — hence the missing face.
- **Motion thresholds, with the reasoning**: flag **relative motion greater than half a voxel** volume-to-volume, and **absolute motion greater than one voxel** across a run. For 3×3×3 mm voxels that is 1.5 mm relative, 3 mm absolute. Options beyond that: **scrubbing** or dropping the run. **"These are only guidelines, and you may decide to change these depending on the population you're studying."**

#### Preprocessing — each step as a justified assumption
- **Framing**: preprocessing is photo editing for volumes. An fMRI volume contains the signal of interest (oxygenated blood changes) plus **noise** — head motion, drifts, breathing, heartbeats. Some noise is **regressed out by modeling**; some is **removed by preprocessing**. *Deciding which is a design choice.*
- **Brain extraction (skull stripping)** — FSL's `bet`. The tunable is the **fractional intensity threshold** (default 0.5; smaller → larger brain estimate). **The asymmetric rule worth remembering: "it is better to err on the side of leaving too much skull, as opposed to removing too much cortex — bits of skull here and there won't cause future preprocessing steps to fail, but once cortex is removed, you cannot recover it."**
- **Motion correction** — via **rigid-body transformations**: 3 **translations** (x, y, z) + 3 **rotations** (**pitch** about x, **roll** about y, **yaw** about z) = 6 degrees of freedom. All volumes are **realigned** to a **reference volume** (typically first, middle, or last). **Three distinct harms from motion, and they are different problems**: (1) blur; (2) a **moving voxel** — you measure one region for part of the run and a different region or tissue type after the movement; (3) **motion generates signal**, so if the subject moves in response to the stimulus (jerking at each shock), stimulus-driven and motion-driven signal become inseparable. *(3) is a confound, not noise — no amount of realignment fixes it.*
- **Slice-timing correction** — volumes are acquired in **slices** (tens to hundreds of ms each), **sequential** or **interleaved**. Modeling assumes simultaneous acquisition, so each slice's time-series is shifted back by its acquisition delay. **Sladky et al. (2011)**: significant power gains for **longer TRs (≥2 s)**, especially dorsally. **Three objections, given fairly**: (1) don't interpolate data unless you need to; (2) for TRs around 1 s or less there is no meaningful power gain; (3) **many of the same problems are addressed by including a temporal derivative in the statistical model.** FSL's default is *no* slice-timing correction plus a temporal derivative. *Note this is a case where the book argues against a standard step.*
- **Smoothing** — replace each voxel with a weighted average of its neighbors. **The trade stated honestly**: it *does* reduce spatial resolution. **Three offsetting benefits**: noise cancellation (noise in fMRI is "frequently greater than the signal"); improved inter-subject overlap after normalization, since small anatomical regions align imperfectly even after warping — more overlap of signal clusters means greater power; and it makes cluster-based correction coherent. **Consequence to remember: some MVPA analyses deliberately skip smoothing**, because pattern information lives in the fine-grained differences smoothing destroys.
- **Registration vs. Normalization — keep these distinct.** **Normalization** = warping to a **template** in **standardized space** so that coordinates are comparable across labs ("if you find an effect at X=3, Y=20, Z=42, someone else who warped to the same template can check their results against yours"). **Registration** = aligning the functional to that subject's anatomical.
  - **Why the two-step route**: warping low-resolution functional images directly to the template "doesn't work very well" — they lack the anatomical detail to match. So normalize the **anatomical**, record the transformations, and apply them to the functionals — valid because anatomical and functional scans are acquired in the same session with little head movement between them.
  - **How registration works — mutual information**: exploit the fact that anatomical and functional images have **different contrast weightings** (CSF dark on anatomical, bright on functional). The algorithm matches bright-to-dark and dark-to-bright until the fit cannot improve.
  - **Affine transformation** = rigid body (translations, rotations) **plus zooms and shears** — the four **linear transformations**. Degrees of freedom: 3, 6, or 12. **BBR (Brain-Boundary Registration)** uses tissue boundaries for finer functional-to-anatomical alignment; slower, usually better.
  - **Template choice is a population claim**: **MNI152** is an average of 152 healthy adults. *"If you are studying another population — such as children or the elderly — consider using a template created from representatives of that population."*
  - **Resolution must match**: masks inherit the resolution of the template they were drawn on; use the same template for mask creation as for normalization, or resample (`flirt -applyxfm -usesqform`).
- **Checking**: overlay the template outline in red on the greyscale functional and confirm it traces the outline *and* that internal structures such as the ventricles align.
- **Order is not universal**: "Different software packages will do these steps in slightly different order — for example, FSL will normalize the statistical maps after model fitting."

#### The BOLD signal and the HRF
- **History with the inferential caveat built in**: PET required an injected radioactive glucose tracer — invasive, deterring subjects. **Ogawa (1990, Bell Labs)** found deoxygenated blood *decreases* the measured signal and oxygenated blood increases it, later shown to correlate with increased neural firing → the **BOLD signal**. **Kwong (1992, MGH)** alternated a flashing checkerboard and a black screen for one minute each, demonstrating BOLD as an **indirect** measure of neural activity — "a way to use blood inside the body as an **endogenous tracer**," eliminating injections and radiation. By the early 2000s fMRI was dominant.
- **The inference chain, spelled out so the number of steps is visible**: stimulus → transduced by sensory organs → nerve impulses → neuronal firing → firing neurons require oxygen → oxygen delivered by blood → oxygenated blood increases signal from nearby hydrogen in water → that is what the scanner measures. **"There was a catch: This new method was an indirect measure of brain activity, a few steps removed from the actual neural firing. Nevertheless, this is the measure used to infer whether a given region of the brain is 'active' or not."**
- **The HRF** — empirically, BOLD rises, **peaks around six seconds**, and returns to baseline over the following seconds. Modeled by a **Gamma Distribution**; parameterized to fit the majority of empirical studies it is the **canonical Hemodynamic Response Function**. Called a **basis function** because it is the fundamental element of the model.
- **Three stimulus cases**: **impulse stimulus** (no duration; a **stick function**) → a plain gamma shape. **Boxcar stimulus** (e.g. 15 s checkerboard) → the gamma is **convolved** with the boxcar, broadening with a sustained peak proportional to duration and descending only after offset. **Overlapping stimuli** → individual HRFs **sum**, producing a moving average that grows more complex as stimuli come closer together. **Convolution defined**: the averaging of two functions over time.

#### The GLM and first-level analysis
- **GLM**: one or more **regressors** (independent variables) fit to a **dependent variable**; **beta weights** are the relative weights that best fit the data; discrepancies are **residuals**. The core assumption is that data can be modeled as a **linear combination** of the regressors — hence the name.
- **The non-imaging worked example** (predicting GPA from height, IQ, drinks/week: IQ positive, drinks negative, height null) is used to make the beta weights concrete before applying them to voxels.
- **Ideal vs. fitted time-series**: the **ideal time-series** is what you expect given the timing of each stimulus (the HRF convolved with your onsets). Estimating beta weights to fit it to the data yields the **fitted time-series**.
- **Mass univariate, named and flagged at the point where it matters**: "Since every voxel has its own time-series, we do the procedure above for every voxel in the brain… **As there are tens or hundreds of thousands of voxels in a typical fMRI dataset, later we will need to correct for all of the tests we have done.**"
- **Timing/onset files** carry three columns: **onset time in seconds relative to scan start**, **duration in seconds**, and **parametric modulation** (set to 1 when unused).
- **The multi-level structure**: 1st level = model fit within a run; 2nd level = averaging contrast estimates **within** each subject; 3rd level = group analysis across subjects. **Which level to extract from for ROI analysis is a common error — use the 2nd level**, because the 3rd level is a single image with one number per voxel, whereas ROI analysis needs **one contrast estimate per subject**.

#### ROI analysis: exploratory vs. confirmatory
- **Whole-brain / exploratory** — appropriate "when the experimenter doesn't have a hypothesis about where the difference may be located; the result will be used as the basis for future research."
- **ROI / confirmatory** — "an analysis in which you choose to analyze a region selected **before** you look at whole-brain results."
- **The independent argument for doing ROI analysis even when the whole-brain result is significant**: "Whole-brain maps can hide important details… The only way to determine what is driving the effect is with ROI analysis, and this is especially important when dealing with **interactions and more sophisticated designs**." (See Appendix D.)
- **Anatomical vs. spherical ROIs**: **atlas-based masks** (e.g. Harvard-Oxford, **probabilistic** — each voxel carries a probability of belonging to a structure) vs. **spherical ROIs** centered on peak coordinates from another study, which are **independent** by construction.
- **A practical failure mode worth carrying**: the anatomical paracingulate-gyrus mask gave a non-significant result, and the diagnosis offered is that "the PCG mask covers a very large region; although the PCG is labeled as a single anatomical region, **we may be extracting data from several distinct functional regions.**" *Anatomical labels are not functional units; averaging across a large anatomical ROI can wash out a real effect.*
- **Binarized vs. probability-weighted extraction** are different analyses (`fslmeants -w` weights by probability) and will give different numbers.
- Both analysis types can appear in one study — whole-brain for extent, ROI for targeted confirmation.

### Machine learning / MVPA
- **The historical shift, and why it matters conceptually.** 1990s fMRI studied **activation** — the *amplitude* of BOLD per region. **Kanwisher (1997)** showed ventral temporal cortex responded more to faces, and selectively so relative to houses and body parts. **This selectivity program appeared consistent with Fodor's modularity thesis**, on which "the only limit of brain imaging was its spatial resolution" — finer voxels would reveal finer selectivity.
- **Haxby et al. (2001)** changed the target from amplitude to ***pattern***. Method: split data into **even and odd runs** and test whether the pattern in one half correlates with the pattern in the other — **cross-validation**. **Result: within-category correlations were far higher than between-category, even though the patterns of the different conditions largely overlapped in location.** Conclusion: "even if the overall activation between conditions wasn't different, the patterns themselves were significantly different, indicating that the patterns of **both activation and de-activation** were important."
- **MVPA (Multi-Voxel Pattern Analysis)** — term coined by Ken Norman. The claim it licenses: a process is "encoded as a pattern within this region" — **not an absolute amount of signal but the relative amount of signal between voxels.**
- **Note on attribution**: Haxby used a correlation method, and *none* of the terms MVPA, classifier, or machine learning appear in the original paper.
- **Machine learning vocabulary**: **training data**, **testing data**, **features**, **accuracy**. The height/hair-length example makes the point that classification is easy at the distribution tails and unreliable near the middle — **expect lower accuracy for ambiguous cases, and treat accuracy as distribution-dependent.**
- **Support Vector Machines** (Cox & Savoy, 2003 for fMRI): find the **hyperplane** best separating condition patterns. **The decisive case for using them**: when both conditions elicit activity from both voxels to slightly different degrees, **a univariate analysis detects nothing while the pattern is clearly different.** Assumption noted: activity in one voxel is independent of others; works best when patterns are **linearly separable**.
- **Searchlight vs. ROI** as the two analysis modes: searchlight scans all voxels within a mask (or the whole brain) to find which are most useful for decoding; ROI computes decoding accuracy across all voxels in a mask.
- **Reproducibility detail worth noting about the Haxby mask**: it was built by manually selecting voxels in lingual, fusiform, inferior occipital and middle occipital gyri, running an **omnibus test** for voxels responding to *any* condition at p=0.000001, and binarizing the survivors. *An omnibus-defined mask is independent of the specific pairwise contrasts tested within it — this is how the original avoided circularity.*
- **RSA (Representational Similarity Analysis)** — rather than classifying, compute the **correlation (e.g. Pearson) between condition patterns** and read the resulting **similarity/dissimilarity matrix**. The structure of the matrix is the result: with 8 conditions across 8 runs you get a 64×64 matrix, symmetric about the diagonal, with eight 8×8 within-run blocks along the diagonal and off-diagonal cells giving **cross-run** correlations of the same condition. *Cross-run cells are the ones that speak to reliability; within-run cells are inflated by shared run-level noise.*
- **Confusion matrix** as the compact representation of classification performance. **Non-parametric / permutation testing** is treated as the appropriate group-level inference for MVPA accuracies.

### Denoising, connectivity, and open science (compressed)
- **ICA (Independent Components Analysis)** — decompose a complex signal into simpler parts. The cocktail-party analogy: separate individual voices, background noise, and equipment noise from a recording. Applied to fMRI, separates physiological noise, movement artifacts, and scanner artifacts from BOLD.
  - **The hand-labeling heuristics — genuinely useful diagnostic criteria**: **Signal** is usually *lower frequency*, with a *smoother time-course*, and loadings that are *smooth, continuous, within grey matter, and often bilateral*. **Noise** is usually *higher frequency*, with a *jagged, discontinuous time-course*, and loadings in *white matter, CSF, eyeballs, or the Circle of Willis* — and **loadings that ring the edge of the brain indicate motion artifacts.**
  - **FIX** trains a classifier on hand-labeled components. Threshold guidance: **~5–20 is moderate; <5 is liberal (retains more, including noise); >20 is conservative.** Especially useful for high-motion populations such as children and some patient groups.
- **Open science stack**: **BIDS (Brain Imaging Data Structure)** — the standardized `anat`/`func` layout with `events.tsv` onset files; **MRIQC** — automated quality metrics; **fMRIPrep** — standardized preprocessing; **OpenNeuro** — public datasets; **GitHub** — script version control; **Neurosynth** — automated meta-analysis maps.
- **Diffusion / MRtrix**: **constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD)** deconvolves each voxel's diffusion signal into overlapping fiber bundles, **reducing the crossing-fibers confound that undermines tensor fitting** — the concrete answer to DTI's main limitation. Supports **tractograms**, **connectomes**, and **fixel-based analysis** of fiber density. Background: T1 (anatomical) images show white matter lighter than grey lighter than CSF; T2 (functional) inverts these intensities.
- **Functional connectivity / CONN toolbox**; **meta-analysis / GingerALE**.

## Worked Example — auditing an activation claim end to end
The reasoning chain this book equips you to run on any paper or your own analysis:

1. **Was there a behavioral effect?** If the task produced no reliable behavioral difference, an imaging difference is suspect at the outset — "if you don't see a behavioral effect in your study, you most likely will not find an effect in your imaging data either."
2. **Is the design capable of separating the conditions?** Was there jitter? With trials <10 s apart and no jitter, the conditions' HRFs are confounded by construction.
3. **Could motion be the effect?** If subjects move *in response to* the condition, motion-driven and stimulus-driven signal are inseparable — realignment does not fix a confound.
4. **Was multiple-comparisons correction applied, and which kind?** Was the reported number a **cluster-defining threshold** (not a correction) or a corrected alpha? Was the cluster extent derived from simulation or permutation, and how many permutations? Which contiguity rule (NN1/2/3) and sidedness?
5. **Was the ROI independent of the contrast?** Was it defined from an atlas, prior coordinates, a meta-analysis, or a localizer — or was it drawn from the very map being tested? **Including the ROI-switching case**: was a preselected region abandoned after seeing the whole-brain result?
6. **Are effect sizes reported from a biased ROI?** If so, the magnitude is systematically inflated, and error bars on that figure are misleading.
7. **What is the contrast actually showing?** Was the difference unpacked into per-condition betas, or is a difference being read as a level?
8. **What is the base rate of the region?** Run the term through Neurosynth. If the region activates for a dozen processes, the reverse inference is weak regardless of the p-value.
9. **Is selectivity claimed?** If so, do the data meet all five double-dissociation criteria, including a non-trivial R2 with real signal — or is it a single dissociation, or a trivial interaction against a dead region?
10. **What would make the claim stronger?** A converging non-imaging measure that discriminates among candidate causes; a lesion or TMS study for necessity; a multivariate analysis for decoding.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **A cluster-defining threshold is not an alpha level.** Always ask what the corrected cluster extent was and how it was derived (simulation with an estimated ACF, or permutation with ≥5,000 permutations).
- **Don't use Bonferroni on voxels** — voxels are spatially autocorrelated, so it is over-conservative and buys a high false-negative rate. Use cluster or permutation correction, and report the contiguity (NN1/NN2/NN3) and sidedness you used.
- **Estimate smoothness from the residuals**, not from your smoothing kernel — the estimate will exceed the kernel because smoothness is already present in the data. Round required cluster sizes up.
- **Define ROIs independently of the contrast being tested**: atlas, prior-study coordinates, meta-analysis map, or localizer. Anything drawn from the map under test is circular.
- **Preselection only counts if it binds.** Abandoning a preselected ROI because the whole-brain map points elsewhere is a biased analysis, however reasonable it feels.
- **Never report effect sizes or error bars from a biased ROI.** Detection and magnitude are different claims, and bias inflates magnitude systematically because small studies can only detect large effects.
- **Unpack every contrast into per-condition betas before interpreting it.** A significant A−B can mean A is high, B is negative, both negative with A less so, or a mix. A difference is not a level.
- **Before writing a discussion, check the region's base rate** (Neurosynth). The ACC's ~20% base activation rate means a dACC blob constrains interpretation very little on its own.
- **Selectivity requires all five double-dissociation criteria**, plus a control region with real signal. An interaction against a region with no activity (the ventricles) is trivially significant and evidentially empty.
- **Necessity requires a lesion or a disruption method** (TMS as a virtual, temporary, reversible lesion). Activation, however strong, is not necessity.
- **State that BOLD is several inference steps from neural firing** — stimulus → transduction → firing → oxygen demand → blood delivery → hydrogen signal — whenever the strength of the claim depends on it.
- **Design for jitter.** Without it, conditions within ~10 s are confounded by overlapping HRFs, and no analysis recovers them.
- **Chase the behavioral effect first.** A weak or absent behavioral effect predicts a null or noise-driven imaging result.
- **When skull stripping, leave too much rather than too little** — extra skull rarely breaks downstream steps; removed cortex is unrecoverable.
- **Justify slice-timing correction rather than defaulting to it**: worth it for TRs ≥2 s (especially dorsally); of little value below ~1 s; and often substitutable with a temporal derivative in the model. Prefer not interpolating when you don't need to.
- **Skip or minimize smoothing for MVPA** — pattern information lives in the fine spatial structure smoothing removes.
- **Match your template to your population.** MNI152 is 152 healthy adults; children and older adults warrant population-specific templates. And match mask resolution to data resolution.
- **Extract ROI data from the 2nd level, not the 3rd** — you need one contrast estimate per subject.
- **Distinguish anatomical labels from functional units.** A large anatomical ROI may span several functional regions and wash out a real effect; a null from an anatomical mask is not a null for the function.
- **In RSA, weight cross-run cells over within-run cells** — within-run correlations share run-level noise.
- **Set the FIX threshold deliberately**: ~5–20 moderate, <5 liberal, >20 conservative — and hand-label components using the frequency/smoothness/anatomical-location heuristics rather than trusting defaults blindly.
- **Be suspicious of explanations that are too neat and too aligned with the hypothesis** — in others' papers and your own.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Mass univariate testing makes multiple-comparisons correction mandatory, and the cluster-defining threshold is not the correction.** Know which number is which.
2. **Circularity is the field's most common serious error**, it inflates effect sizes systematically, and it has subtle forms — including switching ROIs after seeing the map.
3. **Reverse inference strength is set by the region's base rate.** A blob in a promiscuous region licenses almost nothing without converging evidence or a design that ruled out alternatives.
4. **Selectivity claims require the five double-dissociation criteria**, a control region with genuine signal, and preferably an interaction — and necessity requires lesion or TMS evidence.
5. **A contrast is a difference, not a level.** Always unpack it.
6. **Every preprocessing step encodes an assumption** and several are genuinely contested (slice timing, smoothing). Justify each one in both directions.
7. **BOLD is an indirect measure several steps from firing**, and the design (jitter, behavioral effect, motion-condition independence) determines whether any analysis can recover the signal you want.
8. **MVPA changed the question from amplitude to pattern**, and with it what counts as evidence that a region "encodes" something — but it inherits every inferential obligation above.
