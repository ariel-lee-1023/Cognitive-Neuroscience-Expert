## Mental Model (read first)
This is the **cellular-and-clinical substrate layer** of the library. Its governing logic: *cognitive functions are localized enough that lesions dissociate them, but distributed enough that no structure works alone* — and the way you earn a claim about localization is by triangulating **imaging + lesion + disease/injury**, never one alone. Where the computational sources give you an algorithm, this gives you the ion channels, the pathways, the syndromes, and the patient data that any algorithm must answer to.

**Reach for this book when you need**: the actual molecular cascade behind a plasticity claim; the anatomical pathway connecting two areas; the specific clinical syndrome that dissociates two functions; or the physiological basis of a measurement (why BOLD is a blood-flow proxy, not neural activity).

**Coverage note**: this distillation front-loads Section 4 (higher brain functions), Section 1's plasticity chapters, and the systems chapters bearing on cognition. Deliberately dropped as out of scope for higher cognition, with the reason: Section 1 ch. 1–6, 8–15 (resting/action potentials, propagation, NMJ, transmitter release, cell types, transmitter chemistry) — foundational electrophysiology and neurochemistry available in any standard text; Section 2 ch. 1–13 (somatosensation, pain, chemical senses, vestibular, auditory) — sensory systems outside the brief's perception/attention focus; Section 3 ch. 1–3, 6–8 (motor units, spinal reflexes, motor cortex, motor disorders, ocular motor) — retained only where they bear on action selection; Section 4 ch. 1–4 (hypothalamus, pituitary, autonomic, feeding) — homeostatic rather than cognitive; Section 4 ch. 12 (trauma/cell death) — clinical neurology.

## Frameworks & Structure

### S1 Ch 7: Synaptic Plasticity — the taxonomy that organizes all plasticity talk
- **Core idea**: Synapses are not fixed solder joints. The historical shift is the whole point — plasticity is the default, not the exception.
- **The primary axis: homosynaptic vs. heterosynaptic.** **Homosynaptic (intrinsic)** = a synapse changes strength *through its own activity*. **Heterosynaptic (extrinsic)** = a synapse changes *because a third, modulatory pathway fired*. **Use this distinction first when someone claims "the synapse changed" — it determines what could have caused it and what experiment can test it.**
- **Homosynaptic forms**:
  - **Synaptic depression** — second EPSP smaller (~200 ms interval). Common mechanism: **depletion of available transmitter**.
  - **Synaptic facilitation** (paired-pulse / twin-pulse) — second EPSP larger (~20 ms interval). Mechanism: **residual calcium** — Ca²⁺ from spike 1 has not yet cleared, so spike 2's influx sums on top, releasing more transmitter.
  - **Post-tetanic potentiation (PTP)** — an extreme facilitation lasting minutes after a tetanus.
- **Synapse types by target** (needed to read the heterosynaptic cases): **axosomatic** (onto soma), **axodendritic** (onto dendrite; most prominent), **axoaxonic** (onto another synapse) — *axoaxonic synapses are what make presynaptic modulation anatomically possible*.
- **Heterosynaptic forms**: **presynaptic inhibition** — modulator engages **metabotropic** receptors → second messenger phosphorylates Ca²⁺ channels so they open less readily → less Ca²⁺ influx → less transmitter → smaller EPSP. Prominent in spinal cord, regulating what reaches higher centers. **Presynaptic facilitation** — the complement, more Ca²⁺ influx, more release.
- **LTP** — enduring, with both homosynaptic and heterosynaptic components. See the mechanism under S4 Ch 7.
- **Term** — **ionotropic** (receptor is itself an ion channel) vs. **metabotropic** (receptor triggers a second-messenger cascade, passes no ions). Nearly every modulatory phenomenon in this book runs through metabotropic receptors.
- **The scale argument**: each postsynaptic cell makes and receives ~10,000 connections, and this module repeats across billions of cells. Plasticity at each one is what makes the system extraordinary — and is why single-synapse findings do not straightforwardly scale to behavior.

### S4 Ch 7: Learning and Memory (Byrne) — the model-system argument
- **Core idea**: Four organizing questions — *what types of memory, where located, how does it work mechanistically, and how can it be improved/repaired.* And one answer that recurs: **learning is a change in the strength of pre-existing synaptic connections**, not reorganization of the nervous system or growth of new neurons.
- **Declarative vs. nondeclarative** (Squire & Knowlton). **Declarative** ("knowing what") — conscious component; facts (semantic) and events (episodic). **Nondeclarative / implicit** ("knowing how") — skills and habits, **priming**, simple **associative** learning (classical/Pavlovian conditioning), simple **nonassociative** learning (**habituation**, **sensitization**).
- **Anatomical loci of memory systems**: medial temporal lobe/hippocampus → facts and events; **striatum** → skills and habits; **neocortex** → priming; **amygdala** → emotional memories; **cerebellum** → simple associative learning; lower brain/spinal cord → simpler forms still. **Memory is not stored in one place.**
- **Three methods for localizing memory, with what each buys you**: (1) **imaging** (fMRI/PET) — shows activity *presumably* related to processing/storage; correlational. (2) **lesions** in animals — causal, controlled, but species-limited. (3) **brain disease and injury** in humans — causal and human, but uncontrolled lesion extent. *The chapter uses all three on the same claim; that is the standard.*
- **The DRM test** (Deese–Roediger–McDermott) — a *methodological* lesson, not a party trick. People falsely recall "sweet" from a list of sweet-connoted words. **Memory is not a photograph in a filing cabinet; it is torn into fragments stored in different drawers and *reconstructed* at recall.** The diagnostic sting: **Alzheimer's patients generally do *not* falsely report "sweet"** — they cannot make the normal associations that produce the intrusion. **A "failure" that indexes intact associative machinery.** Caveat given: the word-list test is vulnerable to distortion and bias, so **object recognition** and **object location** tests are preferred for "raw" memory ability — and they transfer to animals.
- **Classical (Pavlovian) conditioning** terminology, stated precisely: **CS** (novel/weak stimulus) paired with **US** (elicits **UR** reflexively); after contingent CS–US pairing (sometimes a single trial), CS alone elicits a **CR** resembling the UR or an aspect of it. **Contingency, not mere contiguity, is what is trained.**
- **H.M. (Henry Molaison)** — bilateral hippocampal removal for intractable epilepsy. Severe **anterograde amnesia** (no new facts/events, no new vocabulary, no memory of the previous day's interview); **old memories intact** (childhood, pre-operative). Two conclusions the case licenses and no more: **the hippocampus is critical for the *formation* of new declarative memories, and is *not* where old memories are stored** (they reside elsewhere, e.g. frontal cortex, after **consolidation**). Third finding: he **could form new skill and habit memories but had no awareness of having them** — could not declare them. **That awareness/performance split is the clean dissociation of declarative from nondeclarative.**
- **Aplysia californica as a model system** (Kandel, Nobel 2000) — the explicit methodological argument for why a sea slug earns a chapter. Three technical advantages: (1) it exhibits classical conditioning, operant conditioning, and sensitization; (2) ~10,000 cells total, ~2,000 per ganglion, so a behavior may be controlled by ≤100 neurons — **you can work out the complete circuit and then ask what changed in it after training**; (3) neurons are large, individually identifiable by location and function, and survive in culture where they re-establish normal synaptic connections. **This is the template for choosing a preparation: pick the system where the causal chain is fully traceable, then test whether the principle generalizes.**
- **Sensitization** — defined precisely: *enhancement of the response to a test stimulus as a result of delivering a strong, generally noxious stimulus.* Circuit: skin → sensory neuron (SN) → glutamatergic excitatory synapse → motor neuron (MN) → muscle. Modulation: sensitizing stimulus releases **serotonin (5-HT)** from an interneuron onto the SN–MN synapse.
- **Short-term sensitization mechanism**: 5-HT binds two receptor types on the SN — one coupled to **DAG/PKC**, one to **cyclic AMP/PKA**. The kinases (a) modulate membrane channels so more Ca²⁺ enters the terminal per spike *and* SN excitability rises (more spikes per test stimulus), (b) regulate the size of the releasable vesicle pool, and (c) 5-HT increases postsynaptic glutamate receptor number. **Duration is set by how long substrate proteins stay phosphorylated** — cAMP degrades, PKA falls, phosphatases strip the phosphates. *The memory is transient because the biochemistry is transient.*
- **Three general principles from sensitization, asserted to hold in vertebrates too**: (1) **learning engages second messenger systems** — "in every example of learning that has ever been examined, whether vertebrate or invertebrate"; (2) **memory involves modulation of neuronal membrane channels** (presynaptic Ca²⁺ channels, excitability channels, postsynaptic receptor channels); (3) **cyclic AMP is one of the critical second messengers**.
- **Short-term vs. long-term memory — the two differences that matter**: long-term involves (a) **changes in protein synthesis and gene regulation**, and (b) in many cases **structural modification**. Evidence: dye-filled sensory neurons 24 h after sensitization training have **more branches and more synaptic varicosities** than untrained. Pathway: cAMP → PKA → phosphorylates **CREB** (cAMP responsive element binding protein) → gene expression → proteins that induce and maintain long-term synaptic change.
- **Anti-pattern — the "magic memory gene."** Stated flatly: there is none. Induction and maintenance, *even in a single neuron*, engage multiple genes and proteins acting synergistically, in **temporal phases** (some changes appear early, some 24 h after learning).
- **LTP as the candidate mechanism for declarative memory**: brain slice, test stimulus to afferents, record summated EPSP. Repeated 1/min stimulation → constant EPSP. A **1-second, 100 Hz tetanus** → transient **PTP** (dies in minutes) followed by enduring **LTP**.
- **NMDA-dependent LTP at the CA3–CA1 synapse** — the canonical mechanism: CA1 spines carry **AMPA** and **NMDA** glutamate receptors; both pass Na⁺/K⁺, but NMDA additionally (a) passes **Ca²⁺** and (b) is **blocked by Mg²⁺** at rest. A weak stimulus produces a purely AMPA-mediated EPSP — *"it is as if the NMDA receptor were not even there."* A tetanus produces spatial and temporal summation across multiple afferents onto the common postsynaptic cell → strong depolarization → the positively-charged Mg²⁺ is repelled out → Ca²⁺ enters → protein kinases → **insertion of new AMPA receptors** (plus evidence for increased presynaptic release, acting synergistically).
- **The unification claim, which is the chapter's real payoff**: the mechanism for a *nondeclarative* memory (sensitization) and a *declarative* one (LTP) both involve **second messenger activation and membrane channel regulation**. "At a fundamental mechanistic level, there does not appear to be significant differences between the two major classes of memory systems. **The major difference appears to be the brain region and the neural circuit into which the learning mechanism is embedded.**" *Take this as the source's strongest general thesis — and note it is a claim about mechanism sharing, not about function sharing.*
- **Worked cautionary result — NMDA overexpression** (Tsien et al.): transgenic mice with extra NMDA receptors show **perfect object recognition memory at 1 day** where controls are already poor — **but no better at 1 week.** Interpretation offered without spin: NMDA receptors are important but "are not the whole story"; improving memory further requires manipulating multiple genes. **A model of how to report a positive result honestly bounded.**

### S4 Ch 5: Limbic System — Hippocampus (Wright)
- **Core idea**: The hippocampus is a **one-way three-synapse circuit** embedded in a set of large fiber loops, and its pathology (epilepsy) and its plasticity (LTP) both follow from its cell architecture.
- **Limbic** = Latin *border* — the border between neocortex and subcortical structures. Members: hippocampal formation, **amygdala**, septal nuclei, cingulate cortex, **entorhinal**, **perirhinal**, **parahippocampal** cortex.
- **Hippocampal formation** = dentate gyrus + hippocampus proper (cornu ammonis) + subicular cortex.
- **The trisynaptic circuit** — memorize this, it is referenced throughout the library: (1) **perforant pathway**, entorhinal cortex → dentate gyrus granule cells; (2) **mossy fibers**, DG granule cells → CA3 pyramidal cells; (3) **Schaffer collaterals**, CA3 → CA1. Output: CA fields → subiculum → alveus → fimbria → fornix → rest of brain.
- **The fornix, as an anatomical landmark system**: alveus → fimbria → crura → **hippocampal commissure** → fornix → **anterior commissure**, where it splits three ways: **precommissural fornix** → septal nuclei, ventral striatum, orbital and anterior cingulate cortex; fibers **through** the anterior commissure → contralateral hippocampus; **postcommissural fornix** → **mammillary bodies** of hypothalamus and **anterior thalamic nuclei**.
- **Papez circuit**: hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → (mammillothalamic tract) → anterior thalamic nuclei → cingulate cortex → entorhinal cortex → back to hippocampus. Originally proposed as the substrate of emotional experience. **Correction stated plainly**: the amygdala and neocortical areas are now known to be centrally involved in emotional experience; Papez's circuit is better understood as serving learning, memory, emotion and social behavior jointly.
- **Clinical anchor** — **Korsakoff's syndrome**: mammillary bodies destroyed by alcoholism and **thiamine deficiency** → profound difficulty forming new memories. A non-hippocampal lesion producing hippocampal-type amnesia, *because it cuts the same loop*.
- **Archicortex, and a terminological warning**: hippocampus and DG are **3-layered** cortex, not 6-layered; "paleocortex"/"archicortex" are **misleading** labels because they imply antiquated remnants — these regions "have actually continued to develop structurally and functionally throughout phylogeny." Structure is **inside-out** relative to neocortex: molecular layer inside, polymorphic layer (the alveus, ≈ white matter) outside; middle layer is pyramidal in hippocampus proper and granular in DG.
- **Four special properties of hippocampal pyramidal cells, and what each causes**: (1) dendrites **parallel to one another** → summation of extracellular current flow → hyperexcitability and a readable EEG signal; (2) dendrites **perpendicular to the cortical surface** → different cortical layers impinge at different dendritic points; (3) **dendritic spines amplify** inputs so distant synapses can still drive spikes; (4) **basket cell recurrent inhibition** regulates excitability.
- **Recurrent inhibition** — the general circuit motif: pyramidal cell → excitatory collateral → **basket cell (GABA)** → inhibitory feedback onto the same pyramidal cell. *Activity regulates itself through negative feedback.* Without basket cells, pyramidal cells would burst continuously.
- **Epilepsy as a mechanism, not a label**: a seizure is **synchronous discharge of hundreds of thousands of pyramidal cells**, caused by **breakdown of postsynaptic inhibition**. Two convergent lines: **picrotoxin** (GABA antagonist) disables the basket→pyramidal junction and produces the cascade experimentally; and temporal-lobe epilepsy patients show **loss of basket cells** in the plexus around pyramidal cells. *"The brake on the system has been removed."* **Focal/partial** epilepsy can spread to become **generalized**. Next to stroke, the most common neurological disease (~1% of the population).

### S4 Ch 6: Limbic System — Amygdala (Wright)
- **Core idea**: The amygdala is the **integrative center for emotion, emotional behavior, and motivation** — and the site where the *fast-but-crude vs. slow-but-accurate* routing principle is best demonstrated.
- **Inputs**: all sensory modalities plus **visceral** inputs (hypothalamus, septal area, orbital cortex, parabrachial nucleus) — *"this is where you get your gut reaction."* Olfactory from olfactory bulb; auditory/visual/somatosensory from temporal and anterior cingulate cortices.
- **Outputs**: ventral amygdalofugal pathway; **stria terminalis**; direct to hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and dorsomedial thalamus.
- **The structural analogy worth keeping**: **the stria terminalis is to the amygdala as the fornix is to the hippocampus** — same form, function, and location logic; precommissural branch → septal area, postcommissural branch → hypothalamus (lateral and ventromedial nuclei, vs. the fornix's mammillary bodies); some fibers cross the anterior commissure so the two amygdalae communicate. Difference: the stria terminalis connects **only to subcortical** structures; cortical connection runs through the **ventral amygdalofugal pathway** (→ orbitofrontal, anterior cingulate, ventral striatum incl. nucleus accumbens, piriform cortex).
- **Central nucleus — a functional double role**: **autonomic components of emotion** (heart rate, blood pressure, respiration) via lateral hypothalamus and brain stem; **conscious perception of emotion** via ventral amygdalofugal output to anterior cingulate, orbitofrontal, and prefrontal cortex.
- **Stimulation vs. destruction — opposite signs**: irritative lesions of temporal lobe epilepsy effectively *stimulate* the amygdala → intense emotion, in the extreme a **panic attack** (PET shows increased blood flow to parahippocampal gyri, beginning on the right). **Destructive lesions** → **tameness** in animals, **flatness of affect** in humans (a component of **Kluver-Bucy syndrome**). *Historical note delivered without endorsement*: this observation led to prefrontal lobotomy, since PFC inputs to the amygdala were severed to produce flatness of affect.
- **Urbach-Wiethe disease** — calcium deposition in the amygdala. If early in life, bilateral lesions leave patients **unable to discriminate emotion in facial expressions while face *identity* recognition remains intact** (identity being an inferotemporal multimodal association function). **A clean double-target dissociation: emotion in one area linked to perception in another.**
- **Fear conditioning — the two-route model.** Thalamus→amygdala is **one synaptic link: fast, crude**. Cortex→amygdala is **multi-link: slow, detailed, accurate**. Evidence for the functional split: **lesioning the auditory cortex pathway leaves basic fear conditioning intact but abolishes discrimination** (one tone paired with shock, another not — animals relying on thalamus+amygdala alone cannot tell them apart). Conclusion: **cortex is not needed for simple fear conditioning; it is needed to interpret the environment.** The adaptive rationale given: better to jump back from a garden hose than fail to jump back from a snake — there is time to be embarrassed later.
- **The amygdala/hippocampus double dissociation — the chapter's best experiment.** Patients viewed colored slides; some colors followed by a frightening horn blast; GSR recorded. **Amygdala patients**: no autonomic conditioning, but **correct explicit recall** of which colors were followed by the horn. **Hippocampal patients**: normal conditioning, but **could not recall** which colors. **Both lesions**: neither conditioning nor explicit memory. *This is the cleanest available separation of implicit emotional learning from explicit episodic memory in humans — cite it when the two are being conflated.*
- **The amygdala is not only for fear** — basolateral nucleus lesions disrupt stimulus–reward associations in instrumental learning, where all three pairwise combinations (stimulus, response, reward) are learned.
- **Anti-pattern** — attributing a memory to one structure: "The establishment of memories is a function of the entire network, not any single component."

### S4 Ch 8: Higher Cortical Functions — Language (Wright)
- **Core idea**: The classical Wernicke model is the right *starting* framework and a demonstrated **oversimplification** — the value of the chapter is watching a model earn credit for a successful prediction and then be superseded.
- **Method origin story with a moral**: Gall's **phrenology** correlated skull bumps to faculties. **Broca's** difference was to examine brains of people with clinical disorders for lesions that indicate the responsible region — *"thus was born the field of neuropsychology."* **The lesson is about inference discipline, not about who was right.**
- **The founding cases**: **Broca (1861)**, patient **Leborgne** — could understand but not speak, with no motor deficit accounting for it (could whistle, utter isolated words, sing lyrics); lesion in posterior frontal lobe. **Wernicke (1876)** — could speak but not comprehend; lesion at the **temporal–parietal–occipital junction**. *The reciprocal pattern is what makes the pair evidential.*
- **Wernicke's model**: primary/unimodal sensory areas → **angular gyrus** (written and spoken words converted to a common neural representation) → **Wernicke's area** (recognized as language, associated with meaning) → via **arcuate fasciculus** → **Broca's area** (transformed into motor representation for speech).
- **The model's successful prediction**: **conduction aphasia** — comprehension and articulation spared, but the two areas disconnected; patients omit parts of words, substitute incorrect sounds, **are aware of their errors but cannot correct them**. *Predicting a syndrome before observing it is the strongest thing a box-and-arrow model can do.*
- **Where it fails**: functional imaging shows the model is an oversimplification; Wernicke's area subdivides — **frontal-temporal lesions → lexical deficits** (meaning), **parietal-temporal lesions → syntactical deficits** (relating words in sentences). Conduction aphasia is **not** produced by arcuate fasciculus disconnection alone; damage destroys many interconnections among temporal, parietal, insular and frontal cortices responsible for assembling phonemes into words.
- **Modern three-system framework**: **Implementation system** (Broca's, Wernicke's, insular cortex, basal ganglia — analyze incoming speech into phonemes and grammar) → **Mediational system** (surrounding temporal/parietal/frontal association cortices — brokers between implementation and concepts) → **Conceptual system** (association cortices broadly — learning, memory, conceptual knowledge).
- **Grammar subdivided** (use these terms precisely): **morphology** (combining words into longer words via prefixes/suffixes; subject-predicate agreement), **phonology** (combining sound elements/phonemes into words — D-O-G ≠ G-O-D), **prosody** (intonation and stress; distinguishes question from statement), **syntax** (combining words into phrases/sentences so meaning is clear).
- **Innate vs. learned — the evidence marshalled on both sides.** For innate: all cultures learn language; children in a social group deprived of a developed language **invent their own**; Homo habilis skulls show enlargement at Broca's area. For learned/critical-period: children deprived of language acquire it fully **if before puberty**, and are "very inept" after; **infant left-hemispherectomy → full language develops in the right hemisphere, adult left-hemispherectomy → all language skills gone forever**; multiple languages acquired perfectly young, with accents and grammatical errors later. **Verdict: a critical period, with a developing brain plastic for language for several years.** Corroborating measure: early English learners show highly left-lateralized activation to grammatical violations; later learners show increasingly **bilateral** activation.
- **Is language unique to humans?** Alex the parrot (names/requests/refuses 100+ objects; combines number with color; same/different across matter, shape, color; accurate on novel instances); apes with signs/tokens (hundreds of signs). **Verdict: probably not language — the missing element is grammar.** The specific evidence: apes learn by rote that "Give me" must prefix "apple" without learning the *meaning* of "Give me"; and **their word-string length does not increase with training, as children's does.** *Note the argument turns on a growth curve, not on a ceiling — a good template for "is X really Y" questions.*
- **Lateralization**: left hemisphere in ~96% of the population, somewhat less for left-handers. **Why lateralization is what made language localizable at all**: bilateral representation would let the undamaged hemisphere take over, so lesions would rarely reveal aphasia. Four lines of evidence: neuropsychology of stroke/head injury/herpes encephalitis/degenerative disease; functional imaging in normals; **electrical stimulation during neurosurgery**; **split-brain patients**.
- **The aphasias — differential diagnosis table logic**:
  - **Broca** (BA 44/45, inferior left frontal gyrus + underlying white matter, insula, basal ganglia): slow, effortful, impaired articulation, **lacking prosody**; the difficulty is in *transitions between sounds*, not individual sounds; perseveration.
  - **Wernicke** (BA 22, temporal-parietal-occipital junction, left auditory association cortex): fluent, effortless, melodic, but **jumbled content**; **neologisms** (jumbled phonemes); **semantic paraphasia** ("headman" for "president"); impaired comprehension.
  - **Conduction** (BA 39/40): production and comprehension relatively spared; **cannot repeat sentences accurately**; anomia.
  - **Transcortical motor** (left dorsolateral frontal, anterior/superior to Broca's): trouble saying "kick" in response to "ball" though the words are used correctly in ordinary speech; **can repeat even very long sentences**.
  - **Transcortical sensory** (temporal-parietal-occipital junction): fluent with impaired comprehension like Wernicke, **but repetition is accurate**.
  - **Global** (anterior language region + basal ganglia + insula + posterior language region + superior temporal gyrus, typically MCA infarct): all deficits combined; retains expletives and overlearned series (days of the week).
  - **The organizing axis: repetition intact vs. impaired separates transcortical from Broca/Wernicke/conduction. Use repetition as the discriminating probe.**
- **The most important reinterpretation in the chapter — Broca's aphasia is partly a memory deficit.** Apparent normal comprehension is "largely an illusion": patients extract meaning from a few key words because most sentences are simple. Test with a sentence where world knowledge cannot substitute: they understand *"The apple that the boy is eating is red"* (apples don't eat boys; boys aren't red) but fail *"The boy that the girl is chasing is tall"* (either could be tall, either could chase). Yet grammar is not absent — they preserve subject-verb-object order and detect that *"John was finally kissed Louise"* is ungrammatical. **What fails is linking two elements referring to the same thing** ("The woman is outside, isn't she?" vs. "…isn't it?"), and **difficulty scales with the gap between elements.** Converging imaging: a subregion of Broca's area is more strongly activated in normals when the gap is long. **Conclusion: a short-term memory limitation masquerading as a syntactic one — a standing warning that a deficit's label may misidentify its mechanism.**
- **ASL evidence — the modality-independence result**: deaf signers use the same areas the same way; **Wernicke-area lesions → loss of sign comprehension; Broca-area lesions → loss of sign production.** *Language organization is not organized around sound.*
- **Dysarthria vs. apraxia** — a distinction commonly blurred: **dysarthria** = inability to control articulatory muscles → weak, flaccid, systematically distorted speech. **Apraxia** = deficit in *planning* the speech movements → errors phonemically similar to the target ("yawyer"/"lawyer", "cookun"/"cushion"). Apraxia nearly always accompanies Broca's aphasia and accounts for its effortful style, **but the two dissociate**: 100% of 25 apraxia patients had **insular** lesions, while 19 controls without apraxia had lesions *surrounding* the insula and none within it. **Near-perfect lesion-overlap dissociation — a model of the method.**
- **Category-specific naming**: animals → medial occipital cortex; tools → left middle temporal gyrus plus left **premotor** site — the premotor activation attributed to imagining grasping the tool. Corroborated by matching patient lesion maps for each category.
- **Right hemisphere**: controls **prosody**, with an organization roughly paralleling Broca's/Wernicke's — **right inferior frontal gyrus** produces prosody, **right posterior temporoparietal** comprehends it. Clinical picture: a teacher who could not convey anger to control her class; patients who don't understand jokes and struggle in social gatherings.
- **Alexia** (cannot read) / **agraphia** (cannot write) — alexia from disruption of visual input from *both* hemispheres to left-hemisphere language areas; reading activates a left-hemisphere area just anterior to visual cortex. **Developmental dyslexia** (10–30% estimates; cause not settled, possibly multiple): candidate accounts offered as candidates — abnormal visual-to-language connections; **failure of the left planum temporale to be larger than the right** in dyslexic males, with incomplete cell-layer segregation and inappropriately connected neuron clusters suggesting disrupted neuronal migration; slowed conduction in the **magnocellular** visual pathway (abnormally small cells), consistent with trouble processing fast, high-contrast stimuli like words.

### S4 Ch 9: Higher Cortical Functions — Association and Executive Processing (Wright)
- **Core idea**: A **hierarchy of association areas** (Hughlings Jackson, 1870s), where *the further from primary sensory cortex, the more general the associative function*. These areas are "the site of cognitive processing and the focal areas in the rapidly developing field of cognitive neuroscience."
- **Three multimodal association areas**: **Limbic** (anterior-ventral temporal lobe, parahippocampal gyrus) — links emotion with sensory inputs; learning and memory. **Posterior** (occipital-temporal-parietal junction) — links primary and unimodal sensory areas; perception and language. **Anterior** (prefrontal cortex) — links other association areas; memory, planning, higher-order concept formation.
- **Flow**: receptors → primary sensory cortex → unimodal association → **posterior multimodal** → **anterior association** (concept formation, motor planning) → premotor (BA 6, 8) → motor. **The motor hierarchy is the reverse of the sensory hierarchy** — and **retrieval apparently reverses the paths used in storage** (Fuster).
- **The agnosias — a set of dissociations to keep straight**:
  - **Prosopagnosia**: cannot recognize familiar faces or learn new ones, while identifying a face *as* a face, its parts, and its emotional expression remains intact. They have not lost knowledge of relatives — they identify them by voice. **Lesions are always bilateral**, inferior surface of both occipital lobes extending forward to inner temporal surfaces.
  - **Associative agnosia** (posterior multimodal / posterior parietal): **can perceive and copy drawings but cannot name objects** — and have not lost the names, since they name them correctly **by touch**.
  - **Apperceptive agnosia** (occipital and surrounding): **cannot copy drawings but can name the objects.**
  - **The copy/name double dissociation is the point** — it separates perceptual construction from semantic access, and the by-touch control rules out a naming deficit.
  - **Global vs. local**: right hemisphere damage disrupts perception of **global** objects; left hemisphere damage disrupts **local** — only in acute stages post-stroke, only with lesions centered at the **temporal-parietal junction (TPJ)**.
- **Contralateral neglect syndrome** — right posterior parietal lesions; patients ignore the left, including the left halves of objects and of their own body (**personal neglect**, sometimes disowning the limb — "Who put this arm in my bed"); draw flowers with petals only on the right, clocks with numbers only on the right.
- **The infinite regress argument — why neglect cannot be sensory.** A little thought settles it: it is not that only the right halves of objects reach the sensory system. Most objects are made of parts, and each part is itself an object — yet patients do not see only the right half of *each petal*. **A structural argument that rules out a whole class of explanation without new data. Reuse this form of reasoning.**
- **The Piazza del Duomo study (Milan)** — the decisive evidence that neglect is a *processing/access* deficit, not a storage deficit. Patients imagined facing the cathedral and recalled only buildings on their imagined right. Told to imagine standing on the cathedral steps facing the opposite way, they recalled **exactly the buildings they had previously omitted**. **Their memory of the square is complete and fully accessible; what is impaired is recall of objects on the left *of their current frame of reference*** — a frame centered on the body, in memory as in life. They are apparently unaware of the deficit. Note the temporal control: they knew the square long before the lesion, so the memories were not laid down incomplete.
- **Frame-of-reference complication**: neglect can be **object-centered** rather than body-centered — a patient attends to the examiner's left hand even when the examiner rotates, neglecting the right hand regardless of body orientation.
- **Balint's syndrome** (bilateral parietal): the counterintuitive result. Rather than neglecting both sides and seeing nothing, patients see **one object at a time — simultanagnosia**; objects appear automatically and are replaced at random with no control. Severe ADL impairment (getting lost, cannot grasp, eat, dress, or move around a room unassisted) — **yet they can correctly touch parts of their own bodies.**
- **Phineas Gage** — reliable, organized, hardworking foreman; tamping iron through the prefrontal cortex; physically recovered in weeks; **"Gage was not Gage"** — unreliable, impulsive, disregarding consequences. The founding case for PFC in personality and self-regulation.
- **Prefrontal lobotomy outcomes** (as clinical evidence, the procedure now replaced by drug therapy): loss of ability to **remember and relate things over time**; **delayed reward becomes more detrimental to learning**; diminished attention span and concentration; abstract reasoning largely disappears. **Lesions of the dorsomedial thalamic nucleus produce many of the same symptoms** — because PFC receives massive input from it (plus from somatosensory, visual and auditory association cortices).
- **PFC subdivisions**: dorsal (superior), medial (surrounding the principal sulcus), ventral-orbitofrontal (inferior). **"Prefrontal syndrome"** usually means damage to the dorsal association area, with research concentrated on **dorsolateral prefrontal (BA 46)**.
- **DLPFC + cingulate**: attentional processing, planning, rule learning and memory. Clinical sign: serial-7 subtraction that starts correctly then **inexplicably shifts** ("100, 93, 93, 73…63"). **Key dynamic finding: DLPFC and cingulate are highly active while sequences are being learned, and are no longer active once learning is complete and automated.** *Activation is a signature of acquisition, not of performance — a standing caution for interpreting task-related activation.*
- **WHERE vs. WHAT in PFC — the delay-activity evidence.** Neurons in DLPFC and the principal sulcus mediate memory for **WHERE** an object is; they receive from the **dorsal** pathway through posterior parietal cortex; they respond only to a particular visual-field position and **continue firing through the delay**. **When they stop firing during the delay, the monkey has usually forgotten the location** — the tightest available link between sustained firing and the content of memory. Neurons just **ventral** to the principal sulcus mediate memory for **WHAT** the object is (shape, color), targeted by the ventral pathway through inferior temporal lobe.
- **Affective integration**: orbitofrontal and medial PFC connect directly to amygdala and cingulate, supplying the emotional component of planned behavior and memory.
- **Dopamine and schizophrenia**: dopamine is the dominant PFC neurotransmitter and **depletion can create lesion-like symptoms**. Many people with schizophrenia show PFC hypofunction; symptoms like being controlled by alien voices suggest executive-control dysfunction. **The reward manipulation is the informative bit**: on the **Wisconsin Card Sorting Test** prefrontal blood flow is much lower than in controls, **but when rewarded for correct responses their blood flow increases *more* than controls' and performance improves.** *That converts a "structural deficit" reading into a motivational/engagement one — reason to be careful with hypofrontality claims.* Converging: impaired cingulate activation in cognitive tasks plus postmortem cingulate abnormalities.
- **Split-brain evidence** (corpus callosum transected as a last-resort epilepsy treatment): a cone shown to the **left** visual field (right hemisphere) → patient denies seeing anything and **confabulates** when pressed — *yet* can select the correct object by feel or point to it **with the left hand**. Flashing **D-O-G** to the right hemisphere lets patients pick out a model dog with the left hand, establishing this is language, not cross-modal matching. **Left hemisphere dominant for language and mathematics; right hemisphere dominant for music, face recognition, and spatial relationships** — even right-handed split-brain patients draw and assemble block patterns better with the left hand. **"The right hemisphere is not just like the left hemisphere without language."**
- **The chapter's closing methodological caution, which should govern all lesion inference**: "no part of the brain works in isolation… When a part of the brain is removed, **the resulting behavior may reflect more about the adjusted capacities of the remaining 'parts' than the removed part.**"

### S3 Ch 4: Basal Ganglia (Knierim) — gating and habit
- **Core idea**: **Voluntary movements are not initiated in the basal ganglia — they are initiated in cortex.** The BG *enable* practiced motor acts and *gate* initiation by modulating motor programs stored in cortex.
- **Circuit logic**: BG output (GPint / SNr) is **tonically inhibitory** onto thalamus (or superior colliculus). **Direct pathway** enables the proper program; **indirect pathway** inhibits competing programs.
- **The oculomotor demonstration**: SNr neurons tonically suppress collicular saccade neurons. Frontal eye fields excite direct-pathway striatal neurons → SNr momentarily inhibited → colliculus disinhibited → gaze shifts. **The movement was initiated in FEF; the BG merely released the brake.**
- **Why tonic inhibition exists — the competition argument**: cortex stores "primitive" motor programs, and a sophisticated plan requires activating them in precise temporal order. **Only one program can be active at a time** (bringing food to the mouth must not compete with shielding the face). BG normally suppress inappropriate programs; the direct pathway temporarily releases one.
- **Dopaminergic teaching signal** (Hollerman & Schultz 1998): brisk firing to **unexpected reward**; **no change** when reward was predicted ("all is proceeding normally"); **inhibition** when a predicted reward fails to arrive. Because the nigrostriatal pathway **excites the direct and inhibits the indirect** pathway, this tunes the direct/indirect balance toward programs that produced reward.
- **Cognitive extension**: parallel loops through prefrontal association and limbic cortex mean the BG select and enable **cognitive, executive, or emotional programs** stored in those cortices — the same role as in motor control.
- **Habit learning — the striatal activity shift** (Jog et al. 1999): a rat learning a T-maze tone discrimination shows striatal neurons firing **all over the maze, especially at the choice point, early in training**; once mastered, they fire **only at the start and the end**. Interpretation: the learned program now lives elsewhere; start-firing enables the cortical plan, end-firing evaluates reward outcome. **A within-structure signature of automatization — pairs directly with the DLPFC/cingulate acquisition-vs-automated finding above.**
- **Implicit probabilistic classification** (the weather-prediction task; Packard & Knowlton 2002): symbols probabilistically predict rain or sunshine, so **no explicit rule is available**; subjects improve while still "claiming to be guessing." **Patients with basal ganglia disorders are impaired** — evidence that BG loops support subconscious learning of predictive probabilities.
- **Two disorders as opposite-sign lesions of the same circuit** — the reason to learn direct/indirect properly:
  - **Parkinson's**: loss of SNc dopaminergic neurons → balance tips toward the **indirect** pathway → GPint abnormally active → thalamus over-inhibited → motor cortex under-excited → **bradykinesia/akinesia, rigidity, resting tremor**, difficulty initiating movement.
  - **Huntington's**: selective loss of **indirect-pathway** striatal neurons → balance tips toward the **direct** pathway → thalamus disinhibited → fires randomly → **choreiform movements** (involuntary, continuous, resembling fragments of adaptive movements without behavioral significance).
  - **Symptomatically opposite because they are opposite perturbations of one balance. If a model of BG function cannot generate this reciprocity, it is wrong.**

### S4 Ch 11: Cerebral Metabolism, Blood Flow, and the Physiological Basis of Imaging
- **Core idea — the single most important methodological content in this source.** Functional imaging measures **metabolism and blood flow**, which are *coupled to* neural activity, not identical to it. Everything about interpreting fMRI/PET follows from that gap.
- **Metabolic facts that set the constraints**: the brain **stores no excess energy** and derives almost all energy from **aerobic oxidation of glucose**, requiring continuous glucose and O₂. Most consumption goes to **active transport of ions to restore membrane potentials discharged during excitation and conduction** — *i.e., the signal you image is dominated by the cost of synaptic and spiking activity, which is why coupling works at all.* Blood flow stops → unconsciousness in 5–10 s; not resumed within minutes → permanent damage. **Gray matter uses ~5× the glucose of white matter.**
- **The coupling mechanism, explicitly**: regional blood flow is regulated by **dilation of cerebral vessels**, controlled by local factors — **nitric oxide (NO), PaCO₂, PaO₂, pH**. These are produced as a byproduct of activity and energy metabolism at activity-dependent rates. **Therefore blood flow to a region is related to neuronal activity in that region** — an *indirect, chemically mediated, vascular* relationship. There is an excellent correlation between glucose use and local cerebral blood flow.
- **The 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) method — why it works.** Glucose utilization begins with hexokinase phosphorylation; glucose-6-phosphate is metabolized onward to CO₂ and H₂O and **leaves the tissue**. **2-DG** is transported across the BBB by the same glucose carrier and is phosphorylated by hexokinase to **DG-6-P, which cannot be degraded further and is therefore trapped and accumulates quantitatively.** Label it (**[¹⁸F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose**) and PET reads out the rate. *The trick is a metabolic dead end — understand this and you understand what FDG-PET is actually measuring.*
- **fMRI / BOLD, stated at the right level**: fMRI is based on **increased blood flow accompanying neural activity**; the flow increase produces a **local *decrease* in deoxyhemoglobin because less oxygen is extracted**; **deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic** and is the signal source. **Unlike PET, fMRI uses a signal intrinsic to the brain** — no tracer — which is why it became the technology of choice.
- **Demonstrated functional activations** (the coupling in practice): finger/hand movement raises metabolism in the corresponding regions; spontaneous speech in right-handers raises it in **Broca's region**; visual images raise glucose utilization in primary visual cortex.
- **Clinical uses that show the resolution/limits**: PET metabolic changes complement electrophysiology in localizing **epileptogenic foci** for surgery; PET sizes infarction after ischemic stroke; both locate highly vascularized, metabolically demanding tumors.
- **Substrate flexibility, worth knowing**: during starvation the brain can use **ketone bodies** (acetoacetate, D-β-hydroxybutyrate) formed by hepatic fatty-acid catabolism, entering the TCA cycle fast enough to meet demand. Hypoglycemia (e.g. excess insulin) alters mental state and is **rapidly reversed by glucose**.

### S4 Ch 10: CNS Aging and Alzheimer's Disease (Waymire) — brief
- Structured as: aging as a **normal biological process** distinct from disease → biological changes in aging **absent disease** → AD proper, its **diagnosis** (including the distinction between clinical diagnosis and **definite AD**, which requires histopathology), **classification**, **etiology**, **genetics**, and **risk factors and environment**. **The load-bearing distinction is normal aging vs. disease** — the chapter's framing is that increased longevity "unmasked" AD.
- Ties to the memory chapter: the **DRM false-recall result** (AD patients fail to produce the normal intrusion) is a functional signature of degraded associative structure.

## Worked Example — establishing that a deficit is one of *access*, not *storage* (the Piazza del Duomo logic)
The reasoning template, worth copying whenever someone claims a representation has been lost:
1. **Observation**: neglect patients recall only the buildings on the right of an imagined square. Naive reading: the left-side memories are gone or were never encoded.
2. **Rule out encoding failure by history**: the patients knew the Piazza long before the lesion. Whatever is wrong happened after storage.
3. **Rule out a sensory account structurally** (the infinite-regress argument): if this were sensory, patients would see the right half of *each petal* of a drawn flower, since each part is itself an object. They don't. So the deficit is not at the input.
4. **Design the manipulation that separates access from storage**: hold the memory constant and **rotate the frame of reference** — ask them to imagine facing the opposite way.
5. **Result**: they now recall exactly the buildings they previously omitted. **The full representation was present and reachable the whole time; what is impaired is access relative to a body-centered frame.**
6. **Note the unawareness**: patients do not know they are omitting anything, which is itself data about the relationship between access and metacognition.

Generalization: **to show a representation still exists, find a probe that changes the retrieval route without changing the content.** The amygdala/hippocampus color-conditioning study is the same move in a different key (GSR vs. verbal report as two routes to the same episode), as is the associative-agnosia by-touch naming control.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **Triangulate localization across all three methods before believing it**: imaging (correlational), animal lesion (causal but not human), human injury/disease (human and causal but uncontrolled). One method alone supports a hypothesis, not a conclusion.
- **When interpreting any lesion, remember the deficit may describe the *remainder*, not the removed part.** Ask what the intact system does when deprived of that input before naming the missing "function."
- **Before calling a deficit by its surface label, test whether a more general mechanism explains it.** Broca's "syntactic" deficit scales with the *gap* between elements to be linked — a short-term memory limit. Apply this test routinely.
- **Use repetition as the discriminating probe among aphasias**: intact repetition → transcortical; impaired with fluent speech → Wernicke; impaired with effortful speech → Broca; disproportionately impaired relative to spared production and comprehension → conduction.
- **Distinguish homosynaptic from heterosynaptic before proposing a plasticity mechanism.** If a third pathway is required, the mechanism is metabotropic modulation at an axoaxonic synapse, not activity-dependent change at the synapse itself.
- **A "memory gene" claim is almost certainly wrong.** Expect multiple genes and proteins acting synergistically in temporal phases. The NMDA-overexpression result — better at 1 day, no better at 1 week — is the shape to expect from single-target manipulations.
- **Short-term memory ⇒ phosphorylation and channel modulation; long-term memory ⇒ gene expression (CREB) and structural change.** If a claimed long-term mechanism involves no protein synthesis, be suspicious; if a claimed short-term one requires transcription, likewise.
- **Treat functional imaging as a measure of metabolism and blood flow.** BOLD is a paramagnetic-deoxyhemoglobin signal driven by *over*-supply of blood relative to oxygen extraction, mediated by NO/CO₂/pH-driven vasodilation. Never state a BOLD result as a direct measure of neural firing.
- **Activation may be a signature of acquisition rather than performance.** DLPFC and cingulate are highly active while a sequence is being learned and go quiet once it is automated; striatal neurons fire throughout a maze early and only at start/end late. **Ask where in the learning curve the subjects were before interpreting an activation difference.**
- **Reduced activation is not automatically a structural deficit.** Schizophrenia patients' prefrontal hypoactivation on the WCST reverses — overshooting controls — under reward. Test the motivational reading before the capacity reading.
- **Fast/crude and slow/accurate routes usually coexist; ask which one a task isolates.** Thalamus→amygdala supports basic fear conditioning without cortex; cortex is needed for *discrimination*. A task requiring discrimination cannot test the subcortical route.
- **Reciprocal disorders are a strong test of circuit models.** If your account of the basal ganglia does not generate Parkinson's and Huntington's as opposite perturbations of the direct/indirect balance, the account is incomplete.
- **Whenever a structure is credited with a function, name the network.** "The establishment of memories is a function of the entire network, not any single component."
- **Prefer the lesion-overlap standard for anatomical claims**: 100% of apraxia patients had insular lesions and 0% of non-apraxic controls did, despite lesions surrounding the insula. That is what a localization claim should look like.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Learning changes the strength of existing synapses** — not wiring reorganization, not new neurons. Everything else is detail about how.
2. **The molecular mechanisms of declarative and nondeclarative memory are largely shared** (second messengers, channel modulation); what differs is **the circuit the mechanism is embedded in**.
3. **Short-term = phosphorylation; long-term = transcription (CREB) plus structural growth.** The duration of a memory equals the duration of its physical substrate.
4. **NMDA receptors are coincidence detectors** by virtue of the voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block — this single biophysical fact is the basis of associative LTP.
5. **Dissociations, not activations, carry the inferential weight**: H.M.'s declarative/procedural split, the amygdala/hippocampus conditioning-vs-recall double dissociation, associative vs. apperceptive agnosia, apraxia vs. Broca's aphasia.
6. **Neglect is an access deficit in a body- or object-centered frame**, proved by rotating the frame rather than by finding a lesion.
7. **Functional imaging measures blood flow and glucose metabolism.** Know the vasodilatory chemistry (NO, CO₂, pH), the deoxyhemoglobin basis of BOLD, and the metabolic dead end that makes 2-DG/FDG work — then state imaging results with the right hedges.
8. **Localization is real and partial.** Lesions dissociate functions cleanly enough to build a map, and no region works alone — hold both, and be explicit about which one a given claim depends on.
