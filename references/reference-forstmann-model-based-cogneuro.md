## Mental Model (read first)
This is the library's **decision-making and model-linking** source, and it fills the gap the other sources leave open: how a formal cognitive model gets *connected* to neural data, and what each way of connecting them licenses. Its governing idea is the **model-in-the-middle**: cognition is a latent construct detectable only through manifest variables, so you put a mathematical model between behavior and brain and let each constrain the other.

The book's central technical contribution to this library is the **diffusion decision model (DDM)** — the standard framework for explaining choice *and* response-time distributions from a single noisy evidence-accumulation process — plus a taxonomy of **linking structures** that tells you exactly how strong an inference a given brain–behavior analysis supports.

**Reach for this book when you need**: how decisions are made (evidence accumulation, thresholds, drift rates); what an RT distribution constrains; the difference between model-based fMRI, two-stage correlation, and joint modeling; the meaning of speed–accuracy tradeoff and response bias at the parameter level; or the concept of a **linking proposition**.

**Coverage note**: front-loads the general introduction's linking taxonomy, the DDM chapter (Smith & Ratcliff), the neurophysiological-linking case study (Cox, Palmeri, Logan, Smith & Schall), joint modeling (Turner et al.), and cognitive control (Heathcote, Verbruggen, Boehler & Matzke). Compressed: reinforcement-learning/model-based fMRI (Man & O'Doherty) — overlaps [[reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro]]; M/EEG chapters (Borst & Anderson; van Wijk; Hawkins et al.) — overlaps [[reference-kappenman-luck-erp-components]]; neuroeconomics (Gluth & Fontanesi); working memory subprocesses (Boag et al.); free recall (Polyn). Dropped with reason: ultrahigh-field MRI hardware chapter (Priovoulos et al.) — acquisition physics outside the cognitive brief.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Ch 1: General Introduction — the linking taxonomy (the most reusable content in the book)
- **Model-based cognitive neuroscience (MbCN)** is a subsection of computational cognitive neuroscience that "uses formal mathematical models to articulate a theory or theories about cognition."
- **The founding premise**: "cognition is inherently an unobservable process because it is a **latent construct**." Latent constructs are detectable only through **manifest variables** — choices, RTs, brain activations.
- **The three disciplines the "model-in-the-middle" unifies**: experimental psychology (manipulates design, reads behavior), cognitive neuroscience (manipulates design, reads brain), and mathematical psychology (builds the model). Model **parameters** are "like knobs on a machine"; **fitting** adjusts them to match data; changed parameters across conditions identify which mechanism changed. **The payoff: it "allows researchers to interpret complex changes in the manifest variables in terms of psychologically meaningful mechanisms."**

#### The three linking strategies, and what each licenses — use this to classify any brain–behavior paper
**(A) Neural data constrain the behavioral model** (brain → model)
- **Theoretical approach** — brain function informs what dynamics the model should contain, as a *conceptual* (dashed-line) constraint. Canonical example: PDP models with sigmoidal activation, competitive pooling, and recurrence, all motivated by single-unit behavior.
- **Two-stage behavioral** — parameters describing neural data are **regressed onto** cognitive-model parameters. Logic: if a pattern exists in brain data across trials or subjects, the same pattern should exist in model parameters across the same dimension.
- **Direct input approach** — the neural data *replace* a mathematical abstraction in the model, with appropriate transformation. Example: Purcell et al. (2010, 2012) and Palmeri et al. (2015), feeding recorded spikes into an accumulator.

**(B) Behavioral model predicts neural data** (model → brain) — "typically making the approach very **confirmatory** in nature"
- **Latent input approach** — fit the model to behavior, then use its **trial-level internal representation as a regressor in a whole-brain GLM.** *This is what was originally coined "model-based fMRI"* (O'Doherty et al., 2007). Most prominent case: reinforcement-learning models, with **prediction error** computed per trial and regressed against brain data.
- **Two-stage neural** — mechanically almost identical to two-stage behavioral; the difference is that the *motivation* begins with a theory rather than with an empirical neural phenomenon.

**(C) Simultaneous modeling** (both, in one model)
- **The statistical argument for preferring it**: "uncertainty in the model parameters is not properly accounted for when doing analyses of brain and behavior separately" (Turner et al., 2013).
- **Joint modeling** — a hierarchical model exploiting the covariance between neural and behavioral data. Brain data inform neural submodel parameters (δ), which affect **hyperparameters** (Ω), which indirectly inform behavioral parameters (θ). "This symbiotic relationship enforces constraint on the two submodels that would not otherwise be there."
- **Integrative approach** — one model directly explains both data streams. **Much harder**, because the parameters "must explain data that are likely to be very different, such as on different scales or connected to time in different ways" — brain data give many measurements per trial, behavior often one or two at trial's end. Usual workaround: specify a function over time and let the model modulate it.
- **Terminology to keep straight (Turner et al.)**: links are **partially reciprocal** when only one parameter set is influenced by both data streams, **fully reciprocal** when both are. Technically: whether the likelihood of a data stream is a function of one or both parameter sets.

#### Behavioral and neural measures — what each buys
- **Choice** is the dominant behavioral measure; **continuous** choice (position in space, colour on a continuum) is a growing alternative, but raises the question of how much response variance comes from the **apparatus** rather than the decision process (cf. **Fitts's law**) — so you may need a model of the response-generation process too.
- **Response time** — "provide a considerable amount of constraint on model parameters… because they define a **window in which the dynamics of the model must fit**." Also used as a GLM regressor to identify **default mode network** activation indexing task disengagement.
- **Confidence** — gives a more nuanced certainty measure than RT alone, and the two "are not always directly related."
- **Process tracing** (eye tracking, cursor position) measures behavior and can supply model inputs as well as fixation checks. Eye movements and the act of recording them are distinct. In **EEG**, corneo-retinal dipole rotation, eyelid activity, and extraocular muscle potentials can contaminate scalp voltages; simultaneous tracking can help characterize these artifacts ([Plöchl et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23087632/)). In **fMRI**, consider motion and image artifacts plus genuine visual and oculomotor BOLD activity that may covary with the task. This is not the EEG electrical-dipole mechanism. MR-compatible eye tracking can be combined with imaging; assess the actual apparatus, synchronization, calibration, and artifact controls. These measurement distinctions are editorial corrections supported by external studies, not verified quotations from Ch 1.
- **Neural measures, with the trade-off named**: single/multi-unit recording (usually hundreds to thousands of neurons, mostly animal work); **fMRI** — "an indirect measure… the measurements are delayed from the actual time that cognition was carried out, and **this lag requires careful consideration of the experimental design**"; **EEG** — high temporal precision via **volume conduction**, weak spatial precision due to source-localization difficulty; **MEG** — magnetic rather than electrical, so **not subject to impedance** (the skull impedes charge but not magnetic flux), giving "substantially better spatial resolution compared to EEG."

#### The model zoo
- **DDM (Ratcliff 1978)** — "perhaps the most popular cognitive model that has stood the test of time," rooted in **signal detection theory**.
- Descendants and relatives: **Decision Field Theory** (Busemeyer & Townsend 1993, preferential choice); **Dynamic Signal Detection (DSD)** and **RT-CON** (choice + RT + confidence); **Leaky Competing Accumulator (LCA)** (Usher & McClelland 2001, more neurally inspired stochastic process); **Ballistic Accumulator / Linear Ballistic Accumulator (LBA)** (Brown & Heathcote 2005, 2008 — eliminates moment-to-moment variability).
- **Reinforcement learning** — temporal-difference models adjusting value by expected-vs-received reward difference.
- **Memory models**: REM, BCDMEM, SAM, TCM, **CMR** (Context Maintenance and Retrieval — the one most used in MbCN work), SIMPLE.
- **Cognitive architectures**: PDP, **ACT-R**.

### Ch 4: The Diffusion Model of Decision-Making (Philip L. Smith & Roger Ratcliff) — the core
- **Core idea**: decision-making is **inherently variable** — "People do not always make the same response to repeated presentation of the same stimulus and the time they take to respond to it varies." **Sequential-sampling models treat that variability as the empirical signature of a noisy evidence-accumulation process**, not as measurement error.
- **Intellectual lineage**: **psychophysics** (Fechner, 1860, just noticeable differences) and **reaction time** (Donders, 1868, inspired by Helmholtz on nerve conduction speed) converged on the same realization. Note the historical claim the authors make explicit: **the noisiness of decision processes was proposed on theoretical grounds "many decades before it was possible to use microelectrodes in awake, behaving animals to record this variability directly."**
- **Random walks first**: a random walk is the running cumulative sum $X_i = \sum_{j=1}^{i} Z_j$ of independent evidence samples taken every $\Delta$. Positive values favor $R_a$, negative $R_b$. Criteria $b < 0 < a$; the decision is the **first passage time** through a criterion; $T_D = \min\{T_a, T_b\}$; accuracy is the first-passage probability $P(C) = \text{Prob}\{T_a < T_b\}$. Earlier variants derived from **Wald's sequential probability ratio test (SPRT)** used log-likelihood ratios (Laming 1968); **relative judgment theory** (Link & Heath 1975) accumulated the raw samples instead.
- **The diffusion process** is the continuous-time limit of a random walk (sampling interval → 0 with variance held constant) — the **Wiener / Brownian motion** process, whose study began with Einstein on pollen particles. Ratcliff (1978) adopted it "in part because it seemed more natural to assume that the brain accumulates information **continuously** rather than at discrete time points."
- **Why the model is testable — the decisive methodological point**: sequential-sampling models "not only predict choice probabilities and mean RTs but also predict **entire distributions of RTs for correct responses and errors**. This provides for very rich contact between theory and experimental data, allowing for strong empirical tests." *Fitting a mean RT is nearly unconstraining; fitting the full joint distribution of choice and RT is not.*
- **The parameters — memorize these, they are the vocabulary of the field**:
  - **Drift rate ($\xi$, mean $\nu$)** — rate of evidence accumulation. **Stimulus identity sets its sign; stimulus discriminability sets its magnitude.** Higher drift → faster and more accurate.
  - **Boundary separation ($a$)** — distance between criteria; the **speed–accuracy tradeoff** parameter.
  - **Starting point ($z$)** — position between criteria; the **response bias** parameter.
  - **Non-decision time ($T_{er}$)** — encoding plus response production.
  - **Diffusion coefficient ($s$)** — infinitesimal SD, controlling sample-path noise. **Critically: model parameters are identified only up to a ratio, so $s$ is fixed arbitrarily** (Ratcliff uses 0.1; 1.0 is also used) and everything else is expressed in units of infinitesimal SD.
  - **Across-trial variability**: $\eta$ (drift, normal), $s_z$ (starting point, uniform), $s_t$ (non-decision time).
- **Sample paths** are "theoretical constructs used to explain the observed behavior" — in behavioral experiments **only the response and the RT are observable**.
- **The predicted RT distribution shape** is unimodal and **positively skewed**, and — the strong claim — "the shapes of the RT distributions it predicts are precisely those found in empirical data" across tasks from signal detection to lexical decision and recognition memory. Distributions for low-discriminability stimuli are approximately **scaled copies** of those for high discriminability. **Contrast**: accumulator/counter-class models predict distributions that become *more symmetrical* as discriminability falls, which "tend not to be found empirically" except under external deadlines.
- **The problem that forced the across-trial variability parameters — the book's best worked piece of theory-repair**:
  - Simple random-walk and basic diffusion models predict **equal mean RTs for correct responses and errors** (indeed, equal *entire distributions*). "These predictions almost never hold empirically."
  - The empirical pattern: **high discriminability + speed stressed → errors faster than correct**; **low discriminability + accuracy stressed → errors slower than correct**; some studies show a **crossover**.
  - The fix, and the mapping is exact and worth memorizing: **across-trial variability in drift rate → slow errors** (RT distributions become probability mixtures over drift values; low-drift trials give both high error rates and long RTs). **Across-trial variability in starting point → fast errors.** **Both together → crossover interactions.**
  - **Independent empirical support for drift variability**: the **double-pass procedure** (Ratcliff et al., 2018) presents the same stimuli on pairs of trials; agreement in accuracy is higher than independence would predict, implying genuine stimulus-to-stimulus variability in evidence quality.
- **Speed–accuracy tradeoff, mechanistically**: increasing $a$ (holding $z = a/2$) lengthens the distance to a criterion and reduces the chance of terminating at the wrong one — slower and more accurate. **Caveat the authors flag**: several studies find speed-vs-accuracy instructions also change **non-decision time and drift rates**, and "how these latter effects should be interpreted theoretically is a matter of ongoing debate." *Do not assume threshold-only.*
- **Two distinct kinds of bias — a distinction routinely collapsed**: manipulating **prior probability** (relative stimulus frequency) is well described by a **starting-point shift**. Manipulating **reward rate** shifts the starting point *and* biases how stimulus information is classified — captured by a **drift criterion**, a criterion on the stimulus information analogous to the signal-detection criterion, which makes the drift rates for the two stimuli unequal. "Both kinds of bias effects appear to operate in tasks with unequal reward rates."
- **Mathematics**: the process satisfies **Kolmogorov's backward equation** $-\partial f/\partial\tau = \tfrac{1}{2}s^2\,\partial^2 f/\partial y^2 + \xi\,\partial f/\partial y$ (used to derive RT distributions) and a forward equation in $t$ and $x$ (used for unconstrained accumulation and for the distribution of non-terminated processes — important for **response-signal / deadline** tasks). Equivalently, the **stochastic differential equation** $dX_t = \xi\,dt + s\,dW_t$ — "the change in evidence is the sum of a deterministic and a random part," with $dW_t$ the differential of a Wiener process (**white noise**: "a mathematical abstraction, which cannot be realized physically"). Written in differential rather than derivative form because sample paths are so irregular that $dX_t/dt$ is not well defined.
- **Closed forms**: upper-boundary response probability $P(\xi,a,z) = \dfrac{\exp(-2\xi a/s^2) - \exp(-2\xi z/s^2)}{\exp(-2\xi a/s^2) - 1}$, with an infinite-series expression for the cumulative first-passage distribution. Lower boundary: replace $\xi \to -\xi$ and $z \to a - z$. Alternatives when no series solution exists: **finite-state Markov chain** approximation (Diederich & Busemeyer 2003 — approximate the continuous process by a discrete birth–death process with a transition matrix whose criterion states are absorbing, set to 1.0) and **Monte Carlo simulation**.
- **Fitting — the practical guidance**:
  - **Typically 8–10 parameters.** A single-condition experiment with four discriminabilities needs 10: four drifts, $a$, $z$, $T_{er}$, $\eta$, $s_z$, $s_t$. With no bias evidence, pool across responses and assume $z = a/2$, saving one.
  - **Data reduction is the defense against the parameter count**: with $m$ conditions and six bins per distribution there are $11m$ degrees of freedom (one lost per correct–error pair). The worked case gives 44 df against 9 parameters — nearly **5:1**, with **>10:1 common** for larger datasets. "This represents a high degree of parsimony and explanatory power." *Cite the ratio, not the raw parameter count, when the model is accused of being overparameterized.*
  - **Fit statistic**: Pearson $\chi^2$ or likelihood-ratio $G^2$ over **12 bins** per condition (five quantiles → six bins per distribution, two distributions). **Because the statistic tracks probability mass across correct and error distributions, minimizing it fits RT and accuracy simultaneously.**
  - **Why minimum-$\chi^2$ over maximum likelihood, despite ML's theoretical appeal**: ML "is vulnerable to the effects of **contaminants or outliers**… Almost all data sets have a small proportion of contaminant responses in them, whether from finger errors or from lapses in vigilance." ML requires every RT to get nonzero likelihood, so outliers disrupt estimation. Alternative fix: model contaminants explicitly as a uniform mixture component with an estimated mixing parameter (Ratcliff & Tuerlinckx, 2002).
  - **Group vs. individual fits**: quantile-averaging across participants is less noisy, and studies find both approaches "lead to similar conclusions" — attributed to the relative invariance of the model's distribution shapes.
  - **Bayesian and hierarchical fitting**: $p(\theta|D) = p(D|\theta)p(\theta)/p(D)$, with the denominator requiring a multi-dimensional integral evaluated by **MCMC**. For moderate-to-large samples classical and Bayesian perform similarly; **for small samples (fewer than ~40 observations per condition per participant), hierarchical Bayes gives better parameter recovery** because population-level constraints stabilize the fits. **HDDM** is the standard package — with a named shortcoming: it "assumes the across-trial variability parameters are the same for all participants and uses this assumption to constrain the estimates of the other parameters. **This constraint can result in incorrect estimates under some circumstances.**"
- **Frontier**: extending diffusion models from discrete choice to **continuous outcome** decisions (selecting from a continuous range of actions, as in walking, cycling, driving).

### Ch 6: Spiking, Salience, and Saccades (Cox, Palmeri, Logan, Smith & Schall) — how to link a model to spikes
- **Core idea**: cognitive models sit at Marr's **algorithmic** level and can therefore **unify** the implementational ("how") and functional ("why") levels rather than merely sitting between them. "Computational cognitive models provide a common language for helping to understand a set of phenomena across all three levels simultaneously."
- **Dimensions of constraint — the argument for why RT matters, quantified.** Binary gaze outcome = 2 states, and "there is simply not much for the model to explain." Pixel coordinates = 1024×768 = 786,432 states. Choice × RT to the nearest ms over 2 s = 2 × 2000 = 4,000 states. **Fewer states than pixels, but far more useful**, because "the expansion of the behavioral state space by including response time makes it possible to distinguish between models of the final gaze position which would otherwise make identical predictions." RT is **dynamic** — accumulated variability in process durations manifests in the *shape* of the distribution.
- **And why even RT is not enough**: RT reflects cumulative variability "which, in turn, may be consistent with many possible trajectories of internal states. **Functional neurophysiology provides a glimpse of those trajectories.**"
- **Linking proposition** (Teller 1984; Schall 2004) — a hypothesized relationship between neural states and cognitive states. In SCRI: "the firing rate of an FEF visual neuron at a given time after the onset of the search array represented the **relative salience** of the object in that neuron's RF at that time." **Naming the linking proposition explicitly is what makes a neuro-cognitive model testable rather than merely suggestive.**
- **The FEF empirical base**: FEF receives from both dorsal and ventral streams, so "what" and "where" converge there. **Visual neurons** show a canonical **three-phase response**: (1) flat baseline; (2) increase if any object is in the RF, target or distractor; (3) higher activity for a target than a distractor. The phase-2→3 transition is **Target Selection Time (TST)**. Increasing **set size** or **target–distractor similarity** delays TST, reduces phase-3 activity, and shrinks the target–distractor difference. **Movement neurons** have movement fields, start low, and rise to a threshold that triggers a saccade — "consistent with FEF movement neurons acting like **evidence accumulators**."
- **The two-stage causal chain, and why it counts as evidence**: the **Gated Accumulator Model (GAM)** (Purcell et al., 2010, 2012) took *recorded* FEF visual-neuron spiking as the **input** to a network of accumulators; it fit observed saccade RT distributions closely, and GAM accumulator dynamics mirrored recorded FEF movement neurons. **SCRI** (Salience by Competitive and Recurrent Interactions; Cox et al., 2022) then *explains* the visual-neuron activity that GAM takes as input, such that SCRI-predicted activity can play the same evidentiary role as recorded activity. **The result is a model that explains millisecond-by-millisecond spiking and the behavioral RT distribution with one set of mechanisms — the standard this library holds neuro-cognitive models to.**

### Ch 9: Advancements in Joint Modeling (Turner, Bahg, Galdo & Liu)
- **The historical arc of linking**: **Teller (1984)** specified linking propositions **axiomatically**, using strict equality statements — "which are impossible to satisfy in the context of measurement noise." **Schall (2004)** replaced them with **statistical linking functions**, where probability distributions accommodate measurement uncertainty. **Forstmann et al. (2008, 2010)** concretized these by null-hypothesis testing between neural patterns and model parameters, then (2011) articulated **reciprocity** — latent model representations informing the analysis of both data streams.
- **Constraint, defined operationally**: "the range of predictions that a computational model can make, where the more constraint a model has, the **narrower** its predictions will be." Narrow predictions that match data = strong support (Roberts & Pashler 2000; Pitt & Myung 2002).
- **The historical analogy that motivates joint modeling — and it is a good one.** Models used to be evaluated against choice plus *mean or median* RT. Once they were "rigorously subjected to the **full distribution** of response times, **many models immediately fell out of favor because there existed no parameters that could capture all essential aspects of the data**." The claim: neural measures can impose the same kind of new constraint that full RT distributions once did.
- **Why not just do it in two stages?** You may — "perfectly suitable, especially when a researcher is interested in exploring potentially many hypotheses" — but "using this two-stage approach necessarily **compromises our ability to maximize the amount of constraint** on our computational theory."
- Development directions since the 1st edition: generic accessibility, **scalability** to high-dimensional data, constraints on temporal dynamics, and instantiating **causal** relations between neural dynamics and behavior.

### Ch 14: Cognitive Control of Choices and Actions (Heathcote, Verbruggen, Boehler & Matzke)
- **Core idea**: **Evidence-accumulation models (EAMs)** are the framework of choice for cognitive control because control "impacts the entire gamut of psychological processes from signal detection to action selection and action execution" and EAMs span that range. Architecture: **accumulators racing**, independently or interactively.
- **The Racing Diffusion (RD) model**, and the parameter→function mapping that makes EAMs useful for control research: each accumulator accrues noisy evidence at its own rate; first to threshold wins; RT = winning crossing time + non-decision time. **Rates** are the locus for **signal detection via selective attention (evidence quality) and effort (overall speed)**; the **encoding portion of non-decision time** likewise. **Thresholds** and the **response-production portion of non-decision time** are the loci for **action selection and action execution.** Single-accumulator finishing times follow a **shifted Wald** distribution.
- **The candid limitation**: "EAMs specify **what** can be controlled but not **how** control is achieved, although identifying **where** control is exerted can often be informative on this question."
- **Why model parameters are the right currency for linking**: unlike manifest behavioral quantities, parameters are latent and estimated indirectly. "Correspondences between latent parameters and manifest physiological measures create a **synergy**: the difficult (and in some cases impossible) task of inferring parameters is made easier by linking them to neuroscience measures, and the model aids in the interpretation of neuroscientific data that can otherwise be ambiguous."
- **The canonical MbCN result — Forstmann et al. (2008)**: cued speed-vs-accuracy on a random-dot motion task; **LBA** threshold changes captured the behavioral shift; fMRI showed **right anterior striatum** and **right pre-SMA** modulated by the instruction; and **across participants, the magnitude of the BOLD change correlated with the magnitude of the estimated LBA threshold change.** Consistent with a cortico-striatal loop involving the **subthalamic nucleus** (Frank 2006; Wiecki & Frank 2013) — which connects directly to the STN "global NoGo" account in [[reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro]].
- **The three-part critique of the two-stage procedure** (Turner et al., 2015) — apply this checklist to any parameter–BOLD correlation: (1) it **neglects trial-to-trial changes**; (2) it is **not statistically reciprocal** — neural data do not influence behavioral parameter estimation or vice versa; (3) it **fails to account for uncertainty**, which "attenuates correlation estimates and makes inference overly confident."
- **The Neural Drift Diffusion Model (NDDM)** as the response: an **ICA** model for fMRI plus a **Wiener diffusion** model for behavior, in a Bayesian hierarchical model with trial *and* participant levels, estimating per-trial drift rates and starting points and linking them to trial-by-trial BOLD via a **multivariate normal** covariance. **The scaling problem and its fix**: the MVN link "rapidly becomes impractical when investigating large brain networks because the required number of parameters is a **quadratic** function of the number of brain areas modelled" — replaced in Turner et al. (2017b) by a **factor analysis** link with only linear growth.
- **The conventional wisdom that joint modeling overturned**: that EAMs produce speed–accuracy tradeoffs "purely by threshold changes." More recent work with behavior alone also finds **non-decision time and accumulation rates** change.
- **LBA vs. DDM, precisely**: the LBA is a racing accumulator where **accumulation is deterministic with a constant rate** (straight trajectories). Decision noise lives not in moment-to-moment evidence but in **normally distributed trial-to-trial rate variation** (an assumption shared with the **LATER** model, Carpenter & Williams 1995) and **uniformly distributed starting-point noise**. In both LBA and DDM these variations are "mathematically integrated out, so that only estimates of the parameters of the overall distributions are obtained" — which is exactly what hierarchical joint modeling recovers.
- **Task coverage**: **conflict tasks** (automatic and executive processes in opposition), **delay discounting** (self-control for larger rewards), and **stop-signal / action inhibition**. Methods linked: fMRI, EEG amplitude and frequency, **TMS**, and **EMG**.

## Worked Example — reading an RT-and-accuracy pattern as a parameter change
The book's central diagnostic skill, reconstructed as a procedure:

1. **Get the full joint distribution**, not means. You need RT distributions for **correct responses and errors separately**, per condition. Means alone cannot distinguish the models below.
2. **Ask which experimental variable moved.** The model's constraint is that **discriminability manipulations affect drift rates**, while **instructions, payoffs, and prior probabilities affect criteria or starting points** — and criteria/starting points "are assumed to be independent of drift rates and to have the same average value for all stimuli in a block." *That independence assumption is the testable constraint; it is what makes the model falsifiable rather than merely flexible.*
3. **Read the error-vs-correct RT ordering as a variability signature**: errors *slower* than correct → drift-rate variability dominating (typical when discriminability is low and accuracy stressed). Errors *faster* → starting-point variability dominating (typical when discriminability is high and speed stressed). A **crossover** across conditions → both operating.
4. **Distinguish the two biases.** Frequency manipulation → starting point. Reward manipulation → starting point **and** drift criterion (unequal drifts). If you attribute a reward effect purely to starting point, you will mis-estimate drift.
5. **Do not assume a speed instruction moved only the threshold.** Check non-decision time and drift; the literature finds all three can move, and the interpretation is unsettled.
6. **Fit with minimum $\chi^2$ over quantile bins** unless you have explicitly modelled contaminants; report the **data-reduction ratio**, not just the parameter count. Use hierarchical Bayes if you have fewer than ~40 observations per condition per participant.
7. **Only then link to brain data** — and state which linking structure you used, because that determines the claim you may make (see the Decision Rules).

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **Classify the linking structure before assessing the claim.** Theoretical (conceptual constraint only) < two-stage correlation (no reciprocity, no trial-level information, uncertainty unaccounted) < latent-input model-based fMRI (confirmatory) < joint modeling (partial or full reciprocity) < integrative. **The inference you may draw is bounded by the structure you used.**
- **"Model-based fMRI" specifically means the latent-input approach** — model fit to behavior, trial-level internal quantity (e.g. prediction error) used as a GLM regressor. It is confirmatory by construction; it does not let brain data revise the cognitive model.
- **A two-stage parameter–BOLD correlation is attenuated and overconfident.** State this whenever you report or read one; three defects apply — no trial-level variation, no reciprocity, uncertainty ignored.
- **For accumulation-model inference, use choice and the RT distribution rather than relying on mean RT alone.** Means may describe a result but usually underconstrain the proposed mechanism; choose fitting summaries appropriate to the model and available data.
- **Map manipulations to parameters, then test the mapping**: discriminability → drift; instructions/payoffs/priors → boundary or starting point; and criteria must be independent of drift within a block. Violations of that independence are informative, not nuisances.
- **Errors slower than correct ⇒ across-trial drift variability; errors faster ⇒ across-trial starting-point variability; crossover ⇒ both.** A model without across-trial variability predicts *identical* correct and error distributions, which is empirically false.
- **Separate the two kinds of bias**: prior probability shifts the starting point; unequal reward additionally shifts the **drift criterion**, making the drift rates unequal.
- **Fix the diffusion coefficient.** Parameters are identified only up to a ratio; report which convention ($s = 0.1$ or $1.0$) you used, since parameter magnitudes are not comparable across conventions.
- **Defend parameter count with the data-reduction ratio** (df in the data ÷ free parameters); 5:1 is typical, >10:1 common for larger datasets.
- **Choose the estimator with contamination and recovery in view.** The source's minimum-$\chi^2$ recommendation addresses contaminant sensitivity in its fitting comparisons. Test lapse/error handling and parameter recovery for the chosen model rather than imposing one estimator across tasks.
- **Use hierarchical Bayes for small samples** (<~40 observations per condition per participant), and know HDDM's constraint: it assumes across-trial variability parameters are identical across participants, which can bias other estimates.
- **State the linking proposition explicitly** — the hypothesized correspondence between a neural quantity and a cognitive state. Without it, a neuro-cognitive model is suggestive rather than testable. Prefer **statistical** linking functions (Schall) over **axiomatic** equalities (Teller), which measurement noise makes unsatisfiable.
- **Expand the behavioral state space before adding parameters.** More measured dimensions (choice → choice+RT → +confidence → +neural trajectories) buy more constraint than more flexible models buy explanatory power.
- **Separate eye movements, recording equipment, and measurement mechanisms.** Eye and eyelid movements generate ocular electrical artifacts in EEG; fMRI has motion/image-artifact and task-related visual/oculomotor BOLD concerns. Eye tracking can provide behavioral model inputs and artifact checks with either modality. Verify compatibility for the actual apparatus rather than assuming blanket incompatibility or equipment scarcity.
- **Remember fMRI's lag is a design constraint, not just an analysis one** — the hemodynamic delay "requires careful consideration of the experimental design," especially for trial-level model-based regressors.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Cognition is latent; the model goes in the middle.** Parameters, not manifest variables, are the currency in which brain and behavior are compared.
2. **The linking structure determines the claim.** Theoretical → two-stage → latent-input → joint → integrative, in increasing order of constraint and decreasing order of convenience.
3. **The DDM explains choice and the full RT distribution from one noisy accumulation process**, with drift = evidence quality, boundary = speed–accuracy, starting point = bias, and non-decision time = everything else.
4. **Across-trial variability in drift and starting point is not a fudge** — it is what makes the model predict the observed correct/error RT orderings, and drift variability has independent support from the double-pass procedure.
5. **RT distributions are the constraint that killed a generation of models.** Neural data are the next such constraint, which is the argument for joint modeling.
6. **Speed–accuracy tradeoffs are not purely threshold effects** — non-decision time and drift move too, and the interpretation is unsettled.
7. **A linking proposition must be stated to be tested.** SCRI+GAM is the standard: one mechanism set explaining both millisecond spiking and behavioral RT distributions.
