## Mental Model (read first)
Working memory is a **mental workbench** for retaining goals and information needed by current processing. Competing streams and interruptions can make a task exceed the resources available in that situation, even when the person has learned how to perform it. Klingberg combines this demand–capacity account with an optimistic hypothesis: plasticity in shared working-memory systems may permit training to generalize. Preserve the hypothesis while testing transfer independently of practice gains, neural change, and subjective improvement.

**Use for:** interruptions, competing information, working-memory accounts, and evaluation of cognitive training. Combine [Choke](reference-beilock-choke.md) for pressure-dependent control, [How the Body Knows Its Mind](reference-beilock-body-knows-mind.md) for bodily/material support, and [Hernán & Robins](reference-hernan-robins-causal-inference.md) for intervention comparisons.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapters 1–3 — Identify the information that must remain available
**Locators:** “The Information Portal,” “Different Kinds of Attention”; “The Mental Workbench,” “Controlling Attention,” “Working Memory Versus Short-Term Memory.”

**Controlled attention** selects according to a goal; **stimulus-driven attention** can be captured by salient events. Maintaining an **attentional template** keeps the sought object or task relevant during competition. Losing that goal can produce an everyday omission even though the person knows what to do. These processes interact; stimulus-driven capture is not a comprehensive alternative model of working memory.

**Baddeley's components:** the phonological loop supports verbal information, the visuospatial sketch pad supports spatial/visual information, the central executive coordinates operations, and the episodic buffer integrates information. These are model commitments, not individually located boxes in the brain. A component label does not specify the operation that failed.

**Short-term versus working memory:** Klingberg disputes a clean boundary based solely on whether a test includes manipulation. High-load storage tasks and visuospatial tasks complicate that criterion. State the actual material, retention interval, interference, and response requirement instead of inferring a distinct faculty from a test's name.

**Capacity depends on measurement:** “the magical number seven” is a historical framing, not a universal interface limit. Chunking, learned representations, stimulus complexity, task rules, and response precision change performance. A list-span result is not a direct count of the number of workplace obligations a person can hold.

**Practical decomposition:** identify the goal, intermediate state, next operation, irrelevant input, and what must be reconstructed after switching. The total information available on the internet is less diagnostic than how much relevant state must be maintained or selected now.

### Chapters 4–5 — Neural models and limits of the workbench
**Locators:** “Models of Working Memory,” recurrent activity; “The Brain and the Magical Number Seven,” “Mechanisms of Capacity Limitation.”

The book emphasizes recurrent neural activity and distributed frontal/parietal involvement in maintaining information. Recurrent excitation and inhibition provide one candidate mechanism for stable representations. Increasing task load and increasing BOLD responses do not show that blood or oxygen supply is the normal capacity bottleneck, nor does a regional response reveal a fixed number of memory slots.

**Later qualification:** Rose and colleagues (2016) used TMS to reactivate decodability of a latent memory representation when it remained potentially relevant later in the trial. This supports alternatives to treating continuous decodable activity as necessary for every retained item. It does not establish absence of all neural activity, uniquely identify synaptic storage, or show that persistent activity never matters. [Primary study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aah7011).

Use [O'Reilly](reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro.md) or [Dayan & Abbott](reference-dayan-abbott-theoretical-neuroscience.md) for implemented dynamics, and [Craver](reference-craver-explaining-brain.md) to distinguish a possible mechanism from the actual organization. Do not translate the book's “online” metaphor into a claim that all working-memory contents are consciously experienced at every moment.

### Chapter 6 — Shared demands, distraction, and dual-task interference
**Locators:** “Simultaneous Capacity and Mental Bandwidth,” “The Cocktail Party Effect and Other Distractions,” “The Unifying Capacity Hypothesis.”

**Dual task versus distraction:** intentionally following two streams differs from trying to ignore one while doing another. Both can impair performance, but the required explanation differs. Working-memory load can weaken maintenance of a filtering goal, allowing irrelevant input to compete. Do not conflate this with the effects of perceptual load; increasing different kinds of load need not produce identical distraction patterns.

**Overlap hypothesis:** tasks interfere when their required processing systems overlap. The **unifying capacity hypothesis** links some limits of working memory, concurrent performance, and distractor resistance to shared frontal/parietal systems. The rival account posits additional coordination demands, described as a central executive. Klingberg favors overlap based on his task/imaging comparisons.

**Inference boundary:** spatial overlap does not itself show identical representations or prove a causal bottleneck. Failure to find a region activated only by dual-tasking does not prove coordination is absent. Compare timing, representational overlap, and response selection; use manipulations whose predictions differ. Distinct sensory channels do not guarantee independent processing resources.

**Author's explicit exception:** nearly simultaneous response demands and competing motor actions can interfere for reasons not reducible to working memory. Automatic components can coexist with another task more readily in some settings, but automaticity is operation-specific and does not mean an entire activity needs no attention. The source's categorical absence-of-frontal-activity criterion is not adopted.

### Chapters 6 and 15 — Diagnose and redesign the actual workflow
**Locators:** Linda's office examples; “The Information Flood and Flow,” “Infostress.”

An interruption can replace a maintained goal, introduce another unresolved task, and require reconstruction of the prior state. **Resumption cues**, checklists, and saved intermediate state are editorial applications of the workbench account; they are not a named protocol tested in the book.

| Intervention family | What it changes | Example | Evidence of success | What success does not show |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reduce environmental demands | Fewer competing events or unnecessary switches | Batch nonurgent notifications; separate a calculation from messages | Fewer omissions or faster accurate resumption during real work | Increased internal capacity |
| Provide external support | Store state or expose the next action outside memory | Visible intermediate values, a short checklist, a “next step” note | More reliable supported completion, with support costs counted | Unsupported retention or far transfer |
| Train the relevant task | Improve representations, procedures, retrieval, or coordination | Practice the actual workflow with feedback and realistic cases | Delayed success on new cases of that work | Domain-general working-memory expansion |
| Train putative capacity | Attempt to improve a shared cognitive operation | Adaptive memory exercises | Credible gains on independent untrained measures and relevant outcomes | That practiced-task gains alone establish transfer |

The categories can be combined. A tool may reduce memory demand yet introduce search, verification, or notification costs. Keep the support readable and accessible; assess whether it is used correctly. Some interruptions carry essential information, so suppressing all contact can create other errors. Count missed urgent events as well as gains in focused work.

**Comparison design:** define a primary outcome such as correct completed cases or omission rate, and measure time/resumption costs separately. Compare matched workloads with and without a change, counterbalance order where feasible, and account for practice, fatigue, demand variation, and carryover. For learning, include new cases and delayed testing. A small personal comparison can guide a local choice without establishing a general causal law.

### Chapters 8 and 10 — Plasticity motivates a training hypothesis
**Locators:** “Brain Plasticity,” “What Do We Mean by ‘Use’ and ‘It’?”; “A Cognitive Gym,” “RoboMemo,” “The Effects of Training on Brain Activity.”

**Use it or lose it requires specification:** Klingberg explicitly distinguishes low-intensity use from intensive practice and emphasizes that training affects particular functions. His proposal is that sufficiently demanding, repetitive, adaptive practice of shared working-memory processes might generalize more widely than practicing a narrow sensory skill.

**RoboMemo:** the book describes digit/letter and spatial-sequence exercises with changing stimuli, difficulty adapted to performance, and repeated sessions over weeks. It reports early ADHD and adult comparisons with easier training controls, gains on untrained working-memory tests, some reasoning outcomes, and reported everyday changes. This is the author's evidence and interpretation at the time, not a current product evaluation or a dose recommendation.

**Control quality matters:** easy nonadaptive tasks may differ from adaptive training in challenge, expectations, reward, engagement, and time actually spent. A nominally active control does not automatically equalize these. Parent ratings can have practical value but require attention to awareness of assignment and expectancy. Neural change may reflect strategy, effort, or efficiency as well as a capacity change.

**Transfer ladder:** (1) improvement on trained tasks; (2) near transfer to untrained but similar procedures; (3) far transfer to distinct abilities or practical outcomes; (4) persistence at a relevant follow-up. Define similarity by shared operations, stimuli, and strategies, not just whether a test has a different name or medium. Improvements in computer grids and physical block sequences may share spatial-sequence demands.

### Later evidence — Keep practice gains and generalization separate
These selected checks constrain the broad transfer claim; they do not exhaust the literature or settle every population and protocol.

- **Redick and colleagues (2013):** young adults practiced adaptive dual n-back or adaptive visual search, with a further no-contact group. Gains on the practiced tasks did not produce positive transfer on the assessed cognitive-ability tests. This is direct evidence against generalization for that training design. [Randomized primary study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22708717/).
- **Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme (2016):** their meta-analysis found no convincing broad far-transfer benefit against treated controls. It supplies wider context, while heterogeneity of interventions and controls still matters for individual claims. [Research synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616635612).
- **Zhong and colleagues (2025):** a preregistered double-blind trial compared adaptive visuospatial n-back training with active control at several doses, with a one-month follow-up. Longer training increased practiced-task gains, but the study found neither near nor far transfer on its assessed outcomes. More practice alone did not establish generalization. [Primary study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40914122/).

**Judgment:** general capacity or intelligence gains should not be promised from game scores. This does not erase genuine task learning, demonstrate that every near-transfer effect is absent, or prove that no future training design can transfer. Evaluate a specified program for a specified outcome and population. For workplace improvement, test demand reduction, support, and domain practice directly rather than assuming generic capacity training is the necessary first step.

### Chapters 7, 9, 11–15 — Broader claims with narrower warrant
**Locators:** “Wallace's Paradox,” “Does ADHD Exist?,” “The Everyday Exercising of Our Mental Muscles,” “Computer Games,” “The Flynn Effect,” “Neurocognitive Enhancement,” “Flow.”

**Development and intelligence:** correlations between span and reasoning motivate a shared-process model; they do not show that increasing one test score will cause an equivalent gain in intelligence. Population changes in IQ scores—the Flynn effect—do not identify a single cause such as media complexity or validate an individual training program. Evolutionary explanations here are historical hypotheses, not measured mechanisms of a modern workflow.

**ADHD:** the book links working-memory difficulties with some attentional symptoms. Situational overload in an office is not thereby ADHD, and a “relative attention deficit” is not a clinical diagnosis. Similar behavior can arise from sleep, anxiety, task design, or other causes. Treatment and medication decisions require current clinical evidence outside this source.

**Everyday activity and games:** distinguish correlations with lifestyle from randomized changes in activity. A game can improve practiced or closely related operations without improving school or work performance. Klingberg himself stresses that different games train different functions; “gaming” is not one intervention.

**Infostress and sense of control:** perceived demands depend on their meaning, predictability, and controllability as well as volume. Do not convert this into blaming the person for uncontrolled workloads. Distress does not demonstrate brain damage, and attractive challenge is not evidence of beneficial capacity training.

**Flow:** Csíkszentmihályi's high-skill/high-challenge account organizes experience of absorption. Klingberg proposes relating skill to capacity and challenge to information load. This is a heuristic analogy, not a calibrated equation prescribing seven items or a proven optimal training dose. Being absorbed, learning, and performing accurately are separable outcomes.

## Worked Example
**Reconstruction of “A Cognitive Gym”:** a participant becomes extremely good at remembering digits by grouping them with familiar running records, yet performs much less well when the material changes to letters. Long-term knowledge changes the effective chunks. The dramatic digit score does not establish a larger domain-general working-memory store.

**Editorial workplace extension:** an analyst who uses a visible checklist makes fewer omissions. Test three distinct claims: whether the checklist improves work while available; whether practicing with it produces retained skill after removal; whether improvement reaches unrelated tasks. The first can justify keeping the checklist even if the other two fail. Conversely, if a memory-game score improves but workplace omissions do not, the game has not demonstrated the outcome the employer sought. Count preparation, lookup, and verification time rather than scoring only the aided step.

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- Start with the maintained goal and competing information, not a global judgment of intelligence or attention span.
- Separate missing knowledge, temporary state, environmental load, and persistent difficulty; more than one can contribute.
- Match the intervention to the constraint: remove unnecessary demand, expose state, teach the task, or evaluate capacity training as a separate hypothesis.
- Require independent untrained outcomes, appropriate controls, and delayed measurement for transfer claims. Different apparatus does not guarantee far transfer.
- Treat shared neural activation and plasticity as explanatory leads, not proof that training will improve daily life.
- A support that works need not be removed to count as useful; label the resulting competence accurately as supported.

## Key Takeaways
1. Information demands interact with goals, interference, expertise, and temporary state.
2. Environmental redesign and external support can improve performance without enlarging memory capacity.
3. Training gains, near transfer, far transfer, and persistence need separate evidence.
4. Preserve Klingberg's testable hypothesis while correcting the confidence of broad training promises.
