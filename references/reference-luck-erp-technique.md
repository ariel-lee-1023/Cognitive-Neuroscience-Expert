## Mental Model (read first)
An ERP inference depends on the experiment and measurement chain that produced the waveform. Design a contrast that can answer a cognitive question, obtain clean signals with verified timing, then choose measurements that respect component overlap, noise, and latency variability. Filtering, averaging, subtraction, and artifact correction change what can be inferred; none automatically turns an ambiguous or contaminated signal into a pure cognitive component.

**Peaks are not components.** A scalp waveform sums contributions from multiple sources, and averaging combines trials or individuals whose timing can differ. The size and timing of a visible peak therefore do not uniquely identify the size and timing of a neural or cognitive process.

**Use for:** acquisition and experimental design, reference choice, averaging, baseline and overlap, artifacts, filtering, amplitude/latency measurement, statistics, localization assumptions, and timing validation. Use the existing [Kappenman–Luck component handbook](reference-kappenman-luck-erp-components.md) for the richer component inventory; load both for a component claim whose interpretation depends on acquisition or analysis choices.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapter 1 — ERPs and their neural origins
**Locator:** Ch 1, especially “Advantages and Disadvantages,” “The Neural Origins of ERPs,” and component overview, pp. 21–49.

An **event-related potential** is electrical activity time-locked to an event and typically extracted from ongoing EEG through averaging. Scalp ERPs primarily reflect synchronized postsynaptic activity in appropriately oriented populations; neural activity that cancels spatially, lacks sufficient synchrony, or is not event-locked can be invisible in the average. ERP silence does not establish neural silence.

An **equivalent current dipole** summarizes the field generated by a source configuration. Its orientation and the recording reference affect polarity. Positive and negative scalp voltage cannot be read directly as excitation and inhibition, and the electrode above an area does not exclusively record that area's activity.

The chief advantage for cognition is temporal sensitivity, but temporal precision of the recording does not solve component identification or determine when a cognitive operation begins. The voltage at one time can contain activity from several processes, and a grand average can conceal trial-to-trial variation. Source localization solves an underdetermined inverse problem only with additional constraints.

The chapter's component survey supplies orientation, not a lookup rule from a peak name to a unique function. Use task, latency range, scalp distribution, eliciting manipulation, and alternatives together. Detailed component-specific claims belong with the component handbook and their original studies.

### Chapter 2 — Design and interpretation before analysis
**Locator:** Ch 2, pp. 51–98; rules and strategies summarized on pp. 96–98.

The **Hillyard Principle** compares responses to the same physical stimuli under different psychological conditions. Physical differences, probability and sequential context, and preceding stimuli can themselves change ERPs. When they cannot be removed, use controls that assess their likely effects rather than assuming the differences are too small to matter.

Luck's eleven rules can be grouped into three practical checks:

1. **Interpretation:** distinguish peaks from latent components; a single waveform cannot reveal a latent component's full time course; compare difference effects with appropriate component evidence; amplitude and latency effects can masquerade as one another; an average need not preserve individual-trial or individual-person morphology.
2. **Design:** control physical and contextual stimulus differences; examine unequal trial counts; separate cognitive from motor-response presence or timing; intermix conditions within blocks when the manipulation permits, while accounting for the sequence context intermixing creates.
3. **Cognitive mapping:** do not assume component amplitude or latency is linearly, or even monotonically, related to the quality or timing of the cognitive process. Establish the mapping through evidence.

Seven source strategies remain useful: **focus on a specific component**, **use well-studied manipulations**, **focus on large components**, **isolate components with difference waves**, **choose easily isolated components**, **use component-independent designs**, and **borrow a useful component from another domain**. These are alternatives for making an inference tractable, not a requirement to force every experiment into a familiar component label.

#### Difference waves and component-independent designs
**Locator:** Ch 2, “Isolate Components with Difference Waves,” pp. 63–65, and subsequent design examples.

Subtracting a matched condition removes contributions common to both; what remains is the sum of contributions that differ. A difference wave is not inherently a single component. Multiple processes can respond to the same manipulation, and different factors can interact.

In the source's N400 design, compare semantically unrelated minus related target responses separately for two noun categories. This reduces shared activity and asks whether **semantic-relatedness modulation** differs by category. It can cancel an additive category effect that exists equally in both relatedness conditions. A null difference-of-differences therefore does not establish that the categories have identical raw N400 responses.

For independent averages with equal noise variance σ², subtraction gives noise variance `2σ²`, or standard deviation `√2 σ`. More generally `Var(A−B)=Var(A)+Var(B)−2Cov(A,B)`. The simple square-root rule needs its independence assumption.

A **component-independent design** can test a processing sequence or whether information has reached a stage without requiring a unique component-to-function identity. A lateralized difference can exploit symmetry to remove shared activity, but it still needs controls for sensory asymmetries, eye movements, and response-related processes.

### Chapter 3 — Basic principles of ERP recording
**Locator:** Ch 3, pp. 99–130; references pp. 101–111, impedance pp. 116–124, filtering/digitization pp. 124–130.

**Clean data at the source:** large systematic noise will survive averaging and may defeat postprocessing. Monitor the raw EEG, environmental noise, electrodes, participant state, and event codes during acquisition so problems can be corrected while recording. Artifact rejection after the session cannot recover unmeasured clean trials or remove every systematic contamination.

**Reference:** EEG channels measure voltage differences, not absolute voltage at a point: `Vrecorded=Vactive−Vreference`. There is no electrically neutral scalp reference. Reference activity can alter apparent waveform size and shape across every channel. Keep acquisition and analysis references documented; choose the montage for the contrast and comparability being sought. Average, linked-mastoid, and single-site references carry different assumptions and spatial biases.

**Rereferencing** is an algebraic transformation when the necessary channels were recorded and retained. It does not recover lost information or create a neutral site. Within-recording electrode differences can cancel a common reference, but absolute-looking amplitudes and maps remain tied to the montage. Do not confuse the recording reference with amplifier ground or protective earth.

**Common-mode rejection** suppresses signals shared by differential amplifier inputs. Electrode impedance, imbalance across sites, amplifier input properties, and environmental coupling affect it. High amplifier input impedance can mitigate one source of noise without eliminating skin potentials from sweating or electrode movement. The source's numeric impedance and equipment preferences are historical; they are not universal thresholds for every modern amplifier.

**Amplification and digitization:** avoid saturation and clipping, select sufficient resolution, and prevent aliasing before sampling. **Nyquist** requires an adequately band-limited signal below half the sampling frequency; a filter's nominal cutoff alone does not guarantee that energy above this frequency is sufficiently suppressed. Once high-frequency activity aliases into sampled data, subsequent digital low-pass filtering cannot reconstruct the original signal.

**Procedure:** specify the target time scale and bandwidth; choose a compatible acquisition configuration; record the montage and filter characteristics; test noise and clipping; verify actual event timing. Current electrical safety procedures and manufacturer specifications require current documentation, not this historical textbook alone.

### Chapter 4 — Averaging, artifact rejection, and artifact correction
**Locator:** Ch 4, pp. 131–174, especially latency variability pp. 135–140, overlap pp. 145–151, and artifacts pp. 151–173.

**Signal averaging** aligns epochs to an event and averages pointwise. If each epoch contains the same signal plus independent, zero-mean noise of variance σ², the mean noise variance is `σ²/N`; signal-to-noise ratio improves proportional to `√N` under those assumptions. Event-correlated artifacts are not random noise and can survive or become clearer after averaging. Habituation and changing attention also violate a fixed-signal assumption.

**Latency jitter** broadens an averaged component and reduces its peak even when single-trial component size is unchanged. A grand average similarly mixes participant timing and morphology. For a monophasic component captured by a sufficiently broad window, area can be less sensitive to shifts than the maximum; truncated windows and overlapping opposite-polarity components defeat that simplification.

**Baseline correction** subtracts the mean voltage in a chosen reference interval. If conditions differ in prestimulus activity, subtraction shifts the whole poststimulus waveform and may alter its apparent topography. A baseline is an assumption about the comparison, not a guarantee that the starting state was equal.

**Adjacent-response overlap** occurs when earlier events still contribute during a later epoch. Varying inter-event intervals can smear the overlap without removing its mean or preventing baseline contamination. Model the actual event sequence and check whether preceding responses differ between conditions. Longer intervals, broader jitter, or designs that estimate overlap can help, each with assumptions. An omitted-stimulus control can itself elicit an omission response, so it is not automatically a clean sample of preceding activity alone.

**Artifact rejection** removes affected trials; **artifact correction** estimates and subtracts or separates contamination. Both can introduce problems. Unequal rejection changes retained sample size and can select different behavioral states across conditions. Correction can remove neural signal, leave residual artifact, or rely on a faulty separation model. A smooth result is not sufficient validation.

Distinguish ocular activity, muscle activity, skin potentials, amplifier blocking, and environmental noise. Thresholds and detectors should match the artifact's shape; flat clipping near a maximum can require a plateau-sensitive rule such as the source's **X-within-Y-of-peak** procedure. Eye movement or blinking can also change sensory input and task performance: correcting the electrical field does not reverse those cognitive or perceptual consequences.

**Steady-state responses** summarize activity at stimulation frequencies and harmonics. Their convenient amplitude/phase representation does not preserve the same event-specific temporal interpretation as a transient ERP: activity at a given phase can combine responses to several preceding cycles.

**How:** define artifact and retained-trial rules before interpreting effects; inspect raw and corrected signals; report retained trials by condition and participant; inspect behavioral and event-timing differences; retain the distinction between noise reduction and changing which trials enter the comparison.

### Chapter 5 — Filtering as a temporal operation
**Locator:** Ch 5, pp. 175–224; impulse responses and temporal distortions, especially pp. 204–223.

A linear filter can be expressed as convolution, `y(t)=∫h(τ)x(t−τ)dτ`, where h is the **impulse response**. Its **frequency response** describes the same operation in frequency space. The cutoff, roll-off, phase behavior, and impulse-response duration all matter; listing only a passband does not specify the transformation.

**Low-pass filtering** smooths rapid fluctuations but spreads voltage in time and can change peak amplitude and timing. **High-pass filtering** suppresses slow activity but can create opposite-polarity lobes and apparent early components. Sharp transitions in frequency can produce ringing in time. These distortions can affect even components whose apparent frequency content lies inside the nominal passband because nearby larger components spread into their latency range.

**Causal filters** use current and past samples and can delay or otherwise distort the waveform. **Noncausal/zero-phase filters** use data on both sides of a time point; eliminating phase delay does not eliminate temporal smearing. Later activity can appear earlier, including before the true onset. A zero-phase filter therefore does not certify a neural-onset claim.

**How:** inspect the impulse response; apply the intended filter to a plausible waveform or calibration signal; compare amplitude, onset, duration, and surrounding lobes; test the measurement under defensible alternative settings. Keep boundary handling and the timing of filtering relative to epoching visible. Use the weakest filtering adequate for the scientific target, rather than choosing the prettiest plot.

The book does not supply one universally safe cutoff. Broad mean amplitude already averages nearby samples; extra smoothing may add little precision while importing neighboring activity. Acquisition anti-alias filtering and later display smoothing serve different purposes and should not be conflated.

### Chapter 6 — Plotting, measurement, and analysis
**Locator:** Ch 6, pp. 225–266; amplitude pp. 229–237, latency pp. 237–250, statistics pp. 250–264.

Show interpretable time and voltage scales, reference and polarity conventions, electrodes, and the experimental contrast. Grand averages are visual summaries; many inferential analyses use participant-level measurements. A maximum of the grand average is not the mean of individual maxima because peak extraction is nonlinear.

**Peak amplitude** is sensitive to noise, search-window length, overlap, and extreme-value selection. With fewer clean trials, noise can inflate the selected maximum without increasing underlying activity. Do not compare peaks from different noise levels as though the measurement had equal bias.

**Mean amplitude** averages voltage over an explicit interval. Under zero-mean noise and a fixed window, greater noise changes precision rather than systematically increasing the mean. It is often preferable to peak amplitude, but it remains sensitive to window choice, overlap, and whether the interval captures the target activity. Window choice independent of the tested effect is the integration safeguard: use prior evidence, a suitable independent contrast, or a transparently exploratory analysis instead of selecting the strongest apparent difference and testing it as if prespecified.

**Peak latency** inherits overlap and noise problems and is not a component's onset or duration. **Fractional area latency** finds the time dividing an appropriately defined component area into a chosen fraction. Fifty-percent area latency is a median-of-area measure, not an onset estimate. It depends on window boundaries, polarity handling, and component isolation; it can fail if the target area is near zero or opposite-polarity activity cancels.

**Fractional peak/onset measures** locate a threshold relative to an isolated waveform's amplitude. They can be useful for relative timing under the source's conditions, but amplitude changes, filtering, and noise influence threshold crossing. The first statistically significant sample is not automatically the first moment of processing.

**Jackknife latency estimation** forms leave-one-participant-out averages to estimate the uncertainty of a nonlinear grand-average timing contrast. Those averages are dependent; do not treat them as independent participants in an ordinary uncorrected test. The source points to specialized jackknife procedures rather than providing a license for arbitrary leave-one-out testing.

**Statistical inference:** define contrasts, measurement windows, electrodes, and the unit of analysis; account for repeated measures, multiplicity, and relevant ANOVA assumptions. A condition-by-electrode interaction can reflect a scale difference, not necessarily a distinct generator configuration. The source also rejects treating amplitude normalization followed by that interaction as a reliable test of distinct generators; normalization can introduce or conceal differences. Topographic comparisons require careful treatment of amplitude, overlap, and reference assumptions.

**Attribution boundary:** this first edition's discussion on p. 250 contains an informal interpretation of p=.06 as the probability that an effect is due to chance. That wording is not carried forward. A p-value is a tail probability of a statistic under a specified null model, not the probability the null is true or the effect arose by chance. This correction is an editorial statistical clarification, not a quotation or a claim that Luck used the corrected formulation. Replication, uncertainty, and appropriate design matter, but none constitutes a guarantee of truth.

### Chapter 7 — ERP localization
**Locator:** Ch 7, pp. 267–302, especially “Can We Really Localize ERPs?” and recommendations.

The **forward problem** predicts scalp fields from sources and a head/conductivity model. The **inverse problem** infers sources from fields and admits multiple solutions without added constraints. **Equivalent current dipole** methods and **distributed source** methods impose different source and regularization assumptions.

Good scalp fit is not sufficient evidence for source accuracy. Different sources can fit nearly equally well, and fitting noise more closely can worsen localization. Inspect plausible alternative solutions and sensitivity to constraints, starting points, head model, and component overlap. A selected source configuration should be reported with its assumptions rather than as the unique origin of the measured peak.

Isolating a cleaner component or incorporating independent anatomical evidence can help constrain the problem without removing all ambiguity. Many cognitive timing questions do not need localization; adding a source estimate is useful only when it changes the inference and its assumptions can be defended.

### Chapter 8 and appendix — Build and verify the measurement chain
**Locator:** Ch 8, pp. 303–332, especially digitization testing pp. 317–319 and stimulus timing pp. 322–324; appendix on electrical principles.

A functioning ERP setup combines acquisition, analysis, and stimulus-presentation systems. The source's shopping details, computer interfaces, and CRT-specific recommendations describe historical equipment. Preserve the verification logic; do not reuse its model choices, prices, or numerical amplifier specifications as current advice.

**Timing validation:** record a physical event signal through the acquisition system and compare it with stored event codes. For visual stimuli, a suitable light sensor can measure actual display onset; for auditory stimuli, a suitable microphone can measure acoustic onset. Test many events over realistic session durations to reveal rare errors and drift, not just one calibration pulse.

Distinguish **constant offset**, which can be measured and corrected when stable, from **jitter** and **drift**, which can broaden or systematically shift ERPs. Software timestamps and a requested presentation time do not prove the physical event occurred at that time. Check event identities and condition coding as well as latency.

The electrical appendix distinguishes voltage, current, resistance, and impedance. Its durable role is understanding the measurement chain and units. It is not a substitute for current device instructions, qualified laboratory procedures, or validated human-recording hardware.

## Worked Example — why serial-search averages can have a smaller peak
**Reconstruction:** Ch 4, the visual-search example and Figures 4.2–4.3, pp. 135–140.

In a pop-out search condition, changing set size need not strongly increase the variation in when the target is found. In the serial-search condition discussed by Luck, larger arrays allow a wider range of target-discovery times. Consequently, greater trial-to-trial P3 timing variation is a live prediction alongside changes in mean latency or amplitude.

Align each trial to stimulus onset and average. Equal-size single-trial deflections occurring at more variable times produce a broader and lower averaged peak. Thus a smaller grand-average peak does not uniquely imply weaker cognitive processing. Compare the timing distribution where measurement permits, inspect the width and area within a defensible window, consider response-locked versus stimulus-locked activity, and check overlap and retained trials.

The book's interpretation does not authorize assuming that all apparent amplitude effects are jitter. It shows an alternative mechanism of the measurement that must be discriminated from true amplitude change. Any response alignment or single-trial estimation adds its own assumptions and may shift other components; report those rather than declaring the ambiguity solved by a new plot.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- Start with the experimental contrast and component-identification problem, then choose recording and analysis settings that preserve it.
- Apply the Hillyard Principle where possible, including sequential context. Control motor timing and blockwise state differences when they could mimic the effect.
- Treat a difference wave as selective subtraction, not proof of a pure component. State which main effects it cancels and which interactions it tests.
- Record reference and montage. A common reference contribution and a change of montage can alter apparent component amplitude or topography.
- Prevent systematic noise during acquisition; monitor artifacts and verify real event timing over a realistic duration.
- Distinguish fewer trials, different trials, and noisier trials. Each can change inference through a different route.
- Inspect prestimulus differences and prior-event overlap before interpreting a poststimulus shift. Baseline subtraction can transfer an earlier difference to the whole epoch.
- Evaluate filters in time as well as frequency. Zero phase does not imply undistorted onset, and aggressive high-pass filtering can create early-looking effects.
- Prefer a justified mean-amplitude window when it answers the question; do not select maxima or windows from the same contrast without accounting for selection.
- Keep peak latency, fractional area latency, threshold crossing, and cognitive onset distinct. Use appropriate dependent-sample methods for jackknife estimates.
- Evaluate alternative source solutions and constraints; fit quality does not make the inverse problem unique.
- For current protocols, equipment, or second-edition content, identify the version gap and obtain the appropriate source instead of extrapolating from this edition.

## Key Takeaways
A valid ERP interpretation follows an accountable chain from task design through recording, timing, preprocessing, and measurement. The main traps are inference from a peak, hidden overlap and jitter, systematic artifacts, filter-induced timing, and data-selected measurements. The supplied first edition supports these durable principles while leaving later methodological developments and current hardware specifications unverified.
