## Mental Model (read first)
This is the library's **modelling-craft and explanation-epistemology** source. Its thesis is captured by one image: **the chess-knight argument**. Observing that a knight "defends the centre early and attacks the king in endgame" gives you almost no explanatory power. Knowing exactly *how the knight moves* lets you explain every use and predict future ones. **"When we, as neuroscientists, perform experiments on how a certain brain region is being used, we are in fact just observing how the brain might be using the same chess piece under different conditions."**

The book's discipline: an **explanatory** model embodies a theory whose parameters mean something, as opposed to a machine-learning model that merely approximates a function. Everything else follows — how to fit it, how to read its parameters, and how to choose a level of abstraction.

**Reach for this book when you need**: the anatomy of a model (features vs. free parameters vs. fit); the distinction between using a model as a *theory* and as a *measure*; Marr's levels stated carefully; the RL algorithm family in detail (TD, SARSA, Q-learning, eligibility traces, actor–critic, model-based); or ACT-R's declarative memory equations.

**Coverage note**: front-loads the Introduction (the epistemology), Reinforcement Learning, and Models of Long-Term Memory — its distinctive contributions. Compressed: Accumulator Models of Decision-Making — superseded here by [[reference-forstmann-model-based-cogneuro]]; Part II (Perceptrons and Feedforward Networks, Hebbian Learning and Autoassociators, Recurrent Networks) — overlaps [[reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro]], which treats the same material with more biological constraint, though Stocco adds backpropagation/CHL/Oja/Hopfield mechanics and the McCulloch–Pitts logic-gate construction. The author's own scope note: "much more focused on the systems-level neuroscience than on single neuron properties… much more on large-scale theories (RL, memory associators) than on biophysical models."

## Frameworks & Structure

### Introduction — what an explanatory model is
- **A model** is "an abstract, simplified representation of a complex system" that "explicitly captures its internal workings into a set of formal equations or computational processing steps." Formally, a function connecting conditions to outcomes: $X \to f(X) \to Y$.
- **Explanation vs. prediction — and the tension between them**: both matter, and they are "intertwined… also separate." The candid observation: "because a model, by its very nature, produces an output every time it is run, **it is always making a prediction**" — so the distinction often reduces to whether the prediction concerns past or future data, seen or unseen data (**including past unseen data**).
- **The chess-knight argument** (the book's central image, above). The failure mode it names: if all you can say is that a region "is used at the beginning and the end of a game," you have "not much knowledge than what we started with." **Function-talk without computation-talk is not explanation.**
- **Breiman's two traditions**, applied to cognitive neuroscience:
  - **Data modeling** — top-down: start with assumptions about the process generating the data ("independent and normally distributed"), derive predictions.
  - **Algorithmic modeling** — agnostic about $f$; use tools that approximate it from the data's own constraints. "Nowadays, this approach is commonly known as **machine learning**."
  - **The same technique can serve either.** Linear regression tests a relationship (data modeling) *or* approximates an unknown function (LASSO, machine learning). Neural networks can be a structural model of the brain *or* a universal function approximator.
  - **In Computational Psychiatry the same split is called "explanatory models" vs. "machine learning"** — connect this directly to [[reference-series-computational-psychiatry]]. (In old symbolic AI: "neats" and "scruffies.")
- **The anatomy of any model — three parts, and the distinction that matters most**:
  - **Output** — what the model produces; **fit** is the degree to which it matches data.
  - **Features** — quantities capturing the outside world. "In choosing the appropriate features, the designer of a model implicitly defines the **levels of abstraction and the degree of simplification** they want to impose on the world." Features are in principle *measurable* — with the noted exception of deep-learning models that extract their own.
  - **Free parameters** — quantities that **cannot be measured independently of the equation itself**, mediating the effect of features on output.
  - **The defining property of explanatory models**: "because they embody a theory, **it is somewhat clear what their parameters represent**." Worked on Fitts's law $T = a + b\log_2(2D/W)$: $a$ is the minimum time to initiate any movement; $b$ is the **effort** of controlling it.
- **Fitting** = minimizing a **loss function** (e.g. sum of squares) over parameter values. Three routes, in increasing practicality: **closed form** (linear regression, and Fitts's law once you treat $\log_2(2D/W)$ as $x$); **grid search** — rejected as "rarely used in practice," both because sampling is infeasible for complex models and because discretization makes the true optimum literally invisible ("the grid search examined cases in which $a = 1.44$ and $a = 1.45$, but never examined the case in which $a = 1.4438$"); and **optimization/minimization algorithms** (e.g. **Nelder–Mead**), which exploit the smoothness of the loss surface to converge while exploring only a small portion of parameter space.
- **A subtle point worth carrying**: fitting Fitts's law by regression required *changing the model's features* — "Fitts' model sees the world as made of distances and widths, but the linear regression model sees only a single value $x$. **This is another case in which you need to transform the data to fit it into the model's worldview.**"
- **Models as theories vs. models as measures — a distinction "easily confused… but substantially different"**:
  - **As theory**: the researcher is interested in the model itself; fitting provides "a quantitative measure of how good of a theory the model is." This is how Fitts's law was conceived — derived theoretically (from information theory; "the use of logarithms in base 2 is a dead giveaway"), with data used to corroborate.
  - **As measure**: the researcher **assumes the model is correct** and is interested in its parameter values. Fitts's *Law* is now a de facto assumption in human–computer interaction. Worked example: comparing a mouse to a touchscreen, you might find $a$ smaller for touch (faster to point a finger than grab a mouse) but $b$ smaller for the mouse (covers distance more easily) — concluding that touch suits small screens and mice large ones.
  - **Why this matters for cognitive neuroscience**: "An entire class of explanatory models described herein, that of **accumulator models**, was designed precisely as way to **measure unobserved but conceptually clear processes from the distribution of response times**." Parameters also index individual differences.
- **Marr's three levels — with Stocco's terminological correction**:
  - **Functional level** — the general structure of the problem and its optimal solution (a Bayesian account of when memories are retrieved). **Note: "In Marr's book, this level is actually called 'computational'. This is often confusing, since all of the other models are also computational in the common sense of the word. So I prefer to use 'functional' here."** *Use "functional" and say why.*
  - **Algorithmic level** — the model's elements, representation, and step-by-step operation (memory as radioactive-style decay).
  - **Implementation level** — the physical processes (modelling hippocampal CA3 neuron interactions).
  - **The honest caveat**: "There is no right or wrong levels… the levels are not so clear-cut. For example, is Fitts' Law a model that exists at the functional or at the implementation level? And, finally, **each of these approaches still remains an abstraction of the original phenomenon—even if you dig deep into the implementation level.**"
- **Symbolic vs. connectionist**: symbolic models explicitly represent concepts and relations (a fact stored as `[(Object: Canary), (Type: Bird), (Property: Yellow)]`) — "elegant theories, but they tend to overlook the nitty-gritty details of how networks of neurons carry out the computations." Connectionist models start from how neuronal networks represent and compute. Stocco's position: "there is no unique, correct answer as to which one is the best—it largely depends on what one sets out to achieve."
- **Framing of the whole enterprise**: each framework "evolved from an original simple question ('How should one learn from reward?', 'How should one form memories?', 'What is the best way to recognize objects?'). So, **none of these frameworks really answers the question, "How does the brain work?"** However, all of them provide *partial* answers."

### Part I, Ch 1: Reinforcement Learning — the algorithm family, with the biology attached
- **Core split — learning vs. acting.** "In RL, the two concepts are kept distinct." The value equations estimate future reward whether the agent chooses randomly or optimally; a random agent "will collect less rewards over time and learn more slowly, but it will, nonetheless, still learn the best course of action." **"Conceptually, the agent can behave poorly even if it knows better."**
- **Policy** — a probability distribution over actions. A good policy balances **exploration vs. exploitation**. Two standard forms:
  - **ε-greedy**: with probability $1-\varepsilon$ take the highest-$Q$ action; otherwise pick at random among the rest.
  - **Boltzmann/softmax** (called "somewhat more common and more elegant"): $P(a_i) = e^{Q(s,a_i)/\tau} / \sum_j e^{Q(s,a_j)/\tau}$, where **$\tau$ is the decision temperature**. As $\tau \to 0$ the agent is greedy and deterministic; as $\tau \to \infty$ it chooses at random. *$\tau$ is the parameter most often fitted to individual subjects as an "exploration" trait — know that it is a policy parameter, not a learning parameter.*
- **The value-learning ladder**:
  - **TD-learning on states**: $V_{new}(s_t) \leftarrow V_{old}(s_t) + \alpha\delta_t$, with $\delta_t$ the reward prediction error. The **V-table** stores state values.
  - **SARSA** on actions: $Q_{new}(s_t,a_t) \leftarrow Q_{old}(s_t,a_t) + \alpha[r_t + \gamma Q(s_{t+1},a_{t+1}) - Q_{old}(s_t,a_t)]$ — named for the mnemonic $s, a, r, s, a$. Updates as soon as the next state is perceived and the next action decided.
  - **Q-learning**: $Q_{new}(s_t,a_t) \leftarrow Q_{old}(s_t,a_t) + \alpha[r_t + \gamma \max_a Q(s_{t+1},a) - Q_{old}(s_t,a_t)]$.
- **The SARSA/Q-learning distinction, and why it is not a technicality.** "The key difference… is the action that, at the new state $s_{t+1}$, is chosen as the **yardstick**." SARSA uses the action the agent has actually decided to take → **on-policy**. Q-learning uses the best available action **"whether the agent takes it or not"** → **off-policy**.
  - **Why SARSA does not converge on true $Q$ values, stated crisply**: in TD-learning you estimate quantities (state values) "that do not affect, *per se*, how state transitions happen. But, in SARSA, the agent is actually trying to estimate the value of **actions**, and actions **do** affect how the environment changes states."
  - **The worked demonstration** — a maze with a "pit" (reward $-10$) adjacent to the path to the cheese, both agents on ε-greedy with $\varepsilon = 0.1$: **SARSA learns a longer, safer path** because it accounts for the non-zero chance of accidentally stepping into the pit under its own policy. **Q-learning is insensitive to this risk and learns the shortest path even though the agent will sometimes fall in.** *This is the clearest available intuition for on- vs. off-policy, and the reason to suspect biological agents use something SARSA-like: it "does take the agent's actual behavior into account and avoids overexposing to potentially choosing bad actions."*
- **Non-Markov environments and the credit-assignment gap**: plain TD "cannot be applied to non-Markov environments… the back-propagation of the error term would not work properly, and actions would not get proper credit for later rewards." Two fixes:
  - **Multiple-step backups: TD(n), Q(n)** — widen the update window beyond $t+1$.
  - **Eligibility traces** — give the agent an internal memory: $e(s) \leftarrow \lambda e(s) + 1$ if $s$ is the current state, $\lambda e(s)$ otherwise. **"Eligibility traces decay over time but increase with re-use, roughly capturing the role of recency and frequency in memory."**
  - **TD(λ)**: $V_{new}(s) \leftarrow V_{old}(s) + \alpha\delta_t e(s)$ for **all** states — "the prediction error is propagated back in time to *all* states **in proportion to their distance in time**." TD(λ) is a generalization: $\lambda = 0$ reduces to one-step TD; $\lambda = 1$ approximates TD($n \to \infty$).
  - **SARSA(λ)** is a trivial extension ($e(s,a)$ replaces $e(s)$). **Q(λ) is not**, and the reason is instructive: because Q-learning's backup uses the best action, which may not be executed, "if the best action is not chosen, how should its eligibility trace be updated?" Watkins & Dayan's fix — follow SARSA(λ) while the chosen action *is* the optimal one, and **reset all traces to zero whenever the policy deviates from optimal**, which collapses that trial to plain Q-learning.
- **Actor-Critic**: the actor represents the policy; the critic estimates state values, action values, or both, depending on the algorithm. Value-derived signals guide policy changes, but value estimation and policy optimization remain distinct updates.
  - **Advantage-based actor-critic**: for a policy $\pi$, $A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s)$ measures the expected return from action $a$ relative to following $\pi$ at state $s$; positive advantage means better than that policy baseline. In a one-step on-policy variant, $\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})-V_\phi(s_t)$ can estimate advantage. With the exact $V^\pi$ and the matching Markov reward/transition model, $\mathbb E[\delta_t\mid s_t,a_t]=A^\pi(s_t,a_t)$; an approximate critic can introduce bias. Set the bootstrap value to zero at a true terminal state. A stochastic policy can ascend $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta\hat A_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$, while a tabular critic separately updates $V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha\delta_t$. Here $\gamma$ discounts future reward, $\phi$ and $\theta$ parameterize critic and actor, $\hat A_t$ is the chosen advantage estimate, and $\eta,\alpha>0$ are step sizes. A cumulative TD-driven state-action preference update is not, by itself, an update that estimates the defined advantage function. Actual A2C implementations may use multi-step returns and shared networks. This is an editorial mathematical clarification using [standard policy-gradient definitions](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/spinningup/rl_intro3.html#other-forms-of-the-policy-gradient), not a verified transcription of Stocco's algorithm.
  - **The biological mapping**: the source relates ventral striatum / nucleus accumbens and dorsal striatum to actor-critic roles (Barto 1995; Takahashi, Schoenbaum & Niv 2008). Treat this as a source-specific computational analogy: anatomy alone does not identify an advantage estimator or policy-gradient implementation. Cross-reference the Go/NoGo discussion in [[reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro]].
- **Model-free vs. model-based RL — the distinction and its behavioral signature**:
  - Everything above is **model-free**: the agent needs no internal model of the environment. Cheap in computation and memory, and "a nice framework to explain the function of those brain circuits that, through repeated rewards, **form habits and stimulus-response associations**, such as the basal ganglia."
  - **The limitation, illustrated concretely**: a mouse that is no longer hungry "would find itself drawn to the cheese location, whether it has any desire to eat cheese or not. And, if the mouse is **thirsty**, its previously learned Q-values would be essentially useless." **Model-free RL is habitual and therefore inflexible — this is the mechanism behind outcome-devaluation insensitivity.**
  - **Model-based RL** requires representing the environment's **state-transition function** $P(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$ — in the discrete case an **S-table** mapping state+action to next state and reward. Supports **one-step Q-planning** and **Dyna-Q**, and connects to Tolman's **cognitive maps**.
  - Stocco frames model-based RL two ways: **as declarative memory** and **as planning** — the bridge to the memory chapter and to the symbolic tradition.

### Part I, Ch 3: Models of Long-Term Memory — rational analysis and ACT-R
- **The framing move**: just as RL solves "maximize future reward," declarative memory frameworks treat memory as "an adaptive mechanism to solve the problem of **optimally retrieving information, given the statistics of the environment**." Anderson & Milson likened it to predicting **book rentals in a public library** — an information-retrieval and statistical-inference problem.
- **Rational analysis**: treat the agent as an **ideal observer** with limitless access and no retrieval cost. "This is, obviously, a grand simplification, but it offers some advantages… it becomes possible to formalize memory as a problem about the **statistics of the environment**, rather than a problem of the mechanics of the agents."
- **What memory is *for*, in this framework**: the squirrel needs the nut's location when hungry and the hawk's hunting ground when caching. "**The availability of a memory therefore should adaptively reflect its probability of being needed in a certain context.**"
- **The Bayesian decomposition** — posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood odds:
  $$\frac{P(m|Q)}{P(\neg m|Q)} = \frac{P(m)}{P(\neg m)} \prod_q \frac{P(q|m)}{P(q)}$$
  (the last step approximating $P(q|\neg m) \approx P(q)$ for large numbers of memories). **The clean interpretation: the prior odds capture the memory's history of use; the likelihood captures the effect of contextual cues at retrieval.**
- **The shape of forgetting — a case study in why functional form matters.** Ebbinghaus (1885) measured **percent savings** (relative re-study effort) across intervals from 15 minutes to a month. Is decay **exponential** ($P = ab^t$) or **power** ($P = at^{-b}$)?
  - **"This question might seem idle, but is not."** Exponentials have a **fixed half-life** (like radioactive decay); power functions have an ever-changing pace but are **scale-invariant**.
  - **The plotting diagnostic**: for exponential decay $P(t)=ab^t$ with $a>0$ and $0<b<1$, $\log P=\log a+t\log b$. Plot logarithmic $P$ on the vertical axis against linear time on the horizontal axis: the line has slope $\log b$. Logging time alone does not linearize an exponential. For a power law $P(t)=at^{-b}$ with $a,b>0$ and $t>0$, $\log P=\log a-b\log t$, so a log-log plot is linear. Logarithms require positive values. The source describes Ebbinghaus's log-log data as nearly linear; that is a source-specific illustration, not sufficient evidence that all forgetting follows a power law. Fit candidate functions under a stated observation-noise model, because transforming observations changes the error structure.
  - **The rationality test that closes the loop** (Anderson & Schooler 1991): they monitored three environmental information sources for 100 days — New York Times headlines, child-directed speech (CHILDES), and senders in Anderson's email inbox. In every case the relationship between elapsed time and probability of re-occurrence followed a **power function** — "Not only all three sources of information follow the same law: **their very own parameters are similar to each other!**" *Memory's decay function mirrors the environment's statistics. This is the strongest form of a rational-analysis argument.*
- **The ACT-R declarative memory model** (noted as sharing near-identical assumptions with **REM** and **MINERVA 2**):
  - Each encoding creates a **trace**; trace $i$ created at $t_i$ has retrieval odds decaying as $(t - t_i)^{-d}$, where **$d$ is the decay rate**.
  - Traces contribute **additively** to the memory's odds.
  - **Activation** is the **log odds** of retrieval: $A(m) = \log\frac{P(m)}{P(\neg m)}$, hence $A(m,t) = \log\sum_i (t-t_i)^{-d}$.
  - **Why log odds rather than probability or odds** — a genuinely useful piece of craft: activation "span[s] the entire domain of real numbers," and the point where retrieval and forgetting are equally likely ($P = 0.5$, odds $= 1$) becomes exactly $A = 0$. **So activation has a meaningful zero — the forgetting threshold.** Positive activation → more likely remembered than not; negative → more likely forgotten.
  - **Recency** falls out of the power-law decay; **frequency** falls out of the summation over traces.
- **The spacing effect as a model-failure-and-repair — the chapter's best worked example**:
  - **The model as stated cannot produce it**, and Stocco shows *why* rather than asserting it: trace effects are purely additive, so a longer gap means the first trace was created **earlier** and has decayed **more**, predicting *lower* activation — "exactly the opposite of what is experimentally found."
  - **Pavlik & Anderson's repair**: relax the constraint that $d$ is constant. Give each trace its own decay $d_i$ set at encoding by the memory's activation *at that moment*: $d_i = c\,e^{A(m,t=t_i)} + a$. **When two traces are close together, activation at the second encoding is high, so $d_i$ is large and that trace decays fast — producing the spacing effect.**
  - **The parsimony point**: "even when allowing for different traces to decay at different rates, decay is still determined by a **single parameter**, $a$."
  - **Validation beyond fit**: the model has been used to derive **optimal practice schedules**, and $a$ functions as an **idiographic** (person-specific) parameter that is "stable and reliable… within the same individual across sessions and materials," predicting real-life outcomes such as test performance. *A parameter that is stable within person and predicts out of sample is the strongest evidence an explanatory model can offer.*

## Worked Example — deciding whether a model is doing explanatory work
Stocco's implicit test, made explicit:

1. **Can you state the model's features and free parameters separately?** Features are measurable properties of the world; free parameters "cannot be measured independently of the equation itself." If you cannot separate them, you have a curve fit.
2. **Does each free parameter have a clear interpretation?** In Fitts's law, $a$ = movement initiation time, $b$ = effort. In the DDM, drift = evidence quality, boundary = caution. **If a parameter has no interpretation, the model is algorithmic (ML), not explanatory — which is fine, but changes what you may conclude from it.**
3. **Are you using the model as a theory or as a measure?** As a *theory*, fit quality is the evidence and a poor fit is informative. As a *measure*, you assume correctness and the *parameter values* are the result. **Conflating the two lets a bad fit pass unremarked while its parameters get interpreted.**
4. **Which level are you at, and does the phenomenon need another?** Functional (what is the optimal solution?), algorithmic (what steps?), implementational (what physical process?). Say which, and accept that all three are abstractions.
5. **Does the model fail anywhere informative?** ACT-R's additive traces *cannot* produce the spacing effect — and the diagnosis of *why* pointed directly at the repair (trace-specific decay set by activation at encoding). **A model whose failures are diagnosable is doing more work than one that fits everything.**
6. **Does anything survive out of sample?** Environmental statistics matching the forgetting function (Anderson & Schooler); a decay parameter stable within person across materials and predictive of test performance. **Parameters that transfer are the payoff of explanatory modelling.**

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **Demand computations, not just functions.** "Region X is engaged during task Y" is knight-observation. Ask what rule generates the observed uses.
- **Classify the model before interpreting it**: data modelling / explanatory (assumptions about the generating process, interpretable parameters) vs. algorithmic / machine learning (approximate $f$ from data). The same technique — regression, neural nets — can be either.
- **Separate features from free parameters, and say which abstraction level the feature choice commits you to.** Choosing features *is* choosing the level of simplification.
- **State whether you are using the model as a theory or as a measure.** Only the first treats fit as evidence; only the second licenses reading parameters as measurements — and it presupposes the model is right.
- **Choose a search method for the parameter space.** Continuous optimization often scales better; small grids can be useful for bounded comparisons or diagnostics. Check resolution, convergence, and alternative optima rather than imposing one optimizer.
- **Expect to transform data into the model's worldview** — and note the transformation, because it changes what the fitted parameters mean.
- **Explain the terminology when it matters.** Stocco calls Marr's top level "functional" because computation occurs at several levels. Preserve the distinction without requiring the user to adopt that vocabulary.
- **Distinguish learning from acting.** Value learning proceeds under any policy; the policy determines what gets sampled and therefore, for on-policy methods, what gets learned.
- **On-policy (SARSA) vs. off-policy (Q-learning) is a substantive commitment, not a numerical detail.** On-policy learns the value of behaving as you actually behave — it learns to avoid risk near cliffs; off-policy learns the optimal path regardless. If your subjects behave cautiously near costly errors, an off-policy model may misfit for a principled reason.
- **Use eligibility traces when credit must cross a temporal gap** (non-Markov structure), and remember they "decay over time but increase with re-use" — i.e. they implement recency and frequency.
- **Model-free RL predicts habitual, outcome-insensitive behavior**; if your task involves devaluation, satiety, or goal change, a model-free account predicts the wrong thing, and a state-transition model is required.
- **Fit the softmax temperature $\tau$ as a policy parameter, not a learning-rate**; confusing the two mislocates individual differences between exploration and learning.
- **Choose axes for the proposed functional form.** Exponential decay is linear in log response versus linear time; a power law is linear in log response versus log time, for positive response and time. Use plots to inspect candidates, then compare fits under a justified noise model; visual straightness alone does not select a mechanism.
- **In the source's activation-based memory model, express availability as log odds** so zero has the model's defined interpretation. Do not impose that representation on every memory model.
- **When a model cannot produce a known effect, diagnose the structural reason before adding parameters.** ACT-R's spacing failure was traced to additivity, and the fix added one interpretable mechanism rather than free parameters.
- **Weight out-of-sample and cross-domain validation over fit quality**: environmental statistics matching the model's law, and person-specific parameters that are stable across materials and predict real outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Explanation requires the rule of movement, not the catalogue of uses.** The chess-knight argument is the cleanest statement in this library of what is wrong with pure activation-mapping.
2. **Explanatory models have interpretable parameters; machine-learning models need not.** Know which you are running before you interpret anything.
3. **Models-as-theories and models-as-measures are different activities** with different evidential standards.
4. **RL is a family, and the family members make different psychological commitments**: on-policy vs. off-policy, one-step vs. eligibility traces, model-free (habitual, devaluation-insensitive) vs. model-based (flexible, requires a transition model).
5. **A2C maps onto ventral/dorsal striatum** — the critic/actor division is not a metaphor imposed after the fact.
6. **Memory's power-law forgetting mirrors the statistics of the environment** across three unrelated corpora — the strongest rational-analysis result in the book.
7. **Diagnosable failure beats universal fit.** ACT-R's inability to produce the spacing effect, and the single-parameter repair, is the model of how to revise a theory.
