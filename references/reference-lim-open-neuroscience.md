## Mental Model (read first)
This book's distinctive value in the library is **methodological**: it is the source that asks, for every technique, *what question does this actually answer, at what spatial and temporal resolution, and at what cost.* Its governing discipline is the **resolution/control/generalizability trade-off** — every step you take toward better experimental control moves you further from the intact behaving organism and weakens your ability to generalize. It is also the library's only source that systematically **names the myths and states the evidence refuting them**.

**Reach for this book when you need**: the right method for a research question; the resolution limits of a technique; the difference between an experiment, a quasi-experiment, and a case study and what each licenses; sampling and population validity concerns; or a clean refutation of a popular neuro-myth.

**Coverage note**: this distillation front-loads Ch. 6 (Methods) and Ch. 1 (epistemics and research design) — the chapters that give this source a role no other book in the library fills. Compressed to essentials: Ch. 13 (Learning and Memory), Ch. 14 (Lateralization and Language), Ch. 15 (Emotion), Ch. 11 (Neuropharmacology), Ch. 12 (Sleep) — covered at greater mechanistic depth by [[reference-byrne-neuroscience-online]] and [[reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro]]. Dropped with reason: Ch. 2–5 (gross and cellular anatomy, electrical properties, synaptic signaling) — standard foundations covered more deeply elsewhere in this library; Ch. 7–10 (sensory systems, motor system) — undergraduate-level survey of material treated at mechanism depth by Byrne et al.; Ch. 16 (Diseases of the Brain) — clinical survey outside the higher-cognition brief.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Ch 6: Methods of Neuroscience — the core contribution

#### The two resolutions (the frame everything else hangs on)
- **Spatial resolution** — the ability to differentiate two points *in space*. Measured in distance or volume. Highest: **electron microscopy** (nanometers).
- **Temporal resolution** — the ability to distinguish two events *in time*. Measured in time. Highest: **electrophysiology** (hundreds of microseconds).
- **The critical failure mode of low temporal resolution**: two discrete events separated in time **appear as one event**. When you read a slow-method result, ask what has been smeared together.
- **Anti-pattern** — reporting a result at a resolution the method cannot support. Every technique below is characterized by *what question it answers* first, resolution second.

#### Experimental preparations — the control/generalizability axis
Stated as a single ordered trade-off, and worth internalizing as a gradient rather than three boxes:

| | **in vivo** ("within life") | **ex vivo** | **in vitro** ("within glass") |
|---|---|---|---|
| What | intact living organism | a section of a living organism (brain slice, biopsy, detached frog leg) | cultured cells or isolated DNA/RNA/protein |
| Control over variables | lowest — "thousands of variables… uncontrolled or still unknown" | intermediate | highest |
| Predicts human therapeutic outcome | best | intermediate | worst |
| Ethical regulation | strictest | intermediate | most lax — and the guidelines mostly **protect the experimenter, not the subject** |

- **The governing sentence, to apply to any result**: *"each step you move away from the awake and behaving animal, the more difficult it becomes to draw inferences… What we gain in control over the experimental preparation, we lose in ability to generalize about data."*
- **in silico** — computational modeling, named as a fourth preparation.

#### Human vs. non-human subjects — the case on both sides
- **For humans**: the end goal of biomedical research is curing humans, and "a cure developed for rodents might give us a clue… but without seeing how well it works in humans, the therapy won't save human lives." Humans **follow directions without training** ("lie still"), can perform **complex higher-order cognitive tasks** impossible to instruct in animals ("imagine we give you 20 dollars and take away a dollar per wrong answer" — rats and chimpanzees have no concept of currency), and can **self-report** feelings and symptoms. Counterintuitively, **human subjects are often cheaper** — undergraduate participation may be free or ~$10/hour, whereas housing mouse, rat, or monkey colonies "gets very expensive, very quickly."
- **For non-humans**: ethical constraints forbid harmful manipulations; **invertebrates (worms, flies) face much lighter oversight**, permitting a wider experimental range; some behaviors exist only in non-humans (flying, slithering); and lab animals have **food, housing, and day-night cycles controlled**, eliminating variance sources humans bring.
- **Genetic similarity ladder** (as given): humans are 99.9% identical to each other; **macaque ~93%** and the closest common model; then rat, mouse, zebrafish, *Drosophila*, *C. elegans*. **But**: even macaques "have different metabolic and physiological processes, and our behaviors are much different from theirs" — genetic similarity is not behavioral similarity.
- **WEIRD sampling — the population-validity problem, with numbers.** Most psychology subjects are **W**estern, **E**ducated, **I**ndustrialized, **R**ich, **D**emocratic. **~96% of psychology studies use WEIRD subjects, who are ~12% of the global population.** WEIRD people perform differently on behavioral tasks — **even susceptibility to the Müller-Lyer illusion varies with WEIRD-ness.** *That last fact is the strongest form of the argument: if a low-level perceptual illusion is culturally contingent, "basic" cognitive findings cannot be assumed universal.*
- Human variability generally: different backgrounds, experiences, flaws, biases; mood and mind-state alter behavior profoundly.

#### 6.1 Imaging brain *activity* (structure/anatomy)
- **Historical framing with a methodological moral**: Broca's autopsy of **"Patient Tan"** (severe language deficit, could say only "tan") revealed highly localized left-hemisphere damage. Contemporaneously, **Gall's phrenology** claimed personality from skull bumps. **Both support localization of function** — the difference is that phrenology "is a fad unsupported by the rigorous methodology of science." *The shared conclusion does not validate the method; the method has to stand on its own.* Modern addition: connections *between* areas matter as much as areas.
- **CT / CAT scan** — *"Does the patient have a brain tumor, and where?"* A 3D X-ray: an X-ray gun revolves around the body, a computer detector opposite compiles 2D slices into a 3D reconstruction. Dense material (bone) → white; less dense (air, CSF) → dark. **Spatial resolution ~0.5 mm**; temporal resolution not a consideration (used to track change over days). Strengths: **noninvasive**, usable on the living, **minutes** for a full head scan. Cost: **X-rays are highly mutagenic** (they interfere with DNA replication); a single head CT ≈ a few months of background exposure — prescribed when diagnostic value outweighs the risk.
- **DTI (Diffusion Tensor Imaging, 1994)** — *"Is this white matter tract's volume important for normal language processing?"* Uses MRI to detect water movement. **Isotropic diffusion**: a molecule in a cup moves any direction with equal probability. **Anisotropic diffusion**: in biological tissue movement is constrained — water diffuses easily *along* a white matter tract, poorly *across* it. DTI reads out that anisotropy. **Spatial resolution ~millimeters. Key limitation: DTI cannot give the *directionality* of axonal projections** (soma-to-terminal orientation is invisible). *Cite this whenever a tractography result is read as a directed connection.*
- **CLARITY (Deisseroth lab, 2013)** — *"Do layer-5 motor cortex neurons project into the spinal cord?"* The problem: membrane lipids and cytosolic particles refract light unpredictably, so tissue is opaque. The solution: flush the brain with chemicals forming a **gel matrix** around every structural component (dendritic spines to axon terminals), then use detergent to **solubilize and wash away the lipids**, leaving the matrix. **Spatial resolution on the order of microns.** Cost: **extremely destructive; cannot be done in a living organism.** The author's analogy is exact — the gel is like a **mold of a hand**: you see fingers, wrinkles, nails, but the mold cannot grip or feel. **All function is destroyed.**

#### 6.2 Imaging brain *function*
- **The premise stated plainly**: "the brain of a recently deceased person might look identical anatomically to that of a living person, but they would exhibit significantly less function." *Anatomy and function require different methods; do not substitute one for the other.*
- **EEG (Hans Berger, 1920s)** — *"At what times of night does the sleeping brain show synchronized activity?"* Ion movement during action potentials changes the charge of adjacent regions; when millions of cortical neurons fire together the currents are detectable **outside the skull**. Conductive **NaCl gel** plus 20–128 adhesive electrodes, each sensitive to ~10 μV. Decomposes waveforms into frequency components — **beta (13–30 Hz)** to **delta (0–4 Hz)** and everything between.
  - **Resolution profile — the textbook example of a trade-off**: **excellent temporal resolution** (sampling up to 10,000 Hz, i.e. 10,000 points/second/electrode, matching the millisecond scale of action potentials) but **poor spatial resolution** — signals only from the outermost layer, and even at 128 electrodes only ~**7 cm³**.
  - Other strengths: noninvasive, harmless, **cheap and mobile** — "all the machinery needed to run an EEG can be contained within a backpack." Uses: **polysomnography** (our best characterization of sleep phases rests on EEG), depth of anesthesia, **epilepsy** (large synchronous cortical discharge), migraine (waves of unusual cortical activity), and candidate roles in Alzheimer's, depression, possibly ADHD.
- **PET scan** — *"Which areas decrease in activity in mild cognitive impairment?" "Do drug-dependent people have high opioid receptor density?"* A **tracer** with a radioactive isotope (³H, ¹⁸F) is injected; it emits **positrons**; each positron annihilating with a nearby electron emits **two gamma particles at perpendicular angles**, detected by the scanner. **FDG (fluorodeoxyglucose-F18)** is a radioactive glucose analog taken up by metabolically active tissue; energetically demanding regions get more glucose via vasodilation.
  - **Second, distinct use worth separating: receptor density in vivo** — radiolabeled ligands binding specific receptors (**[¹¹C]raclopride** for dopamine receptors, **[¹⁸F]ASEM** for acetylcholine receptors). *This is a molecular measurement, not an activity measurement — do not conflate the two PET literatures.*
  - **Resolution: poor on both axes** — differences visible only at **5–10 cm³** (nothing below ~1 cm³ detectable), and **tens of seconds to minutes** for a signal change. Costs: **mutagenic** radiation exposure; **tissue boundaries are hard to identify**, so PET is frequently run **simultaneously with CT** for anatomical reference. Largely superseded by fMRI for task-related activity.
- **fMRI** — *"Which areas change activity when planning a motor action?"* Mechanism, in order: protons spin and align **with or against** a strong magnetic field (hospital magnets ~1 Tesla / 10,000 gauss, "strong enough to lift a car"; research up to 10 T); a **radio wave** knocks them into a high-energy state; as they relax back they **release detectable energy**. **Oxygenated hemoglobin is diamagnetic; deoxygenated hemoglobin is paramagnetic** — so magnetic sensitivity indexes oxygenation. Active regions demand more oxygen, adjacent vessels dilate, and the resulting change is the **BOLD (blood oxygenation level-dependent) signal**.
  - **Resolution**: spatial ~millimeters (better with stronger magnets); **temporal limited by the speed of vessel dilation — seconds to tens of seconds.** *The hemodynamic response, not the scanner, is the temporal bottleneck.*
  - **Limitations, and this is the most important paragraph in the chapter**:
    1. **The effect is tiny** — "one study estimates that perfusion increases by only **0.4%** even under the greatest activation."
    2. **Data are hard to analyze and frequently subject to false positives** — "an infamous study demonstrated that even a **dead salmon** in an fMRI exhibits brain activity that is similar to those reported in other fMRI studies," using standard analysis methods. *This is a multiple-comparisons demonstration; treat it as the standing argument for correction, not as a joke.*
    3. **The core inferential assumption**: "fMRI also assumes that increased blood flow is directly correlated with the amount of neural activity, **which may not always be the case**."
    4. **Physical/population constraints that bias what can be studied**: the tunnel is small and claustrophobic — "difficult to safely study anxiety or panic disorder without endangering the patient"; the machine is loud (a real problem for younger subjects); and **magnetosensitive implants** (aneurysm clips, IUDs, shrapnel, even older-generation tattoos with trace metal) risk severe injury or death. *These are not footnotes — they systematically exclude subject populations and topics.*

#### 6.3 Imaging the *cells*
- **Microscopy** — from van Leeuwenhoek (~40×) to standard lab scopes (400–1,000×) to **electron microscopes (1930s)** resolving nanometers, up to ~1,000,000×. **Fluorescence microscopy** uses filtered light or lasers at specific wavelengths to excite proteins such as **GFP**, which emit at a different wavelength.
- **Staining** — chemical affinities for cell parts: **Luxol Fast Blue** (myelin), **cresyl violet** (neurons), **DAPI** (nuclear genetic material). **The Golgi stain** (Camillo Golgi, late 1800s) is silver-based and fills the entire neuron black — **and works precisely because it is taken up by fewer than 1% of neurons**, so individual cells can be traced in their entirety. *The sparseness is the feature, not a defect.* It fueled the Golgi–Cajal dispute over whether neurons are continuous.
  - **Required chemistry**: **fixation** (e.g. **paraformaldehyde/PFA**) covalently cross-links adjacent proteins so they become "fixed in time"; the thorough route is **perfusion** — flushing fixative through the arteries to hijack the circulatory system. Fixatives kill microorganisms and inactivate degradative enzymes, and are "particularly nasty carcinogens that can permeate easily through latex gloves." Then a **microtome** (down to ~100 μm) or **cryostat** (down to ~10 μm) sections the tissue, because stains do not reliably penetrate thick tissue.
  - **Tract tracing**: **anterograde** trace stains soma → axon terminal; **retrograde** trace is taken up by terminals and stains the soma. **This is how directionality is recovered — the thing DTI cannot give you.**
  - **Hard limit: staining works only on fixed tissue, never living tissue.**
- **Immunohistochemistry (IHC)** — higher specificity than chemical stains, localizing **specific proteins at subcellular resolution**. Uses **antibodies** binding **antigens** with high specificity; protocol typically uses a **primary antibody** chosen for the target (anti-NeuN for NeuN) plus a secondary. Requires fixed, thin-sectioned tissue.

#### 6.4 Changing nervous system activity (causal intervention)
- **Precedents**: **Galvani** (electrically activating a nerve contracts the frog leg — hence "galvanized to action"). **Wilder Penfield (1950s)**: developing an epilepsy treatment, he resected scalp over the skull under **local anesthetic** and stimulated the exposed cortex **in a fully awake patient** — possible because **the brain surface has no pain receptors**. Moving incrementally across motor cortex, he mapped which region controls which muscles.
- **Electrophysiology ("ephys")** — the **rig**; measures *and* manipulates electrical properties. Origin: the **Loligo squid giant axon** (~1 mm diameter, easily impaled) → **Hodgkin and Huxley, Nobel 1963**. Cellular ephys pairs a microscope with glass **micropipettes** filled with electrolyte, inserted into neurons (or rupturing the membrane) to record and control.
  - **Its distinctive strength is range**: the same family of techniques addresses behavior, circuits, single neurons, and **individual ion channels** — you can record while a rat performs a task *and* stimulate to see whether behavior changes.
  - **Its weakness is the preparation ladder**: intact anesthetized → living brain slice → neuronal culture → **frog oocytes expressing neuronal receptors**. Each rung trades inference for control (see the preparations table).
- **TMS (Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation)** — physics: current through a coil produces a magnetic field, which by **induction** generates an electrical current at a distance. A handheld coil at the scalp electrically activates small brain areas: over motor cortex → muscle contraction; over occipital lobe → **perceived flashes of light**. **Completely noninvasive.** Reported moderate benefits across conditions (motor cortex: chronic pain, Parkinsonian symptoms, post-stroke contralateral motor function; prefrontal: anxiety, depression, cigarette craving, schizophrenia; elsewhere: antiepileptic, auditory hallucinations, tinnitus). **Risks stated, not buried**: temporary headache, localized pain, hearing changes, "bizarre changes in somatosensation," and most severely **seizures**. **"Use of TMS is still highly experimental."** Same magnetosensitive-implant contraindications as fMRI (deep brain stimulators, cochlear implants, aneurysm clips).
- **Genetic modification** — genomes mapped: *C. elegans* (1998), *Drosophila* (2000), mouse (2002), human (2003), rat (2004), macaque (2007). The manipulation spectrum, worth using precisely: **knock-out** (gene excision, removes function), **knock-in** (gene insertion — e.g. **humanized mice** expressing a human gene), **knock-down** (moderate decrease), **upregulation** (increase), **conditional knock-out** (normal until chemically induced). **CRISPR-Cas9 (2012)** — targeted editing; advantages are simplicity, efficiency, precision.
  - **Two limitations to carry**: (1) findings are **hard to generalize beyond the specific genetic strain** — a therapy that helps a knock-in mouse model "is certainly a starting place," with "no certainty that the therapy will also be effective in humans"; (2) **unexpected side effects** are hard to predict, since a single manipulation interacts with the rest of physiology.
- **Optogenetics / Chemogenetics — and the problem they were built to solve.** Early electrical stimulation had two specificity failures: (a) brain regions contain "a hodgepodge of cell populations" releasing different transmitters, so you cannot know which population produced the effect; and (b) the **fibers of passage problem** — the effect may come from axons merely *passing through* the stimulated area rather than from cells in it. **Name this problem explicitly whenever a stimulation or lesion result is interpreted regionally.**
  - **Optogenetics (Stanford, 2006)**: **channelrhodopsin (ChR2)**, a light-sensitive cation channel built from a rhodopsin-derived light sensor plus a Na⁺-permeable pore. **Blue light (~470 nm)** changes its shape, opening the channel and depolarizing the cell. **A 0.5 ms blue flash suffices to trigger a single action potential; the channel closes in under a millisecond → temporal resolution on the order of milliseconds.** Inhibitory variants: **halorhodopsin** (yellow-green light, pumps Cl⁻ *in*), **archaerhodopsin** (proton pump, extrudes H⁺). **Specificity comes from combining ChR2 with knock-in or viral gene delivery targeted to a defined cell population** — this is what solves the hodgepodge problem.
  - **Chemogenetics**: **DREADD** — *designer receptor exclusively activated by designer drugs* — a GPCR activated **only by an exogenous drug, never by endogenous neurotransmitters**, so it is silent at rest. Can excite or inhibit depending on the DREADD.
  - **The trade-off between them, stated as numbers**: DREADD requires drug entry plus GPCR signaling, so it has **far worse temporal resolution than optogenetics — seconds to tens of seconds ex vivo, minutes in a behaving animal.** Choose optogenetics for millisecond-timed causal claims; DREADDs for sustained state manipulation.
  - **Both are currently used exclusively in non-humans.** Both combine well with other methods — optogenetics + ephys for inter-area connectivity; optogenetics/chemogenetics + behavior for circuit-level causal roles.

#### Sidebar: Introduction to Computational Neuroscience — three model types
A taxonomy worth using when someone says "we built a model":
1. **Descriptive models** — characterize **what** nervous systems do. Qualitatively describe data via **neural encoding** (relationship between stimulus and neuronal response) and **neural decoding** (reconstructing already-encoded information — predicting the sensory stimulus purely from action potentials).
2. **Mechanistic models** — determine **how**, in relation to known anatomy and physiology; simulate a neuron or network.
3. **Interpretive models** — explain **why** the nervous system operates a particular way.
- **The stated tension**: a model that focuses on rendering biology in mathematical terms "may stray from its intended purpose and not accurately reflect neural behavior"; conversely a mathematical model "may oversimplify neural behavior and may lead to false conclusions." Hence the emphasis on **biologically plausible** neural networks. *Compare [[reference-oreilly-comp-cog-neuro]], which takes an explicit position in this debate — constrain at as many levels as possible and generate predictions.*
- **in silico** as a preparation label.

### Ch 1: Introduction — research design and epistemics

#### The three research designs, and exactly what each licenses
- **Experimental design — "the gold standard."** Goal: establish a **causal** relationship between the **independent variable** (manipulated) and the **dependent variable** (measured), by controlling **confounding variables**. Control achieved by comparing an experimental group with a **control group** as similar as possible in every way except the manipulation, with **random assignment**.
  - **Worked example given**: *Does studying more increase exam performance?* IV = hours studied; DV = exam grade; **confound = hours slept** (poor sleep impairs recall, and students trade sleep for study). Control it by comparing only students with similar sleep. Control group = students tested without opportunity to study, matched on age, gender distribution, educational history.
  - **What it licenses**: causality, and — *if the sample is representative* — extrapolation to a larger population.
  - **Its own artifact**: participants are placed in artificial circumstances. Knowing you are in a study could **worsen** performance through pressure, or **improve** it if you know you are in the experimental group and focus harder. Remedy: experimental and control groups must experience **exactly the same circumstances**.
- **Observational study / quasi-experimental design** — used when a true experiment is impractical or unethical. **No random assignment**, so uncontrolled variables persist.
  - **Worked example**: *Do people with traumatic brain injury have worse hand-eye coordination?* You cannot ethically inflict head injuries, so you compare an already-diagnosed group against a demographically similar undiagnosed group.
  - **What it licenses: correlation, not causation** — and the specific failure is a **"chicken or the egg" problem**: perhaps poor hand-eye coordination *caused* the accident that produced the head injury. **Always articulate the reverse-causal story explicitly before interpreting a quasi-experimental result.**
  - Real instance cited: the **CDC Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs)** study, ~17,000 people, linking childhood trauma to premature death.
- **Case study** — a highly detailed description of a single patient. **Its unique value, stated as a principle: "Examining millions of healthy people may not give us the same insight as studying just one person with a specific injury or disorder."** Instances: **HM** (memory), **Patient Tan** (language), **Patient SM** (fear processing), **Phineas Gage** (1848, frontal lobe and inhibition).
  - **What it licenses: correlation, not causation, and poor generalizability** — case studies are "descriptions of nearly-one-of-a-kind individuals."
  - **Its proper role in the inferential chain**: **generating hypotheses that are then tested experimentally.** The model sequence given: HM's bilateral hippocampal removal → research question ("Is the hippocampus needed for the creation of navigational memory?") → rodent experiment with hippocampal lesion (experimental) vs. a different lesion (control) on a memory task. **Use this three-step chain — case → hypothesis → controlled experiment — as the template for how single-case findings should be handled.**

#### What neuroscience is NOT — myths and their refutations
- **"We only use 10% of our brain."** False. We use every part, and most of the brain is active most of the time — **just not all at the same time**. **Ramachandran's traffic-light analogy**: does a traffic light only use 33% of its lights? A properly functioning one uses all three at **precisely controlled times**. And the consequence of failure is real: if too many cells were active at the wrong times — green and red simultaneously — **chaos ensues; one cause of seizures is excessive neural activity.**
- **"Forming memories causes new neurons to be born."** False as a general account. Memories are most likely stored at **synapses**; the mechanism is **change in how neurons connect and communicate**, not creation of new neurons. **The nuance that must accompany the correction**: **neurogenesis** does occur in a few areas — notably the **hippocampus** and the **olfactory epithelium** — so the myth is a mislocation of a real phenomenon, not a pure fabrication.
- **"The brain cannot repair itself."** Overstated. **Plasticity** — the ability to change over time — lets the brain "rewire itself," carrying out functions without the damaged connections, which is how people spontaneously recover from stroke. **The honest limit is stated too**: **neurodegenerative** conditions (Parkinson's, Alzheimer's) progressively worsen, lack simple risk-free treatments, and "there isn't strong evidence that the brain can recover from the destruction caused by these diseases."
- **"Analytical = left-brained, creative = right-brained."** False. "Nearly every function that the left half of the brain can do, the right half can do just as well, and vice versa" — sensory processing, voluntary muscle control, memory. **The genuine exception is language**, heavily left-lateralized in most people for reasons unknown. Separately real, and often confused with the myth: **contralateral organization** — left brain signals the right body and vice versa, a distinguishing feature of the vertebrate brain.

#### Localization vs. distributed processing — the standing tension
- **Localization theory** (Broca, mid-1800s): specific areas carry out specific functions. Evidence: language comprehension in a small left-hemisphere patch; face perception in cells at the base of the brain; balance and motor coordination in cerebellum.
- **Distributive processing theory**: behavioral functions require activation across several areas. Complex behaviors — emotion, consciousness, **cognition** (defined as "the act of generating knowledge through a combination of senses, memories, and thoughts") — require coordinated action across distinct areas.
- **The verdict offered, and it is the right one to carry**: *"Most likely, some behaviors are more localized than others, but still rely on signals from across many other brain areas. **As with most fields of biology, absolutes are rare in neuroscience.**"*

#### The field is provisional
- More neuroscience studies were published between 2015 and 2020 than in the previous seventy years.
- The history is a sequence of superseded frames: Egyptians (~2500 BCE) discarded the brain through the nose while preserving stomach, lungs, liver — "the brain, apparently, wasn't needed for the afterlife"; **Aristotle** placed the soul in the heart and made the brain a device for **cooling the blood** (hence "hot-blooded" impulsivity); Golgi vs. Cajal settled cellular structure only once microscopy arrived; the **electron microscope reached the synapse in 1954** (~20 nm, a thousandth the width of a hair); **fMRI arrived in neuroscience in 1991**; CLARITY is current.
- **The stance to adopt**: current understanding "is only a snapshot along the timeline of scientific discoveries," and some future findings "will find compelling evidence against long-standing accepted theories in the field, prompting a shift in the paradigm."

#### Neuroscience as an integrative field — the levels, and their directions
- **Biology** at the root, subdivided: **molecular** neurobiologists (proteins, gene regulation), **cellular** neurobiologists (network communication), **cognitive** neuroscientists (underlying causes of behavior). Also genetics (Huntington's as autosomal dominant), and ecology/evolution (**Toxoplasma** altering fear responses to move through the food web).
- **Psychology** contributes the **top-down** view — understand the whole organism, then its components — explicitly contrasted with biology's **bottom-up** view starting at cells or molecules. It brings the **mind-body problem** and the concept of **emergence** (the whole is greater than the sum of its parts).
- **Chemistry** — **endogenous** signaling molecules (acidic/basic, polar/fat-soluble, some gaseous) and **exogenous** influences (caffeine, cocaine).
- **Physics** — membrane voltages on the scale of tens of millivolts; signaling as temporary voltage change.
- **Use this as the cross-level checklist**: any complete mechanistic account should be locatable at molecular, cellular, systems, and behavioral levels, and should say which direction — top-down or bottom-up — the argument runs.

## Worked Example — choosing a method from a question (the chapter's own procedure)
The book pairs every technique with *"Example questions answered."* Reconstructed as a decision procedure:

**Question: "Does activating cortical glutamatergic input change firing rate in the striatum?"**
1. **What kind of claim?** Causal, not correlational → you need an **intervention**, not an imaging method. This eliminates CT, DTI, PET, fMRI, EEG at a stroke.
2. **What specificity is required?** A *specific transmitter-defined population* in a region containing many cell types → electrical stimulation fails twice over: the hodgepodge problem and the **fibers of passage** problem. → **optogenetics or chemogenetics.**
3. **What temporal precision?** "Change in firing rate" driven by a defined input implies millisecond-scale causal timing → **optogenetics (ChR2, ~1 ms)**, not DREADDs (seconds to minutes).
4. **What readout?** Firing rate of individual striatal neurons → **electrophysiology**, combined with the optogenetic stimulation.
5. **Which preparation?** If the claim is about circuit connectivity, **ex vivo slice** buys control. If the claim is about behavior, you must pay the in vivo cost — more uncontrolled variables, stricter ethics, but the only route to a behaviorally meaningful inference.
6. **What does the result NOT license?** Non-human only; strain-specific; and generalization to humans requires its own evidence.

**The reusable move**: work from *claim type* (causal vs. correlational) → *required specificity* → *required temporal and spatial resolution* → *readout* → *preparation*, and finish by stating what the design cannot support. Running this in reverse — picking a method first and fitting a question to it — is the failure mode the chapter is organized against.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- **State both resolutions before interpreting any imaging result.** EEG: milliseconds but ~7 cm³. fMRI: millimeters but seconds-to-tens-of-seconds. PET: 5–10 cm³ and tens of seconds. If a claim requires finer resolution on either axis than the method provides, the claim exceeds its evidence.
- **When temporal resolution is poor, ask what got smeared together.** Two events separated in time appear as one.
- **Every step away from the awake behaving animal buys control and costs generalizability.** Name where on the in vivo / ex vivo / in vitro / in silico ladder a finding sits before extending it.
- **For BOLD, carry all four caveats**: the effect is ~0.4% perfusion change at maximum activation; standard pipelines produce false positives (the dead salmon); the neurovascular coupling assumption "may not always be the case"; and the scanner environment systematically excludes subjects and topics (claustrophobia, noise, metal implants).
- **Before any regional stimulation or lesion interpretation, rule out fibers of passage** — the effect may belong to axons transiting the region, not cells within it. This is the specific problem optogenetics with cell-type-targeted expression was built to solve.
- **Match the intervention to the required timescale**: optogenetics (~1 ms) for timed causal claims; DREADDs (seconds ex vivo, minutes in behavior) for sustained state changes. Using DREADDs for a millisecond claim, or optogenetics for a chronic manipulation, is a design error.
- **Ask what a method destroys.** CLARITY yields micron resolution and abolishes all function — it is a mold of a hand. Staining and IHC require fixed tissue. If the question concerns dynamics, these are unavailable regardless of their resolution.
- **DTI gives tract volume and orientation, never directionality.** Directionality requires anterograde/retrograde tract tracing.
- **Classify the design before believing the conclusion**: randomized experiment → causation; quasi-experiment → correlation, and you must state the reverse-causal alternative out loud; case study → hypothesis generation only.
- **A case study's job is to generate a hypothesis for controlled test.** HM → "is the hippocampus needed for navigational memory?" → rodent lesion experiment. A case study cited as if it settled a mechanism is being misused.
- **Check the sample against the population.** ~96% of psychology studies use WEIRD subjects who are ~12% of humanity, and WEIRD-ness modulates even the Müller-Lyer illusion. Perceptual "basics" are not automatically universal.
- **Genetic similarity is not behavioral similarity.** Macaques share ~93% of our genome and still differ metabolically, physiologically, and behaviorally.
- **Findings from genetically modified animals are strain-bound** and carry unpredictable interaction effects; a therapy that works in a knock-in model is "a starting place," not a result about humans.
- **A shared conclusion does not validate a method.** Phrenology and Broca both supported localization of function; only one was science.
- **When correcting a neuro-myth, give the real phenomenon it distorts.** Neurogenesis is real in hippocampus and olfactory epithelium; plasticity is real but does not extend to neurodegeneration; contralateral organization is real while left/right personality types are not. A correction without the nuance produces a new myth.
- **Default to "some functions are more localized than others."** Absolutes are rare in neuroscience.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Method selection runs from the claim, not from the tool**: causal vs. correlational → specificity needed → spatial and temporal resolution → readout → preparation → an explicit statement of what the design cannot support.
2. **Spatial and temporal resolution trade off, and the trade is the story** — EEG and fMRI are near-mirror images, which is why they answer different questions and why combining them is the standard move.
3. **Control and generalizability trade off along the in vivo → in vitro ladder.** Know which rung a finding is on.
4. **fMRI measures a ~0.4% blood-flow-derived signal under an assumption that may fail**, in an environment that excludes whole subject populations. Report it with those hedges or not at all.
5. **Specificity problems — cell-type heterogeneity and fibers of passage — are what modern causal tools exist to solve.** Invoke them whenever a regional intervention is read as a claim about a population.
6. **Experiment → causation; quasi-experiment → correlation plus a reverse-causal alternative; case study → hypothesis only.** Nothing in neuroscience licenses skipping this classification.
7. **WEIRD sampling limits generalization even for perception.** Population validity is a substantive constraint, not a boilerplate disclaimer.
8. **The field is a snapshot.** Hold current frameworks provisionally, and expect some to be overturned by instruments not yet built.
