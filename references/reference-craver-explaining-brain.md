# Explaining the Brain: Mechanisms and the Mosaic Unity of Neuroscience — Carl F. Craver

**Source edition:** Oxford University Press, 2007. Source locators below use chapter/section titles; the supplied Markdown preserves printed page labels but has OCR and heading defects. This is a synthesis of Craver's account, not a claim that all philosophers endorse it.

## Mental Model (read first)
An explanation of a neural phenomenon describes the organized entities and activities that produce it. Reproducing an input–output pattern, naming an activated region, and establishing an actual mechanism are different achievements. Craver evaluates mechanisms by how evidence constrains their components, activities, organization, and relevance to a carefully characterized phenomenon. Progress comes from integrating constraints across fields and mechanistic levels; it does not require reducing every explanation to molecules.

Two distinctions remain independent: **how-possibly → how-plausibly → how-actually** concerns support for the proposed mechanism's actuality; **mechanism sketch → more complete mechanism model** concerns missing detail. A biologically supported sketch can still be incomplete, while a detailed simulation can remain speculative.

**Use for:** judging mechanistic claims, planning convergent experiments, diagnosing explanatory placeholders, separating causal from constitutive relevance, and comparing multilevel integration with reduction. For identifying a population intervention effect, pair this with [Hernán & Robins](reference-hernan-robins-causal-inference.md); their identifying assumptions do not by themselves establish the component organization Craver asks for.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapter 1 — Starting with neuroscience: define the phenomenon before naming its mechanism
**Locator:** Ch 1, “Explanations in Neuroscience Describe Mechanisms,” “Explanations are Multilevel,” and “Integrate Multiple Fields.”

A mechanism consists of **entities and activities organized such that they produce a phenomenon**. The explanatory target, or **explanandum phenomenon**, may be a behavior, a capacity, a cellular event, or a disease process. The **explanans** describes what produces that target. A mechanism is the system doing the work; a model or diagram represents it. Properties of a representation are not automatically properties of the represented system.

A useful mechanistic question specifies the system, phenomenon, relevant conditions, and contrast: why does transmission occur with calcium present but fail when calcium entry is blocked? “Explain memory” needs refinement into tasks, temporal features, and conditions before components can be judged relevant. Normality is relative to the explanatory project: a disease mechanism must work under disease conditions, and an experimentally induced phenomenon remains a legitimate target.

Multilevel explanations telescope: a component at one level can become the phenomenon to explain at another. Different research fields can also contribute at the **same** mechanistic level. Neither using molecular methods nor assembling several disciplines automatically establishes integration; the findings must constrain a common explanation.

### Chapter 2 — Explanation and causal relevance: success at prediction is insufficient
**Locator:** Ch 2, calcium and neurotransmitter release; “Explanation and Representation”; covering-law, unification, and Hodgkin–Huxley discussions.

The **covering-law model** treats explanation as deriving the phenomenon from laws and conditions. The **unification model** emphasizes deriving many phenomena through a small stock of argument patterns. Craver argues that derivation and unification alone do not adequately sort explanatory relationships from temporal sequence, irrelevant regularity, reverse explanation, or association generated by a common cause. A formal derivation can be correct while the biological interpretation is wrong.

The Hodgkin–Huxley case separates several accomplishments within one scientific achievement. Equations describing voltage-dependent conductance changes can predict action potentials without specifying the actual molecular mechanism of those changes. Hypothesized activation particles help construct a model, but predictive success does not establish that the particles literally exist. Subsequent channel evidence can strengthen a mechanistic interpretation without making every modeling device a discovered entity.

**How to use the distinction:** identify which part of a model describes the phenomenon, which part posits a mechanism, and which observations test those posits. Do not reject quantitative models for being abstract; ask what explanatory claim their abstraction supports. Craver's stronger standard for actual mechanisms differs from allowing any systematic functional interpretation that reproduces the capacity.

### Chapter 3 — Causal relevance and manipulation
**Locator:** Ch 3, “Causation as Transmission,” “Omission and Prevention,” and “Manipulation and Causation.”

**Etiological relevance** concerns what brings about an effect. On the manipulationist account, changing a variable makes a difference to another under appropriate conditions. The relevant question is a contrast: which change in X, under which background conditions, changes Y? It is not a claim that every alteration in X changes every feature of Y.

An **ideal intervention** separates the candidate cause from rival routes: it changes the putative cause; it does not directly set the outcome; it does not change the outcome through another route; and it is not associated with an independent cause of the outcome. Real experiments approximate these conditions using controls, specificity checks, and knowledge of the system. A tool being called “selective” is evidence to examine, not a substitute for checking its effects.

Manipulationist relevance accommodates **omissions and prevention**, which a picture restricted to transferring a substance or conserved quantity can miss. Blocking a channel or preventing inhibition can make a systematic difference. The explanatory relation need not be universal across all environments: **invariance** is assessed across a stated range of interventions and conditions. Historical contingency or biological fragility does not automatically make a relationship nonexplanatory.

**Boundary:** this is an account of relevance, not a turnkey statistical estimator. Establishing that the experimental comparison isolates X still needs assumptions about assignment, measurement, selection, and competing pathways.

### Chapter 4 — Norms of mechanistic explanation
**Locator:** Ch 4 §§2–8; especially printed pp. 112–139 and 152–160.

**How-possibly models** describe one way parts and activities might produce the phenomenon, without establishing that the proposed parts exist or operate that way. **How-plausibly models** respect an increasing set of known constraints. **How-actually models** describe the actual components and organization. These are evidential distinctions, not automatic labels assigned by model complexity or fit statistics.

A **mechanism sketch** leaves gaps. Terms such as encode, inhibit, process, and represent can function as **filler terms** when they replace the operation that needs explaining. They remain useful when explicitly marked as placeholders or unpacked using an independently understood mechanism. The error is concealing the gap, not using the vocabulary.

Characterize more than a normal output:

- **Precipitating conditions:** what starts the phenomenon?
- **Manifestations:** what features must the model reproduce, including timing and magnitude?
- **Inhibiting conditions:** when does it fail to occur?
- **Modulating conditions:** how does it change when relevant conditions vary?
- **Nonstandard conditions and by-products:** which perturbations or incidental signatures distinguish otherwise adequate rivals?

Candidate components become credible through a **stable cluster of properties**, **robustness** across different detection methods, **intervention** on their activities, and **physiological plausibility** within the relevant explanatory context. Craver presents these as useful, nonexhaustive criteria, not a numerical threshold proving existence. Repeated measurements with the same assumptions are weaker than convergence across causally and theoretically different routes.

Organization has **active, spatial, and temporal** dimensions. Specify which components interact, their connections and compartments, and the order, rates, and durations of activity. Wimsatt's **aggregativity** contrasts diagnose when arrangement matters: could parts be rearranged or substituted, added or removed, disaggregated and reassembled without changing the relevant property, and are cooperative or inhibitory interactions absent? A mechanism typically violates these conditions. A component inventory or time-ordered diagram therefore does not suffice.

#### Constitutive relevance and mutual manipulability
**Locator:** Ch 4 §8.3, pp. 152–160.

**Constitutive relevance** asks whether an activity of a part contributes to the behavior of the organized whole. It differs from an earlier event causing a later event. Craver's **mutual manipulability** account combines:

1. A genuine part–whole relation between X and system S.
2. Bottom-up evidence: intervening on X's activity changes S's phenomenon under appropriate conditions.
3. Top-down evidence: manipulating the system's phenomenon changes X's activity under appropriate conditions.

**Interference**, **stimulation**, and **activation** experiments address different vulnerabilities and are strongest together. Interference can implicate nonspecific background support; stimulation may spread or recruit an unnatural route; activation can reveal a downstream correlate instead of a working component. Sham procedures, task controls, timing, and convergent manipulations test these alternatives.

A null perturbation is not an automatic finding of irrelevance: redundancy, recovery, compensation, or reorganization may mask the contribution. An activation null can miss a tonically active component. Conversely, dependence on general bodily support does not locate that support inside the specific cognitive mechanism. Craver acknowledges difficult intermediate cases where only one manipulability direction is supported. Use the framework as a constrained evidential strategy, not as an exceptionless pass/fail checklist.

**Do not translate mutual manipulability into reciprocal interlevel causation.** Constitution is a part–whole relation and can be synchronic; an activity and the whole it partly constitutes are not independent variables in the same way as an ordinary cause and subsequent effect. “Top-down experiment” here names an investigative strategy, not proof of a separate downward causal force.

### Chapter 5 — A field-guide to levels
**Locator:** Ch 5, taxonomy of levels and “Levels of Mechanisms.”

Distinguish **levels of science**—theories, descriptions, fields—from **levels of nature**—entities, properties, activities. Within nature, size, containment, composition, realization, and mechanistic organization pick out different relations. A smaller object is not necessarily a component in the explanation under discussion; occupying the same spatial scale does not establish that two objects share a mechanistic level.

**Levels of mechanisms** are local to the phenomenon: lower-level components are organized to produce a higher-level activity. There need not be a single universal hierarchy ordering every scientific object. State the relata and relationship whenever a “cross-level” argument depends on them.

**Integration with the existing library:** Marr-like distinctions among computational problem, algorithm, and implementation classify explanatory questions. Craver's component–whole levels classify organization in mechanisms. They can inform one another but must not be equated. A transition from one vocabulary to the other needs a linking claim and evidence.

### Chapter 6 — Nonfundamental explanation
**Locator:** Ch 6, organization, causal exclusion, realization, and effect contrasts.

A demand to put all causal powers at a fundamental physical level does not eliminate experimentally useful higher-level difference-making relations. Organized components can have collective properties, and the intervention contrast may be captured better by a realized property than by an arbitrarily specific microdescription. Extra microscopic detail can obscure which difference matters.

The neurotransmitter-release discussion distinguishes membrane depolarization from one particular way of producing it. If different microchanges produce the relevant voltage change and comparable release, the explanatory contrast need not be specific to that one microrealization. Conversely, microscopic differences matter when they alter the phenomenon being explained.

**Multiple realization** permits different organized systems to produce a similar phenomenon. It does not prove that implementation is irrelevant, nor that all realizations support every intervention equivalently. Craver separates metaphysical disputes about causal powers from experimentally assessable relations of causal relevance; his defense of higher-level explanation should not be silently converted into a settled metaphysical conclusion.

### Chapter 7 — Mosaic unity
**Locator:** Ch 7 §§3–4, “The Space of Possible Mechanisms” and interlevel integration.

The **space of possible mechanisms** contains alternative arrangements of components and activities that might produce the phenomenon. Evidence restricts this space, shifts support within it, or sometimes expands it by revealing new components. A promising hypothesis is one that survives relevant constraints, not simply the one with the most familiar biological nouns.

**Intralevel constraints** include componency, spatial arrangement, timing, and active interactions. **Interlevel constraints** include mutual manipulability, spatial and temporal compatibility, and **accommodative constraints**: the description of a phenomenon and its mechanistic account may need to be revised together. Mechanism discovery can reclassify a psychological category rather than merely attach a brain region to it.

The **mosaic unity of neuroscience** arises when different fields constrain the same multilevel mechanism. Upward-looking work asks how an activity contributes to a larger system; downward-looking work identifies its components. Neither direction exhausts integration, and integration within a level does not require one field's theories to reduce to another's.

**How:** list rival mechanisms; identify the constraint each experiment supplies; distinguish independent constraints from restatements; track what each finding excludes and what survives. Agreement among methods is valuable when their vulnerabilities differ. A disagreement can expose an incorrect task taxonomy, measurement assumption, or proposed organizational link.

## Worked Example — the action potential as an explanatory audit
**Reconstruction:** Chs 2 and 4, Hodgkin–Huxley and channel-gating cases; no new empirical result is claimed.

Begin with a model that reproduces an action-potential waveform. This establishes a predictive achievement for the tested conditions. Separate the conductance equations from their biological interpretation: a good fitted gating variable does not alone identify a molecular gate.

Next require the model to address inhibition by sodium-channel blockade, modulation by extracellular ion concentrations, and nonstandard voltage-clamp behavior. These tests restrict candidate mechanisms that fit the ordinary waveform equally well. Ask whether the proposed entities have independently established properties and whether manipulating them produces the predicted changes. Then check spatial, active, and temporal organization: ion selectivity and the sequence of conductance changes matter, not merely the presence of channels.

Finally, distinguish the remaining gaps. A model with verified channels but an unresolved conformational transition is an evidence-supported sketch at that point. A detailed gating story without the required biological evidence remains how-possibly or how-plausibly. The useful next experiment targets the unresolved distinction, rather than collecting another instance of the already fitted waveform.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- When a mechanism claim rests on behavioral fit, identify the descriptive achievement and name the missing evidence about actual parts, activities, and organization.
- When a term carries the explanation, ask what operation would replace it. Keep unresolved operations explicitly marked as sketch elements.
- When comparing models, test inhibiting, modulating, nonstandard, and by-product predictions in addition to the canonical behavior.
- When interpreting a lesion, examine intervention specificity, nonspecific support, and reorganization before localizing a function. A null effect under compensation is not decisive.
- When interpreting activation, ask whether it is a working component, a tonic process missed by subtraction, or a correlated consequence. Pair activation with appropriately controlled perturbation where feasible.
- When invoking mutual manipulability, preserve the part–whole premise, intervention qualifications, and the distinction from reciprocal causation.
- When using multiple methods, identify their separate constraints and shared assumptions. A larger number of mutually dependent measurements does not guarantee robustness.
- When a higher-level explanation is informative, test its relevant contrast before demanding more microscopic detail. Keep realization and component hierarchy distinct.
- When integrating disciplines, document how their findings constrain a common mechanism; do not require a single vocabulary or a global reduction hierarchy.

## Key Takeaways
A supported mechanism sketch, a detailed possible mechanism, and a behaviorally successful model have different evidential statuses. Mechanistic adequacy concerns organized reality under relevant contrasts, including failure conditions. Mosaic integration preserves useful higher-level explanations while demanding explicit evidence for their connections to component mechanisms.
